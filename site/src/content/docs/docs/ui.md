## Menus and HUDs

**Entity → UI → Button** makes a clickable button: a background sprite, a label and a **Button** component. Set its **Action** and a menu needs no code at all: **LoadScene** (with **Scene**, e.g. `Game`), **RestartScene** or **Quit**, with a **Fade** (in seconds) to fade the screen out and back in around the switch. A **Shortcut** key (Enter for Play, Esc for Back) clicks it too, or a **Shortcut Action** does: an input action's name, like `OpenMap` added in **Game → Controls** and bound to M, so the key shows in the Controls window and players can change it with a Rebind button. Either one is ignored while the button can't be clicked, while a panel it isn't on is open, and (for Enter, Space or A) while another button is picked. **Do** does any action of the entities named in **Targets**, as a Trigger does (an RPG hero's **Attack**, **Cast Spell** or **Use Item**, a door's **Open**, with **With** for an action that takes a value), and is faded while none of them can do it now (a fighter off its turn or without the mana for its spell; a script component says so by implementing `IActionReady`). In a network game it's done where the game is run, except on what each machine keeps for itself (a menu, a calibration), which it does there and then. Add a **ScreenAnchor** to pin anything to an edge or corner of the screen, like a score in the top-left: it stays put whatever the window's shape, while the camera follows the player or shakes. The Scene view shows it where the game does, on the camera's 16:9 frame, and dragging it there sets its **Offset**. For a camera that zooms, set the camera's **HudSize** to the **Size** the HUD is laid out at: everything pinned then keeps its size and place on the screen as the camera zooms in and out, and its buttons click where they're drawn. Left at 0, the HUD is in world units and zooms with the world. The Scene view outlines buttons' clickable areas in blue. Menus work without a mouse: the arrow keys, WASD, D-pad or stick pick a button (it lights up), and Enter, Space or the A button click it; with a controller, the top button is picked straight away, and B clicks whichever button Esc would. Untick a button's **Navigable** if it sits on a HUD during play, so moving the player doesn't pick it.

## Options menus

**Entity → UI → Slider** and **Toggle** make an options menu with no code. Set a slider's **Setting** to **Volume**, **MusicVolume**, **SoundVolume** or **AutoSpeed** (how quickly dialogue reads on by itself), or a toggle's to **Sound** (on or off), **Fullscreen**, **SkipUnread** (whether skipping goes through lines not read yet), or **Skip** or **Auto** (dialogue skipping or reading on by itself now; see [Skip and Auto](/docs/visual-novels/#skip-and-auto)), and it changes that setting and always shows it. A slider's Setting can also be the player's **AudioOffset** or **VisualOffset** (how late they hear the sound or see the screen, for Bluetooth headphones and TVs; see [Rhythm games](/docs/rhythm/)), set in milliseconds from its **Min** to its **Max** (say -200 to 500, in steps of 5), or their **NoteSpeed** (how fast rhythm notes come, times what the lanes say: 0.5 to 3, in steps of 0.25). A text inside a slider with **Output** `Value` (`{0:0} ms`) shows how it's set. The game remembers the player's choices (whether made by a slider, a toggle or a script) and puts them back next time it runs. For anything else, like a difficulty, set **Save As** to a name instead and read it from scripts with `Game.SaveData.GetFloat("difficulty")`. A slider is dragged with the mouse, turned with the wheel, or moved by its **Step** with left and right once picked; a toggle is a button whose **On Text** and **Off Text** become its label ("Sound: On"). Built games go fullscreen with F11 too.

**Display settings** go in an options menu the same way. A **Choice** (**Entity → UI → Choice**) whose **Setting** is **WindowSize** offers the common 16:9 window sizes that fit the player's screen (960x540 up to 3840x2160: on a 1080p screen 1280x720, 1366x768 and 1600x900, since a 1920x1080 window doesn't fit beside the taskbar) in place of its own **Options**, and picking one resizes the built game's window there and then; the window opens at it next time (1280x720 until the player picks one). Fullscreen stays borderless at the screen's own resolution: Griff never changes the display's mode, so a window size only counts while windowed (one picked while fullscreen is the window's once it's windowed again). One whose Setting is **FrameCap** offers Off, 30, 60, 120, 144, 165 and 240 frames a second, and a toggle set to **VSync** turns keeping in step with the screen off and on. With VSync on (the default) the game draws one frame for each the screen shows, as it always has, and a cap skips some to stay under it (30 on a 60 Hz screen draws every other frame); with VSync off it draws each frame as soon as the last is done, as many as the computer can, up to the cap. A slider set to **UiScale** (say 0.75 to 1.5, in steps of 0.05, with a text showing `{0:0%}`) draws everything pinned with a **Screen Anchor**, and tooltips, that many times their size, for a player who wants the HUD bigger or smaller; buttons click where they're drawn. Camera settings go in the same menu: a toggle set to **EdgeScroll** and a slider set to **PanSpeed** (say 0.5 to 2) change how a Camera Controls pans (see the players guide). All four display settings are remembered like the rest, and scripts have them as `Game.WindowSize` (a `WindowSize`, null until picked; `Game.WindowSizes` lists those that fit), `Game.VSync`, `Game.FrameCap` (0 for none) and `Game.UiScale`. The frames are only how often the picture is drawn: the game's steps stay at its step rate whatever they are, each frame taking the steps its time holds and drawing between the last two, so a capped game plays at the same speed, drawn less often. In the editor's Game tab and a browser there's no window to size, so Window Size is kept for the built game and changes nothing there (the choice still lists the sizes that fit this screen, or up to 1920x1080 in a browser); both always keep in step with the screen whatever VSync says (the editor's own window shares the game's thread), while the frame cap counts everywhere.

## Pause menus and panels

**Entity → UI → Panel** makes a panel: a backdrop pinned to the middle of the screen. Select it and create buttons, sliders and choices to put them on it. While the game runs it's hidden until it opens (tick **Open** to have it open from the start), so you can lay it out in the Scene view. Set its **Open On** to `Pause` and it's a pause menu with no code: Esc, P or Start opens it and stops the game, and pressing it again, Esc or the gamepad's B closes it (if the panel has a button with the Esc **Shortcut**, or a **Shortcut Action** with Esc among its keys, Esc clicks that instead). A button's **Action** opens and closes panels too: **OpenPanel** (with **Panel** naming one, like `Options`) and **ClosePanel** (Resume, Back). **SaveGame**, **LoadGame** and **DeleteSave** (with a **Slot**, or on a **Save Slot** that shows it), **QuickSave** and **QuickLoad** save the game and load it again, and **Continue** loads the game saved last (a title screen's Continue), the scene, the talk and the flags as they were (see [Saving and loading](/docs/visual-novels/#saving-and-loading)). While a panel is open, the mouse, keyboard and gamepad only reach the controls on it, so the HUD and the menu underneath don't react; its top control (or its **First**) is picked straight away, and closing it gives the keyboard back to what was picked before. Panels stack: an options panel inside the pause menu closes with it. **Pause Game** (on by default) stops game time and runs only the scripts on open panels, a line typing out in a dialogue box waits under it, and Interact and the arrow keys don't reach levers, doors or dialogue (or its choices) behind it; untick it for a game over screen that the game goes on behind. **Pauses** names what it stops instead of the whole game: `Arcade` stops only that entity and everything inside it (a minigame on a cabinet, the world behind a shop) while the rest runs on behind the panel (see [Pausing part of the game](/docs/ui/#pausing-part-of-the-game)). **Close On Back** off makes the player choose their way out. **Open Message** opens it when that message is sent, with no code: `Solved` from a puzzle's goals, a `GameOver` from a player's **Death Message**, or anything a pickup, switch or script sends. **Needs Flag** keeps it shut until a flag is set (`FoundMap`; `not FoundMap` for while it isn't): its **Open On** key, **Open Message** and buttons that open it do nothing until then, though its **Open** action and scripts still open it.

A builder's Esc puts down the tool in hand before it opens the game menu. A script says so by overriding `ClaimsBack` (`protected override bool ClaimsBack => tool is not null;`): while it's true, Esc and B don't open a panel on its **Open On** (P and Start still do) or close one the game goes on behind, so the script reads them itself, and the next Esc, with the tool put down, opens the menu. Only a running script claims Back, not one on a disabled or paused entity or behind a panel that pauses the game, so Esc always closes the pause menu. A press a panel acts on is used up: the Esc or B that closes one, its **Open On** key opening or closing it, and Enter, A or a **Shortcut** clicking a button that opens or closes one don't reach the game's scripts in that frame, so closing the game menu doesn't also put the tool down or skip a tour (`Input.WasPressed` sees the key again only when it's pressed again, buffered or not). A button with **Over Panels** ticked keeps working while a panel it isn't on is open, even one that pauses the game: a menu bar's Game button opens the game menu over a letter, by a click or its **Shortcut Action**, on top of it, and closing the menu leaves the letter open. The arrow keys stay on the panel.

Tick **Popup** for a panel that goes away as soon as the player is done with it: a tool palette's fly-out (a panel with **Pause Game** off, inside the palette so it sits beside its icon), a dropdown, a context menu. A mouse press anywhere off it closes it and does nothing else: no button there clicks, scripts don't see the press, and `Game.MouseOnUi` is true for it (and for a press on the pop-up's backdrop), so a builder's tool doesn't lay a wall where the player only meant to close the fly-out. Clicking one of its buttons closes it once the button has done its **Action** and its scripts have heard the click, so a tool picked on it is picked, and an **OpenPanel** button on it opens its window in the pop-up's place (keep the window outside the pop-up, or it closes with it). Esc closes it as it closes any panel.

## Pausing part of the game

A **Pause Group** on an entity makes it and everything inside it a group that pauses on its own: a match-3 minigame on an arcade cabinet in a platformer, one player's half of a split screen, the enemies while a boss talks. While its **Paused** is ticked, nothing in the group moves, falls, animates, emits, counts down, spawns, shoots, runs its scripts or takes the player's input (a board in it stands still, a player in it doesn't move on the arrow keys), but it's still drawn and still solid to what's outside it, and the rest of the game runs on. Its **Pause**, **Resume** and **Toggle** actions let a trigger, a wire or a script do it with no code ("when `Insert Coin` arrives, Resume the Arcade"), and a switch or a wire into it runs it while on and pauses it while off. **Pause Message** and **Resume Message** go out as it changes, and its output **Paused** can be shown or wired. A panel's or a dialogue's **Pauses** pauses a group the same way while it's open or being said. Scripts call `Game.Pause(entity)` and `Game.Resume(entity)` and read `entity.IsPaused`. What's paused is worked out on every step from the pause groups, the open panels and the dialogue being said, so rewinding puts it back as it was; online, the host pauses groups as it runs everything else (a panel still can't pause anything there).

## Game speed

A **Game Speed** anywhere in the scene sets how fast the game runs, for a sim's or a strategy game's speed buttons: its **Speed** is 1 at normal speed, 4 four times as fast, 0.5 half and 0 paused. Buttons set it with no code: **Action** `Do`, **Targets** the entity it's on, **Do** `Set Speed` **With** `4` (or `0` for a pause button), or **Faster** and **Slower**, which go through its **Speeds** (`1`, `2`, `4` unless changed; add `16`), and a **Trigger** can do the same on a key's message. Its **Speed** is an output, for a **Counter** or a **Text** to show. While it's **Exact** (the default) a faster game takes more steps of the usual length each frame (`Time.FastForward`) rather than longer ones, so everything goes as at normal speed, only sooner: workers walking a grid cross as many cells in a game second at 16x as at 1x, bodies fall the same arcs, timers and coroutines finish on the same step, and the game plays out the same at any speed. The camera's pan keys, fades and menus go by real time and keep their pace. Untick **Exact** to make steps longer instead (`Time.Scale`), which costs the machine less; speeds below 1 always make steps shorter. A machine that can't take that many steps (it spends at most a twentieth of a second of each frame on them) runs the game slower rather than skipping any, and the profiler counts every step. Rewinding steps back through a fast-forwarded game as it does any other, so it reaches back the same game time in less real time. Fast-forward does nothing in a network game, whose steps are kept in step between the players; a script sets `Time.FastForward` and `Time.Scale` itself, and a Game Speed doesn't undo them unless its own speed changes.

## Button prompts

Put an action's name in braces in any text and it shows the key or button that does it, on whatever the player is using: `Press {Interact}` reads "Press E" on the keyboard and "Press Y" on a gamepad, and follows the player's own bindings. Braces around anything else are left alone.

## Controls screens

A button whose **Action** is **Rebind** and whose **Input Action** is `Jump` waits for the next key or gamepad button when clicked and makes it jump from then on: a key or mouse button takes the place of Jump's first key or mouse button, a gamepad button its first gamepad button, and the rest stay, so rebinding Drop Tool (Esc and Right Click) to Q keeps Right Click. Its **Binding Slot** picks which one it replaces (0 the first, 1 the second, for a row with a primary and a secondary key); a key pressed for a mouse button's slot (or the other way round) goes in before it, keeping both. Esc cancels. It's remembered next time. Label it `Jump: {Jump}` and it always shows the binding (`...` while it waits); `{DropTool:all}` shows every key and mouse button of the action (every gamepad button with a gamepad), `Esc / Right Click`, and `{PanUp:1}` the one in slot 1. **ResetControls** puts every action back.

Rather than a Rebind button for each action, add a **Controls List** to an entity with a **Layout**, such as an options panel: while the game runs it makes a row for every input action, its name and a Rebind button showing all its keys, which the layout lines up (and scrolls, with **Clip**). The rows follow the device in use and the player's rebinding, and are made again when the actions change. **Actions** picks which to list and in what order (`DropTool, Pan*`, with `*` for any letters); empty lists them all, or only the game's own with **Show** set to **GameOnly**. **Hide** leaves some out (`Debug*, Pause`), **Labels** renames them (`DropTool = Put Down`; otherwise `DropTool` reads `Drop Tool`), and **Slots** set to 2 gives each row a button for its first key and one for its second. The plain rows take a **Font**, **Text Size**, **Color**, **Button Color**, **Row Height** and **Button Width**. For rows of your own, style a row, disable it and name it in **Template**: each row is a copy, with `{name}` in its texts for the action's name, `{action}` (or `{action:all}`, `{action:1}`) for its keys, and a Rebind button with no Input Action rebinding its action. The Scene view shows sample rows where they'll go; the list's other children (a heading) are left alone. Scripts read its **Listed** actions and **Rows**.

For an on/off switch for each row of a data table (the events that slow the game, say), add a **Toggles List** to an entity with a **Layout**: name the **Table** and while the game runs it makes a row for each table row, its label and a real **Toggle** button, which the layout lines up. **Label Column** names the column holding the words (empty shows the row's name), **Default Column** a yes or no column saying whether each row starts on (empty starts them all on), and **Save As** the name each switch is kept under in the save data, with `{row}` for the row's name: `fire.{row}` keeps the Rain row's as `fire.Rain`, which scripts read with `Game.SaveData.GetBool("fire.Rain")` like a hand-placed Toggle. A value the player already saved wins over the table's default. **Hide** leaves rows out (`Debug*`), **On Text** and **Off Text** are what the buttons say, and the plain rows take the same **Font**, **Text Size**, **Color**, **Button Color**, **Row Height** and **Button Width**. For rows of your own, style a row with a Toggle, disable it and name it in **Template**: each row is a copy, with `{name}` in its texts for the label and `{row}` for the row's name, and a Toggle with no Save As is kept under the list's. The rows are made again when the table changes, and scripts read the list's **Listed** row names and **Rows**.

**Entity → UI → Choice** makes a button that picks one of its **Options** (one per row): left and right change it while it's picked, a click or the mouse wheel moves to the next, and its **Format** (`Difficulty: < {0} >`) labels it. With **Save As** (`difficulty`) it's remembered, for scripts to read with `Game.SaveData.GetString("difficulty")`.

## Buttons that can't be used yet

Untick a button's **Interactable** and it's drawn faded, ignores the mouse and its shortcut, and is skipped by the arrow keys. **Needs Saved** keeps it that way until something is saved under that name, for a Continue button that waits for a saved game.

## Showing things as they're unlocked

A **Show When** (Gameplay) on anything (a button, a sprite, a text, a whole toolbar) shows it only while its **Needs Flag** holds, with no code: a HUD that starts bare and brings in its tools as goals are met. With **Needs Flag** `run.Contracts`, the entity and everything inside it are hidden while the game runs until a trigger's **Set Flag**, a dialogue's `[set run.Contracts]` or a script's `Game.SetFlag("run.Contracts")` sets the flag, and hidden again when it's cleared; `not Tutorial` shows it only while that flag isn't set, and `run.Contracts or run.Work` while either is (a group's icon that shows once any of its tools is unlocked). "or" works in every **Needs Flag**: a door's, a switch's, a spawner's, a panel's. A hidden button can't be clicked and its **Shortcut** or **Shortcut Action** does nothing. It's looked at every frame, so it decides whether its entity shows: to hide it some other way, hide what it's inside. The Scene view shows it as ever. A flag named `run.` lasts one run of the game, and a **New Game** forgets every flag, so either way an unlock starts hidden again (see [Dialogue flags](/docs/story/#dialogue-flags)). A **Panel** has its own **Needs Flag**, which says when it can open. **Zoom Below** shows an entity by the camera instead of (or as well as) a flag: with `7` it shows while the main camera's **Size** is 7 or less and is hidden when the camera pulls out past that, so a label under each worker (a **Text** with a **Background**) is there when the camera is zoomed in on the floor and gone from afar, while the worker's icon, with no Zoom Below, shows at any zoom. 0, the default, ignores the zoom, an entity needs no flag to go by zoom alone, and with both a **Needs Flag** and a **Zoom Below** both have to hold. It shows or hides all at once rather than fading.

## New game and carrying on

A button whose **Action** is **NewGame** starts the game over: it forgets the progress kept in the save data (flags, saved inventories, health and counts, what menus saved, best scores) and the scenes remembered as they were left (see [Scenes that remember](/docs/mashups/#scenes-that-remember)) but not the player's own settings and controls (volumes, fullscreen, rebound keys), saved games or the lines they've read, and goes to its **Scene**. A game's own settings are kept too when their names start `settings.`: a Toggle's or Slider's **Save As** `settings.difficulty`, or a Toggles List's `settings.fire.{row}` (scripts ask `SaveData.IsSetting(name)`). Flags named `run.` aren't kept past the game closing either, so a game that starts straight into play without a New Game button still starts each time with them unset. Give it a **Needs Saved** and it waits until there's something to forget. A **LoadScene** button's **Saved Scene** names a value in the save data holding a scene to go to instead of its **Scene**: a Play button that carries on where the player left off, which goes to **Scene** until something's saved there (an adventure's walker keeps the room it's in with its **Save Room As**; see [Rooms](/docs/adventures/#rooms)). A **Continue** button loads the game saved last, in any slot, as it was (see [Saving and loading](/docs/visual-novels/#saving-and-loading)), an **Autosave**'s too (see [Autosave](/docs/visual-novels/#autosave)), and is faded while there's none. The Adventure template's menu has New Game and a carrying-on Play; the Visual Novel template's title screen has New Game and Continue.

## Nine-slice panels

Set a sprite's **Slice** to how much of its image is border (say 0.25 for a quarter at each edge) and its **Slice Size** to how big the border is drawn: its corners then stay crisp however you stretch its **Size**, for menu panels, dialogue boxes and buttons from one small image.

## Lists and grids

Add a **Layout** to an entity and it lines up its children, so nothing has to place them: a board of offer cards, a list of quest rows, a shop's shelf. Its **Direction** says how: **Vertical** one under another from the top, **Horizontal** one beside another from the left, or **Grid** in rows of **Columns**, each in a cell the size of the biggest child. They start at the top left corner of its **Size** (the area the Scene view's resize handles show, centered on the entity), inside its **Padding**, with **Spacing** between them, in the Hierarchy's order. Their own positions don't matter: a child spawned into it goes after the last, one destroyed or hidden leaves no gap, and in the Scene view they're put in place as you add, move or undo them.

```csharp
// A contract board: the layout places each card, no position needed.
var card = Game.Spawn("OfferCard", board);
```

A child takes the room of its Button's clickable area, its Sprite's **Size** or its own Layout's (a list of shelves), whichever is biggest, times its scale; one with only text takes what its letters are guessed to. Set **Cell Size** to give every child the same room (say 2 by 3 for cards). Put the background sprite on the layout's own entity: every child showing is placed.

Tick **Clip** and what doesn't fit its **Size** is cut off at its edges, and the mouse wheel over it scrolls it a row at a time (a click or the wheel there isn't the world's, as on a button). Scripts read how far it can go in **Most Scroll** and set **Scroll** themselves; `layout.Arrange()` places the children straight away, for a script that wants to know where one it just spawned went (otherwise they're placed after LateUpdate, before the frame is drawn).

Tick **Slide** and, while the game runs, its children slide to new places over **Slide Time** rather than jumping there: the rest close up when one is destroyed or hidden, make room for one spawned in, and glide as the list is scrolled or put in a new order. A child that's just been added (or shown again) takes its place at once.

### Rows the player puts in order

Tick **Reorderable** and the player sets the order of its children: a build queue, a research queue, the batches waiting for a machine. Press on a row and drag it up or down the list (about the grid, for a grid): it follows the mouse (or a finger), drawn over the rest, while the other rows slide out of the way (over **Slide Time**) to show where it would land, and letting go puts it there. A press that doesn't move is a click, so a Button on the row still works; Esc, or letting go off the list, puts the row back. Dragged near the end of a layout that clips, the list scrolls. With keys or a controller, pick a row's button with the arrows and press the layout's **Grab Action** ("Interact": E or Y) to lift it; the arrows then move it, and a second press (or Enter or A) puts it down, Esc or B back where it was.

Each move raises the layout's `Reordered(row, from, to)` event, the places counted from 0 among its **Rows** (the children it shows), and sends its **Reorder Message** with the row. A script that keeps its own list in the same order moves its item with `Layout.MoveIn`, and moves a row itself (for ▲ and ▼ buttons) with `Move`:

```csharp
var tray = Game.Find("Tray").GetComponent<Layout>();
tray.Reordered += (row, from, to) => Layout.MoveIn(batches, from, to);
tray.Move(walkIn, 0);   // to the top: Reordered fires as if the player had dragged it there
```

Set **Save Order As** to keep the order in the save data (the rows' names, separated by commas): it's saved as the player changes it and put back whenever rows with those names are in the list, such as a Toggles List's rows made again from its table, or a game loaded. Rows it doesn't name keep their places, so give each row its own name.

## HUD bars

**Entity → UI → Bar** makes a bar that fills to its **Value** out of **Max**, which way its **Direction** says. Set its **Track** to an entity's name (`Player`, `Boss`) and it shows that entity's health with no code; give it an **Output** too and it shows any of that entity's outputs (see [Outputs](/docs/story/#outputs)), out of what the output says it's out of (a match board's **Score** fills towards its **Target Score**) or the bar's own **Max**: a turn's **Time Left**, a faction's **Amount:Gold**. When it goes down, the part just lost lingers in the **Trail** color for a moment before draining away.

A bar can be drawn with your own pictures. Its **Fill Image** is laid over the whole bar and shown only as far as it's full, cut off there rather than squeezed, so a gauge's texture or tick marks stay crisp and in place as it fills (from the left, the right, the bottom or the top, as its **Direction** says); the trail shows the same picture. Its **Background Image** is drawn whole behind it, and with a **Background Slice** (and **Background Slice Size**) it's a nine-slice like a sprite's (see [Nine-slice panels](#nine-slice-panels)), so a frame's corners keep their shape however long the bar is. **Fill**, **Background** and **Trail** tint the pictures: set them to white to show the pictures as they are. A script's `drawing.Bar(...)` takes the same pictures as a `BarImages`.

A slider takes the same pictures, each optional: its **Fill Image** is laid over the whole slider and shown only as far as it's set, cut off rather than squeezed, tinted by **Fill**; its **Background Image** is the track, whole or a nine-slice with **Background Slice** and **Background Slice Size**, tinted by **Background**; and its **Knob Image** replaces the handle, drawn at the end of the filled part in the handle's usual box and tinted by **Knob**. Where it can be clicked and dragged doesn't change, and with no pictures set it is drawn in plain colors as before.

**Entity → UI → Stars** makes a row of **Stars**, so many filled: how well a level went. It shows an output (a Level Goal's **Stars**), a number in the save data (**Saved**: the best a level has had, for a level select menu), or its own **Value**, and with a **Delay** the stars pop in one after another with its **Sound**, going on under a pause. See [Winning a level](/docs/physics-games/#winning-a-level).

## Charts

**Entity → UI → Chart** makes a line graph for the HUD (pin it with a Screen Anchor) or the world: a factory's cards a day over the last three days, a town's population, a price. Its **Series** are a row each: a name and a color (`Cards: #4C9AFF`). Each keeps its last **Keep** points (60 to begin with), oldest on the left: adding one past that drops the oldest, so the chart scrolls. **Kind** draws a line through the points or a bar for each, the series' bars side by side.

With no code, a series samples an output: give its row one after `=` (`Cards: #4C9AFF = Cards Today`, `Gold: #F2BC4B = Amount:Gold`; the Inspector offers the outputs the entity has, see [Outputs](/docs/story/#outputs)) and set **Track** to the entity that has it (empty is the chart's own entity or what it's inside, `*` the first in the scene that has it). The chart takes a point as the game starts and another every **Sample Every** seconds of game time (1 to begin with), so a sped-up game fills it as fast as it plays, and a paused one not at all; one it can't read yet leaves a gap. A script feeds the rest: `chart.Add("Cards", 120)` adds a point (`chart.Add(120)` to the first series; a name no row has makes a series in the next color of `Chart.Palette`), `chart.Set("Cards", values)` gives a series all its points, and `chart.Clear()` (or `Clear("Cards")`) takes them away.

Its range runs from **Min** (0 to begin with, lower if a point is below it) up to a round number at or above the highest point (1, 1.2, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6 or 8 times a power of ten, as the Tables panel's chart does); set **Max** to fix it, points past it cut off at the top. Lines across mark the bottom, the middle and the top (and 0 when it's between), each with its number written short (950, 1.2k, 15k, 2.5M) or as **Format** says (`0.0`, `#,0`, `0%`). **X Labels** are words spread along the bottom from the left end to the right (`3 days ago`, `2 days ago`, `Yesterday`, `Today`), and **Legend** shows each series' name in its color along the top. It's drawn in its **Size** (resized in the Scene view like a bar), over its **Background**, with **Grid Color**, **Text Color**, **Text Size**, **Font** and **Line Width**, at its **Order**.

A chart is cheap to draw: a quad for each point and line, and its words are written again only when its range changes. A rewind puts its points back, and in a network game the host's points are sent to the other players.

## Tooltips

Add a **Tooltip** to anything (a sprite in the world, a button, a card on the HUD) and resting the mouse on it for its **Delay** (half a second to begin with, in real time, so it works under a pause menu) shows its **Value** in a box by the cursor, which goes away as the mouse leaves. Only the topmost one under the mouse shows: what's pinned to the screen (or a button the mouse is on) before the world, then the highest draw order; one in the world doesn't show under a button or a system's HUD, and while a panel is open only those on it do. Moving straight from one tooltip to the next shows the next at once. The part hovered is the entity's button, else its collider, else its sprite, or the **Area** you give it. The box is pinned to the screen and drawn over everything: its **Size** (a line's height, as a screen-anchored Text's), **Color**, **Background**, **Font**, **Offset** from the cursor (it flips to the other side near the screen's edge) and **Width** (lines break between words past it). Name a Text in **Into** (a `Hover Info` line on the HUD) and it says the tooltip there instead, then goes back to what it said before. Like a Text, it can show an output: **Track** and **Output** (`Cards`), with `{0}` and `{1}` in its Value (`{0} / {1} cards`), read only while it's the one hovered, so a thousand machines with tooltips cost nothing until one is. An action's name in braces in its Value shows the key that does it, as in a Text: `Key: {OpenWork}` reads `Key: K`, and follows the gamepad and the player's rebinding. A script sets `GetComponent<Tooltip>().Value` to say anything else (a deck's contract, its cards and when it's due), and reads `IsHovered`, `HoveredFor`, `IsShowing` and `Shown`.

## Pointing to something off the screen

**Entity → UI → Edge Pointer** makes an objective marker: an amber arrow with an **EdgePointer**, which shows the way to its **Target** (an entity's name, e.g. `Goal`) while that's out of the camera's view. It sits on the screen's edge, in by its **Margin**, where the line from the screen's middle to the target crosses it, turned to point at the target, and slides along the edge as the camera pans and zooms; once the target is in view it hides. With **In View** set to **Over Target** it sits over the target instead (at **Over Target** from it, (0, 1) over a worker's head) pointing down at it, so one arrow marks the target on the screen and off it. Its sprite is drawn pointing right. It's pinned to the screen as a ScreenAnchor is (drawn over the world, keeping its size with the camera's **HudSize** and the player's UI Scale, and a click on it isn't one in the world), laid out after the anchored HUD each frame, on the main camera's view. Its Button's **Do** is `Glide`, so clicking it glides the camera there with the camera's **CameraControls** and leaves it the player's (as `GlideTo` does). The `Distance` text inside it shows its **Distance** output (how far the target is from the screen's middle, in world units) as `{0:0}`; it sits behind the arrow, so on the screen's side of it, and stays upright however the arrow turns. Moving the goal on is a Trigger's or Button's **Point At** action, or a script's `pointer.Target = "Reader"` (or `TargetEntity` for an entity it has in hand); with no target, or none of that name, it hides. It decides whether its entity shows, so to hide it some other way, hide what it's inside. A script reads `IsOnEdge` and `Distance`.

## Juice: pops, counting numbers and kicks

A few touches make a game feel alive, with no code, each off until it's set:

- **Buttons that pop.** A **Button**'s **Hover Grow** draws it bigger under the mouse (0.08 is 8% bigger), **Press Shrink** smaller while it's held (0.06), and **Pop Time** eases it into each, its shading too (0.1 for a soft pop). Only how it's drawn changes: its clickable area stays its size.
- **Numbers that count.** A **Text** showing an **Output** with a **Count Time** counts up or down to a new number over that many seconds rather than jumping there: a score adding up as points come in, gold draining as it's spent.
- **Tween Player.** Add a **TweenPlayer** to anything for a little animation of how it's drawn: grown or shrunk (**Scale**, times its size), moved (**Move**), turned (**Rotate**, in degrees) and faded (**Fade**, 1 is gone), all at once, over **Duration** after a **Delay**, along an **Ease** (Out Back overshoots and settles, Out Bounce lands like a dropped ball). **From** (the default) starts at those values and settles to how the entity is, so as it comes it pops in from nothing; **To** goes the other way and stays there (fading out). It plays as the entity appears (**On Start**, also each time it's shown again), when its **Message** is sent, or from a script (`Play()`, `Stop()`, or a Trigger's Do), once or over and over (**Loop**: Repeat, or Ping Pong for a bob). What's inside the entity moves and fades with it, and its transform and collider stay put. A card popping in, a stamp landing (Scale 2, Fade 1, Out Bounce), a score jumping on its message (Scale 1.3), a coin bobbing (Move 0, 0.2, Ping Pong, In Out Sine).
- **Camera kicks.** Where a shake rattles the view every way, a kick pushes it one way and eases it back: a **Camera**'s **Kick By** says which way and how far (0, -0.3 jolts it down, as a landing does), over **Kick Time**. A Trigger's or Button's Do `Kick` on the camera kicks it; scripts call `Game.KickCamera(direction, seconds)`.

## Pickups and scores

Add a **Pickup** to a coin (with a trigger collider) and it's collected when the player touches it: it sends its **Message** (`Score`) with its **Amount**, plays its **Sound**, spawns its **Effect** (a sparkle prefab, in the coin's color) and disappears. **Layers** says who can collect it (`Player`; empty is anything with a body), and **Spin** turns it while it waits. A **Counter** on a text adds up its **Message** by itself, whatever sends it (a pickup, `Game.Send("Score", 10)`, an enemy's **Death Message**), and shows it with its **Format** (`Score: {0}`, `{1}` is the best; a counter that counts goals, moves or a match board's gems has `{2}` for what it's out of and `{3}` for how many are left). With **Save Best As** (`best`) it keeps the best score between runs, so a counter on the menu with the format `Best: {1}` shows it, and with **Save As** (`Coins`) it keeps the count itself, so a counter saved as the same name in the next scene (or a minigame) carries on from it (see [Carrying inventory, health and counts](/docs/mashups/#carrying-inventory-health-and-counts)). **One Per Message** adds 1 for each message whatever number it carries (a beat clock's **Beat** sends the beat's number, so a counter of beats would otherwise add 0, 1, 2...). With **Track** and **Output** it counts any entity's output instead of a message (a board's **Score**, the dungeon's **Floor**, a faction's **Amount:Gold**; see [Outputs](/docs/story/#outputs)), with `{2}` what it's out of. **Reaches** makes it do something at a value: once it's **At Least** its **Reach** (counting up to 10 coins) or **At Most** it (counting down to no moves left), it sends its **Reach Message** each time it gets there (a trigger opens a door on it), and its own output **Reached** is on while it's there, for a wire into a door's **Power**. Starting there (or a rewind putting it back there) isn't reaching it.

A counter can be multiplied by another: with **Multiply By** (`Multiplier`) everything its Message (and its **Add** action) adds counts that other counter's count times over, so a pinball score counts double and triple while a multiplier counter says so. **Max** stops a count going past it (a multiplier up to 5), and its **Set** action puts it at a value (a trigger setting the multiplier back to 1 as the ball drains). With **Save Best As**, its **New Best** output is on once the count beats the best kept as the scene started (a game over panel's text says "New best!" on it: `{0:'New best!';;''}`), and it sends its **New Best Message** the moment it does, with the count (a sound player's sting, a callout), unless nothing was kept yet. A count changing pops it bigger by its **Pop**, springing back, without any garbage.

The Arcade template shows all of this: a main menu, options (with a difficulty, a rebindable Fire button and an Audio Offset button that opens a calibration screen to tap along to), a game with a pause menu, and a game over panel with Retry and Menu, fading between them. Its bullets use collision layers to fly out of the ship, and its enemy spawner is a coroutine. On a gamepad it plays twin-stick: move with the left stick, aim with the right, fire with the right trigger. The Platformer and Top-down templates' coins, gems and scores are pickups and counters, with no scripts.
