## Players with no code

**Entity → Characters → Platformer Player** makes a player that runs and jumps straight away: its **Platformer Movement** runs at **Speed**, gets up to it and stops at its **Acceleration** and **Deceleration** (less of both in the air, by **Air Control**), and jumps **Jump Height** high with the Jump action. Jumps are forgiving: one pressed a moment before landing (**Jump Buffer**) or a moment after running off a ledge (**Coyote Time**) still happens, and letting go early makes a short hop. Falling is a little heavier than rising (**Fall Multiplier**), up to **Max Fall Speed**. Tick what the game needs: **Air Jumps** (1 is a double jump), **Wall Slide** and **Wall Jump**, and **Dash** (the Dash action, Shift or the gamepad's B: **Dash Speed** for **Dash Time**, once in the air until landing). Down and Jump on a one-way platform drops through it. It flips its sprite to face where it's going, plays sounds and a **Dust Effect** if you give it some, and if it has an **Animation** with clips named `Idle`, `Run`, `Jump`, `Fall`, `Climb`, `Dash` or `WallSlide`, it plays them itself. **Entity → Characters → Top-down Player** makes one with **Top-down Movement** instead: eight directions (or **Four Way**), an optional dash, and `Idle`/`Run` clips (with `IdleUp`, `RunDown` and so on, facing that way). It ignores gravity. **Entity → Characters → Grid Player** makes one with **Grid Movement**, for puzzle games, roguelikes and old-school RPGs: each press steps it one cell of the scene's tilemap (or the one its **Map** names), gliding there in **Step Time** seconds, and holding a way keeps it walking (after **Repeat Delay** seconds, or untick **Repeat** to step once a press); a press during a step is taken when it ends. Solid tiles, solid colliders and other grid movers block it, and it turns to face them instead, playing its **Bump Sound** once a press; triggers (coins, doors), one-way and background tiles don't, and layers its collider ignores don't either. It starts on the middle of the cell it's placed in, moves four ways (the way pressed last wins) or **Diagonal**ly too (never cutting a wall's corner), stays on the map unless **Stay On Map** is unticked, and plays `Idle` and `Walk` (or `Run`) clips, with `IdleUp`, `WalkDown` and so on. With no tilemap it steps a world unit at a time, between whole-number positions. Untick **Controllable** for a cutscene. When a player is selected, the Scene view draws how far it jumps each way.

## Who has the player's input

Everything that reads the keys, the mouse or the gamepad has a **Controllable** tick: the movements (platformer, top-down, grid and first person), match-3 and falling-block boards, the strategy **Commander**, the **Tactics Controls**, a card game's **Hand**, a visual novel's **Stage** (its Advance, Skip and Auto actions), the **Sandbox Tools**, a **Block Builder** (its hotbar and crafting go with it) and an RPG **Battler** (its attack, spell and item keys). Unticked, it ignores the player while the rest of it plays on: a board still clears and falls, a character still falls and lands. Each has a **Focus** action, which a trigger, a wire or a script (`Game.Focus(entity)`) does to hand it the input: it ticks Controllable on that entity and whatever is inside it, and unticks it on everything else the same player plays. A platformer's arcade cabinet focuses its match-3 board as the player walks up to it ("when `Play` arrives, Focus the Board"), and the board's `Won` message focuses the player again. A script's own component joins in by marking its true-or-false property `[Focus]`. In a network game the host says who has focus, and a player's own character gives it up only to what that player (or everyone) plays. See [Mixing genres](/docs/mashups/).

## Ladders, crates, springs and crumbling ledges

**Entity → Level → Ladder** makes a ladder (a trigger with a **Ladder**); up and down climb it without gravity, and Jump leaves it. Put a one-way platform at the top to stand on: climbing passes through it both ways, so down from the top climbs back down. **Entity → Physics → Crate** makes a crate with a **Pushable**: players walk into it to push it at its **Speed** (0.6 of theirs), along the ground in a platformer and any way in a top-down game, and it pushes crates in front of it until a wall stops them. It's on the `Crate` layer, so a pressure plate with `Crate` in its **Layers** can wait for it. In a scene with grid movers it's a grid crate instead (see [Sokoban crates and goals](/docs/puzzles/#sokoban-crates-and-goals)). **Entity → Physics → Spring** makes a spring: a **Bouncer** sends whatever lands on it flying at its **Strength** in its **Direction** (sideways makes a bumper; on a trigger it's a boost pad), giving a player its air jumps and dash back, and the Scene view draws where it sends things. Add a **Crumble** to a platform and once the player stands on it, it shakes for **Delay** seconds, falls away and comes back **Respawn** seconds later, once nobody's in the way.

## Bodies that rotate

Tick a **Body**'s **Rotates** and it turns: a crate landing on its corner tips over and falls flat, a plank hanging over a ledge tips off it, a ball thrown at the top of a tall plank knocks it over, and its box collider turns with the entity (the Scene view draws it turned). **Spin** is how fast it turns (degrees a second, anticlockwise; set it to start it spinning), **Grip** how well it grips what it touches (0 slides off an edge like ice, 1 hardly slips, so it tips over instead), and **Mass** with its collider's size how hard it is to turn. Everything else meets it as it's turned: rays and overlaps, shadows, a player who walks into a crate shoves it along and one who runs into a plank's end tips it, a weight dropped on one end of a seesaw lowers it, and a player walks up a tilted plank like a ramp (a turned, **Frozen** box is a ramp or a tilted wall that stays put). It lands on tilemaps, slopes and one-way platforms, meets a hex map's hexagons with its turned box, and on an isometric map meets the walls with the diamond under it, as everything does there. Bodies that don't rotate (the default) stay upright whatever hits them, as players and enemies should, and every existing scene and template works as it did. Welded into a contraption, bodies are one body that turns when all of them rotate (see [Contraptions and wiring](/docs/genres/#contraptions-and-wiring)).

## Stacks

Bodies that rotate are worked out together: every one touching another, and what they touch, in one go, in quarter steps, each starting from how hard every point pushed in the quarter before (and the first from the step before, which the scene remembers), so a tower's weight is carried all the way down to the floor from the start; the two corners of a crate lying flat on another are pushed together, so it lands straight and neither rocks nor creeps, and overlaps are eased apart rather than kicked. Towers of ten crates and pyramids of fifty-five (or two hundred and ten) stand, falling asleep within a second or two, crates stacked a little off true settle without toppling, and crates dropped in a heap settle, on colliders, tile floors, slopes and hex maps alike (a jumbled heap of sixty in a bin sleeps within two seconds: one wedged between a slant below it and a side a little above its middle still counts as held up). Bodies touching fall asleep together while any of them is moving; once they're all as good as still, each falls asleep after half a second still by itself, so a deep heap sleeps a part at a time rather than waiting for every crate in it to be still at once (two hundred jumbled crates in a bin sleep within four seconds). A body moving into a sleeper wakes it; one that's all but still leans on it as on something that holds still. Once a pile has stayed still for half a second it falls **Asleep**: it stays exactly where it is and costs a fraction of what it did (each step it only checks it still has something under it) until something disturbs it: a hit, a push or a spin from a script, a player walking into it, something pushed into it, or what it rests on moving or being taken away. Then it wakes, and so does everything moving it touches; taking out or turning off something beside it, or clearing tiles under it, wakes it too, so a crate held up by one that's gone falls. Someone walking into a tower leans on it (a crate rocking into their side doesn't lift them on top of it), and a pile shoved against someone standing still settles and sleeps there. **Still For** counts how long it's been still. Tick **Asleep** to start a pile asleep, so a tower built resting on itself stands untouched until something hits it (a crate set asleep in the air isn't held up by anything, so it wakes and falls). Sleep is saved with the scene, and how hard each point pushed is kept with it (rewind snapshots keep it too), so a rewind, or the same game played twice, ends the same to the last bit; in a network game the host works it out and players see it as it is. A pyramid of 55 crates and a tower of 10 cost about 0.3 ms a physics step while they're awake and 0.14 ms asleep; a pyramid of 210, 1.2 and 0.5 ms; none of it makes garbage, and BunnyMark is as fast as before (`dotnet run -c Release tools\stackbench.cs -- --rows 10 --tower` times them on your computer).

## Balls that roll

A body with a **Circle Collider** that **Rotates** is a ball, and it rolls. On flat ground, down slopes (a tilted **Frozen** box, or slope tiles), over tile floors and hex floors it rolls without slipping: its spin keeps pace with how fast it goes, so its sprite turns just as far as it has rolled (a ball of radius 0.5 turns a full circle every 3.14 units). Set going without spin, it skids at first and its **Grip** soon has it rolling, at two thirds of the speed it skidded at, as a real ball does. **Rolling Resistance** brings it to rest: 0 rolls on and on, 0.1 (the default) stops a ball rolling at 5 units a second in about 3 seconds and 7.5 units, and a ball rests on a slope gentler than its rolling resistance (rise over run) and rolls down a steeper one. **Friction** slows only its sliding, so it turns a skid into rolling and leaves rolling alone. Its spin goes into its bounces: one dropped spinning bounces off the way its spin carries it (topspin forwards, backspin back), one thrown along lands spinning, and a bouncy one rolling into a wall bounces back off it. Rolling over where two boxes, platforms or tile runs meet end to end it doesn't catch on the next one's corner. Balls roll up against crates and walls and rest there, heap up in a bin, stand stacked two with one on top, and fall asleep like any pile (a heap of two hundred dropped in a bin within three seconds); a pile that wakes picks up from how hard its balls pushed when they fell asleep, so it doesn't sag.

A player walking into a ball pushes it along ahead of them and it rolls on when they stop; running into it harder kicks it further. From a script, set its `Velocity` (a kick: it skids, then rolls) or push it off its middle with `PushAt` to put spin on it (under its middle for backspin, over it for topspin). In a top-down game (its **Gravity Scale** 0, on an isometric map or anywhere else) it rolls on the ground under the view: rolling resistance slows it just the same, and its spin (from a glancing hit) dies away too, but its sprite only turns with that spin, since a picture turned on the screen can't show a ball rolling away from you. Give something floating in space a rolling resistance of 0 and it drifts on. A heap of 60 balls costs about 0.14 ms a physics step awake and 0.06 ms asleep, 200 balls 0.5 and 0.23 ms, with no garbage (`dotnet run -c Release tools\stackbench.cs -- --balls 60`). The Contraption template's **Ball** rolls.

## Polygon colliders

A **Polygon Collider** is a shape with straight sides between its **Points**: a ramp, a wedge, a rock, a hill, a boat's hull, a cup. Its points are "x y" pairs separated by semicolons, in its own units round the entity (plus its **Offset**, and grown with the entity's scale): `-1 0; 1 0; 0 1` is a triangle two wide and one tall, and they can go round either way. Select it and the Scene view puts a knob on each point and a plus halfway along each side: drag a knob to move its point, drag a plus to add one there, double-click a knob to take its point off (it keeps three). Hold Ctrl to snap to half units. Each drag is one undo step, and clicking inside a polygon with nothing drawn there selects it. An adventure's walk areas and hotspots are edited the same way (see [Point-and-click adventures](/docs/adventures/)). **Entity → Fit Polygon Collider to Sprite** traces round the solid part of the selected entity's sprite (the frame it shows, its biggest blob with any holes filled in) and makes that its points, up to twelve that hug it without crossing, in place of its box or circle collider (keeping whether it's a trigger and its layers); tidy it up by dragging the points.

A convex shape is one piece; a concave one (an L, a cup, a crescent) is split into convex pieces by itself, which the Scene view shows as faint lines inside it, and it collides as the shape it is: a ball dropped into a cup rolls to its floor and stays in it, and something sliding over a join between two pieces doesn't catch on it. Points that don't make a proper shape never stop anything working: they're fixed up and the Inspector says what was wrong above its fields (agents see the same note). Fewer than three points make a square one unit across (round the one point, if it has one), two points or points all in a line a bar a tenth of a unit thick along them, sides that cross the shape round all the points, and a point that doubles back on itself along a side is left out; the Scene view draws such a shape in red as it's fixed up, with the points as they're written dashed over it. Only the first 64 points count.

Like a box, it stays upright whatever the entity's rotation unless its **Body** **Rotates**: then it turns with the entity round where it balances (its `WorldCentroid`, worked out from its area, so a triangle turns round its middle third of the way up), and its mass is spread over its area, so a long thin shape turns slowly. It tips over onto a side and rests there, rolls end over end down a slope if it's round enough (an octagon rolls down a ramp and comes to rest flat on the floor), stacks (a tower of hexagons stands), heaps up in a bin with crates and balls and falls asleep with them, and lands on tile floors, slope tiles and hex floors, and, on an isometric map, meets the walls with the diamond under it, as everything does there. A body that doesn't rotate can have one too, staying upright, and a static one (with no body) is a wall or a hill: a player walks up and down its slant as they do a tilted plank, and lands on it where it is rather than on the box round it. Everything else meets it as the shape it is: rays (`Game.Physics.Raycast` hits its sides, never a join inside it), overlaps, triggers (a polygon trigger tells what comes into it), shadows (from each side) and grid movers (a cell whose middle it covers is a wall). Rewinds and network games play it out the same to the last bit, and it makes no garbage a step. A heap of 60 polygons (triangles, pentagons, hexagons and concave Ls) in a bin costs about 0.23 ms a physics step awake and 0.15 ms asleep, and falls asleep within two seconds (two hundred take about nine) (`dotnet run -c Release tools\stackbench.cs -- --shapes 60`).

## Hinges

A **Hinge Joint** pins its body to another (**To**, by name) or, with **To** empty, to a point in the world, at its **Anchor** (in its own units from its position: (0, 0) is its middle, as a wheel's; (-2, 0) one end of a plank four long, as a door's), and it turns there: a wheel on a cart, a door, a flipper, a seesaw, a windmill, a chain of links. **Entity → Add Hinge** hinges the selection at its middle, to the world, or with two selected, to the one picked first (pick the cart, then Ctrl+click the wheel), ticking its body's **Rotates**. The Scene view draws a pin where every hinge is, and on the selection puts a knob on its anchor to drag (Ctrl snaps to half units) and, with **Limit** ticked, a wedge it swings through with a knob on each end to drag its **Lower Angle** and **Upper Angle** (whole degrees; Ctrl snaps to fifteen), each drag one undo step (double-click an end to untick Limit). The two are joined as they stand when the game starts (**Joined**, with the **Other Anchor** and **Start Angle** the game works out; untick it to join them again where they are), so its angles count from how they're placed, from -180 to 180, and **Angle** says how far it has turned (an output, so a wire can read it).

**Limit** keeps it between its two angles (a door that opens one way, a flipper's travel). **Motor** turns it at **Motor Speed** (degrees a second, anticlockwise) as hard as **Motor Strength** lets it (a torque: a heavy load turns it slowly or stalls it); a switch or a wire into its **Power** turns the motor on and off (a flipper on a button), and its **Reverse** action turns it the other way. **Friction** (0.05 by default) makes it stiff, the more so the more weight hangs on it: 0 swings on and on, 1 stops a swinging door soon (a little off straight down). The two it joins don't bump into each other unless **Collide** is ticked. What it's hinged to turns with it if that rotates too, and is pulled about by it if it moves with physics (a body that rotates, a welded contraption, one hung on a hinge itself); anything else, a player, a wall, a platform moved by a script, just carries the hinge along. A body that doesn't rotate hangs upright from its hinge, as a Ferris wheel's seats do.

Hinges are worked out with the bodies that turn, in the same quarter steps and before their contacts, each starting from how hard it pulled the step before (kept with the scene, so a rewind or a network game plays them out the same to the last bit), and a hinge holds its two together to about a thousandth of a unit. Hinged bodies fall asleep together when they've been still a while (one hanging from a hinge counts as held up), but not while a motor runs. Twelve chains of five links cost about 0.1 ms a physics step swinging, with no garbage (`dotnet run -c Release tools\stackbench.cs -- --hinges 60`; `--welded 60` heaps welded pairs).

## Ropes

A **Rope** ties its entity (at its **Anchor**, in its own units from its position: (0, 0.5) is the top of a crate one high) to another (**To**, by name, at its **Other Anchor** in that one's own units) or, with **To** empty, to a point in the world (**Other Anchor** is then where in the world). Tick **Loose End** to leave that end hanging free instead, starting out towards **Other Anchor** (and on straight down for whatever length is left): a rope to climb and swing on, a bell pull. Its **Length** is how long it is (0: as far apart as its ends are as the game starts); the ends can come closer, and it sags, but no further apart. Either end can be a body, pulled about by it (and turned, if it rotates: a crate hung by its corner turns to hang corner up and rocks about), something that doesn't move, or the world; a balloon tied to a crate floats up to the end of its rope and lifts a light one. A body that doesn't rotate hangs upright from it. Changing **Length** while the game runs winches what hangs on it up and down.

**Entity → Add Rope** ties a rope to the selection: with two selected, from the middle of the one picked last to the middle of the one picked first, a little longer than they're apart; one with a body, hanging from a hook in the world three above its top; one without (a ceiling, a branch), hanging loose three down from its bottom. The Scene view draws it hanging as it will at rest, a ring at each tied end, and on the selection a knob at each end to drag (its start in its entity's own units, its other end in the own units of what it's tied to, or in the world) and one at its middle for its **Length**: drag it down to make it longer, up to shorten it (Ctrl snaps to half units); double-click it for as long as its ends are apart. Each drag is one undo step.

It hangs and sags under gravity (**Gravity Scale**: 0 in a top-down game, where it lies where it's put), swings, and comes to rest. With **Collide** ticked (the default) it meets the world, as thick as it's drawn (**Width**, at least 0.08): it lies on the ground, drapes over boxes, crates and tile floors, slopes and hex walls, and wraps round corners, so a crate swinging on it round a peg hangs from the peg and swings round it. It doesn't push what it lies on (it weighs next to nothing), and it hangs in front of players rather than meeting them, for them to grab. **Stiffness** (1, the default) doesn't stretch; lower stretches like elastic, so what hangs on it bounces: at 0.5 a bungee cord (a crate stretches it about 0.3), at 0 a rubber band. It's drawn as a smooth curve in its **Color**.

**Climbable** (on by default): a player with **Platformer Movement** grabs it with up where its hands meet it (in the air too), hangs on it, climbs up and down it at its **Climb Speed** (never into what it's tied to; off the bottom of a loose end, it lets go), swings on it left and right, and jumps off it with Jump, keeping its swing (holding down, it just lets go). **Is On Rope** says it's on one, and it plays its Climb clip. Its weight hangs on the rope, so a rope between two posts sags under it.

**Break Force** (0, never) is how hard it can be pulled before it snaps, in the units of gravity's pull (a crate of Mass 1 hanging still pulls 25); its **Tension** output says how hard it's pulled now. Snapped (or **Cut**, an action a wire or script can call), its two halves hang from their ends, **Broke** turns on (an output: wire it to a lamp or a gate; untick it, or call **Mend**, to join it up again) and it sends its **Break Message**, with the entity as its data.

Ropes are worked out with the bodies that turn, in the same quarter steps as contacts and hinges, as a chain of short pieces (one about every quarter unit, up to 48), each held its length apart and every point held within the rope's length of a tied end so it doesn't stretch under its own weight, while what hangs on it is held no further from what holds it up than the rope between them. A rope still a while falls asleep until something moves near it, its ends move, someone grabs it, or it's cut. Everything it knows (its points, how fast they go, how hard each piece pulls, who hangs on it) is kept with the scene, so a rewind plays it out the same to the last bit; in a network game the host swings ropes and what hangs on them, and each player's machine hangs its own copy of each rope between where the host has its ends (a player climbs on their own machine). Forty ropes swinging over pegs, half with crates on, cost about half a millisecond a physics step, with no garbage (`dotnet run -c Release tools\stackbench.cs -- --ropes 40`).

## Cameras

Add a **Follow** to the camera and set its **Target** (`Player`) and it follows with no code. **Smoothing** sets how quickly it catches up (0 sticks exactly), **Offset** shifts the view (say `0, 2` to see more above the hero), **Dead Zone** is a box the target can move about in without the camera moving (a tall one keeps a platformer's camera still through jumps), and **Look Ahead** leads the way the target is moving. Set **Stay Inside** to the name of the level's tilemap, a big box collider or a backdrop sprite, and the camera never shows past its edges. Follow works on anything, not just cameras: a pet that trails its owner. For top-down games, tick the camera's **Sort By Y** so whatever is lower on screen draws in front. A sprite sorts by the bottom of its picture, where it stands, so a tall tree and the hero beside it sort by their feet.

## Viewports

A camera with a **Viewport** draws into part of the screen, over the main view: a minimap in a corner, each player's half of a split screen, or a window looking through someone's eyes in a 2D game. **Left**, **Top**, **Width** and **Height** place it, as fractions of the screen from its top left (`0, 0, 0.5, 1` is the left half), so it keeps its place at any window size; the camera's **Size** is half the view's height, and its width follows the part's shape. **Order** says which goes on top where views overlap, **Border** frames it (transparent for none), and **Shown** hides it (a switch or wire powers it, for a map shown while a lever is on). The main view is the first camera without a viewport. Anything riding inside a viewport's camera (its children) is that view's own HUD; the main view's HUD (anything with a **Screen Anchor**, or inside the main camera), menus and fades stay on top of every view. A **Follow** on a viewport's camera keeps its own view inside **Stay Inside**. Give a viewport's camera a **First Person View** and that window shows the game through the viewer's eyes while the rest of the screen stays 2D (it leaves the mouse to the 2D game). Viewports only draw, so rewinds and network games are as ever, and they're drawn the same on the desktop and in the browser. In a script, `Game.MouseIn(camera, out var world)` says whether the mouse is over a camera's part of the screen and where in the world that is through it (for clicking on a minimap), and `Game.AspectOf(camera)` the shape it's drawn at.
