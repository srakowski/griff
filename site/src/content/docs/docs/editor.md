Griff's editor is where a game is made and played at once. These are its parts and what each does, starting with the shortcuts. Every command is listed in the [command reference](/reference/commands/).

## Shortcuts

| Action | Shortcut |
| --- | --- |
| New project (pick a template) | Ctrl+Shift+N |
| Open project | Ctrl+O |
| Save scene | Ctrl+S |
| Undo / redo | Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y |
| Add Entity (find something to make by typing) | Ctrl+Shift+A |
| Create empty entity | Ctrl+Alt+N |
| Rename, duplicate, delete | F2, Ctrl+D, Delete |
| Play / pause / step / stop | F5 / F6 / F10 / Shift+F5 |
| Play with 2 players (a window for player 2) | Ctrl+F5 |
| Breakpoint on the line (in a script) / remove them all | F9 / Ctrl+Shift+F9 |
| Stopped at a line: continue / step over / step into / step out | F5 (or F6) / F10 / F11 / Shift+F11 |
| Cut / copy / paste entities | Ctrl+X / Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V |
| Build game / small game | Ctrl+B / Ctrl+Shift+B |
| Mute the editor (kept between sessions) | Ctrl+Shift+M |

**New Project** takes a name, a folder and a template. Its template list scrolls (mouse wheel, or the arrow keys, Page
Up/Down, Home and End once it has focus) and keeps the picked template in view, while the name, the folder and Create
always show. The window can be resized, and like every editor window that opens centered (the Component Catalog,
Controls, the Sprite Painter...), it opens no bigger than the work area of the screen it opens on, and wholly on it,
whatever that screen's size and scaling.

In the **Scene view**:
- Scroll to zoom.
- Middle-drag, right-drag or Alt-drag to pan.
- Click to select; Ctrl- or Shift-click adds one or takes it away.
- Drag from empty space to select everything inside the box (hold Ctrl or Shift to add to the selection).
- Drag to move: everything selected moves together, as one undo step (hold Ctrl to snap the one picked last to the grid).
- Drag the round handle above the selected entity to rotate it. With several selected, they all turn around the one picked last, as one undo step. Angles land on whole degrees; hold Ctrl to snap to 15 degrees.
- Drag the square handles on the selected entity's corners to scale it, or on its edges to stretch it one way. It grows or shrinks around its position, along its own axes; with several selected, they all scale around the one picked last, as one undo step. Hold Shift to keep its proportions, and Ctrl to snap to tenths; otherwise the scale lands on hundredths.
- Press R (or **View → Resize Handles**) and the square handles resize the selected entity's sprite instead, changing its **Size** rather than its scale, so a platform grows longer without stretching the coins on it. Like the scale handles it grows around its position: the opposite edge moves out as much, and its colliders and children stay where they are. Press C (**Collider Handles**) for green handles on its box and circle colliders: a box's edges and corners change its **Size** and **Offset** (the opposite side stays put), and a circle's edge changes its **Radius** around its middle. Hold Shift to keep proportions, and Ctrl to snap sizes to half units (a circle's radius to quarters); otherwise they land on hundredths. Each drag is one undo step. Press S to go back to scaling; the corner of the Scene view says which handles are out.
- The selected entity's distances show as dashed circles around it: how far its light reaches, its sound is heard, it notices the player or shoots, a unit sees. Press D (**View → Distance Handles**) for a round knob on each circle, named beside it: drag one to change how far it reaches, as one undo step. Hold Ctrl to snap to half units; otherwise it lands on hundredths. A distance of 0 (often "anywhere") has no circle; set it in the Inspector.
- A selected mover's path and a selected patrol's or grid patrol's route have knobs to drag, whichever handles are out. A **Mover**'s points are numbered from its start (where it's placed: move the entity to move the start); drag one to move it, drag the small plus between two points (or past the last, or on the way back to the start of a loop) to add one there, and double-click one to take it off. A **Grid Patrol**'s points work the same way, but each lands in the middle of the cell it's dropped on (counted in cells from the one it was placed on), and its pluses sit in cells too: the one halfway between two points, and the next along its last stretch. A **Patrol**'s **Left** and **Right** turns slide along its route; drag one back past where the patrol was placed to clear it, so it walks on until a wall or an edge on that side (a hollow knob at the end of a dotted arrow; drag it out to set it). Hold Ctrl to snap to half units; otherwise points land on hundredths (a grid patrol's always land on cells). Each drag is one undo step.
- Arrows link the selection to the entities its fields name, and to it from the fields elsewhere that name it, each labelled with its fields: what a switch turns on and hides, what a camera follows, where a door leads, what a trigger drives (labelled with what it does there, "Score: Damage 2"). Where something comes from (a wire's or a trigger's **From**, what a counter, bar or text reads in its **Track**) points the other way, from it, labelled with what comes: a wire's "On to Power", a counter's "Score". A name several entities share links to each of them; one that matches nothing has no arrow (the Inspector flags it).
- Press F to frame the selection.
- Arrow keys nudge everything selected by half a unit (Shift for a tenth).
- Drop an image to create a sprite, or a prefab to place a copy of it (linked to the prefab: see [Prefabs](/docs/scenes-and-components/#prefabs)).

## Arranging panels

Drag a panel's tab (Hierarchy, Inspector, Assets, Output, Profiler, Tables, Simulations) to move it; a box shows where it will go before you let go. Drop it right by an edge of the window to dock it along that whole side, near an edge of another panel (or of the Scene and Game tabs) to dock it beside that, or on another panel's tabs or middle to tab it with them. Drop it over the middle of the Scene view, or outside the editor, and it floats in a window of its own, which keeps the editor's shortcuts; hold Ctrl to float it wherever it's dropped, and press Escape to call a drag off. Drag the gutters between panels to resize them. Closing a floating window puts its panels back with the ones they start beside.

## Saving layouts

The editor opens where you left it, with the panels where you left them, floating windows and all. **View → Save Layout...** saves where they are under a name (an art layout, a level-building one, one for recording), **View → Layouts** switches to one, **View → Delete Layout** forgets one, and **View → Reset Layout** puts every panel back where it starts out. Layouts are yours, not the project's: they're kept for every project you open. A floating window that was on a screen that's since gone opens in the middle of the editor.
- Lock a backdrop or a big tilemap so clicks and box selections pass through it to what's behind: click the lock by its row in the Hierarchy (it shows when you hover), or **Entity → Lock**. Everything inside a locked entity is locked with it. Locked entities can still be selected in the Hierarchy, and nudged or edited in the Inspector; the game ignores locking.
- Hide a pause menu, a dialogue box or a tilemap layer that covers the level while you work on what's under it: click the eye by its row in the Hierarchy (it shows when you hover), press H in the Scene view or the Hierarchy, or **Entity → Hide**. The Scene view doesn't draw it or anything inside it, and clicks and box selections go to what's underneath. Hidden is saved with the scene, so it stays out of the way next time; the Hierarchy still selects it (and the Scene view outlines it while it's selected), and the game shows it as usual.

In the **Hierarchy**:
- Type in the filter at the top to show only the entities whose names have it in them (`slime` finds **Slime** and **Big Slime**), with the entities they're inside shown faintly and opened to show them. Escape (or the ×) empties it and puts the tree back as it was; it stays while you work, so new slimes show up in it.
- Click to select; Ctrl-click adds or takes one away, Shift-click selects every row from the last one clicked (Ctrl+Shift-click adds them).
- Delete, duplicate, cut, copy and paste work on everything selected, as one undo step. Duplicates go right after their originals, pastes after the selected entity, and the copies end up selected.
- Drag a row onto another to put it inside, or above or below it.
- The lock by a row locks it in the Scene view (all of the selection, if the row is one of them). Locked rows keep their lock showing; rows a parent's lock covers show it faintly.
- The eye by a row hides it in the Scene view the same way, and shows it again. Hidden rows keep a shut eye and a faded name; rows a parent's hiding covers show it faintly.
- The Inspector edits them all at once, and the Scene view outlines them all.

## The Controls window

**Game → Controls...** lists every input action the game has and the keys, mouse buttons and gamepad buttons that do each. Pick an action, click **Press a Key** and press one (or click the button with a mouse button) to add it; controller buttons come from **Add Button** with no controller needed, or **Press on Controller** with one. A key that another action also uses is pointed out, the × on a key takes it off, and **Reset to Default** puts one of Griff's twenty-two actions (Jump, Fire, Dash, Attack, Interact, Pause, EndTurn, Undo, Restart, Skip, Auto, Log, LeftFlipper, RightFlipper, Plunger, Nudge, Punch, Kick, and Lane1 to Lane4, a rhythm game's lanes) back; they can be rebound but keep their names, since components use them. **Add Action** makes one of the game's own (`Shield`), which scripts read with `Input.WasPressed("Shield")` and button prompts and Rebind buttons work with. Changes are saved in `project.griff` as you make them (only what differs from Griff's defaults), reach a running game straight away, and go into exported games. A player's own Rebind choice still wins over them, and a script's `Input.Bind` over both; the window says when a choice you made while testing is in the way (**Game → Clear Save Data** clears it).

## Step rate

**Game → Step Rate** sets how many steps a second the game takes: 60 unless you change it, with 30, 120 and 240 in its submenu (the one in use is ticked) and any rate from 10 to 240 when you run it from an agent or give it a number. A faster rate gives fast bodies finer steps for more work a second; the screen is drawn smoothly between steps whatever it is. It's saved in `project.griff` straight away (undo puts it back, without leaving the scene unsaved), reaches a running game, and goes into exported games; a script can still change `Time.StepRate` as the game runs.

## The Profiler

The **Profiler** panel (beside Assets and Output, or **Game → Profiler**) shows how the game playing in the Game tab runs. Its graph has a bar for each of the last ten seconds of frames, newest on the right: how long the frame was, and from the bottom, what of it the game's steps took (scripts, physics and everything else that moves it on; orange), building the picture to draw (blue) and drawing it (green), the rest being time to spare, with dashed lines where a frame is as long as 60 and 30 frames a second allow (the graph's height doubles to fit a long frame). Above it are the frame rate over the graph's frames, its 1% low (the rate of its slowest 1% of frames), the average and worst frame, what the steps, building and drawing took on average, and the **garbage** a frame makes on average (memory the game takes for a moment and lets go, which the garbage collector has to clear up, holding up a frame now and then) with how many times the collector ran. Beside it, **Costs a frame** lists what each kind of script (all the Bunny scripts together, their Update, FixedUpdate and LateUpdate), each of the game's own steps (Physics, Animations, Particles, Movers...: Griff's components at work), each system a script added, each part of building the picture, drawing it, and the editor's own work (its rewind snapshots and its panels following the game, which an exported game doesn't do) cost a frame on average, in milliseconds, and the garbage each makes, the most first, with a bar for its share of the frame's work; what takes next to nothing and makes no garbage is left out. Building the picture is split by what's drawn (marked *Build*): each kind of component that draws (Sprite, Tilemap, Text, ParticleEmitter, a script's own drawing), with its entities' upkeep (placing each between steps, what it sorts by), each system's Draw (Griff's own too, like Tactics or Strategy, named for the system), **Sorting** (putting the draws back to front; for a big scene built across the cores, merging the chunks' runs and putting each draw in its place), **Lights** (which lights reach the view and their shadows, in the dark), **Clearing** (emptying the last frame's draws to reuse them; a big scene's frame overwrites them instead), **Camera**, **Systems** (getting the frame ready for them) and **Screen** (fades, flashes and debug shapes over it all); together they're the whole build, timed a run of one kind at a time, so a crowd of 50,000 sprites costs a pair of timestamps (built across the cores, its sprites are timed as they're drawn on all of them at once: the time the frame waits for them). A crowd of scripts (see Crowds, under [Scripting](/docs/scripting/)) is timed as its kind the same way, and the garbage counted includes what the worker threads make. Then come how many entities the scene holds, how many are active (enabled, with every parent enabled: what the game runs and draws), how many things were drawn in the newest frame, and how many of each component the active entities have, the most first, counted twice a second. The game is timed only while the panel is on screen and recording, so with its tab hidden it costs nothing; **Record** pauses the recording to hold the graph still while the game plays on, **Clear** starts it afresh, and each Play starts a fresh graph. Agents show it with the `Game_Profiler` tool, pause and clear it with `Profiler_Record` and `Profiler_Clear`, and read it with `ui_inspect` on `ProfilerPanel` (`Summary`, `Costs`, `Counts`). A script or tool timing frames of its own sets a `FrameProfiler` as `Game.Profiler`: whatever runs the game adds each frame it shows to it, and while it's set the game times its steps, scripts and systems, and the parts of its picture, into it (`FrameProfiler.Costs`; `FrameCostKind.Build` for the picture's parts); with none set, the game takes the same steps it always does, timing nothing.

## The BunnyMark template

The **BunnyMark** template is a benchmark rather than a game: bunnies pour in from the top left and bounce about the view, more and more while the game keeps up 60 frames a second, until it has found how many this computer holds there. Each bunny is an ordinary entity, a **Sprite** and a `Bunny` script (falling, bouncing off the floor and the sides), copied from the `Bunny` prefab, so the count says how much an ordinary game can put on screen. `Bunny` is a crowd script (`[Crowd]`: it changes nothing but its own bunny), so the bunnies' scripts run across the cores, as their sprites are drawn. The `BunnyMark` script starts with **Start Count** (`100`) and times each count for **Window** (`1`) second: while the frame rate (the frames the screen shows a second, from `Time.DrawnFrames` and `Time.RealTotal`; the game itself takes 60 steps a second however many frames are drawn) keeps up **Target Fps** (a frame missed in the second still counts), it adds **Growth** (`0.5`, half as many again); when it falls behind, it takes the last lot away and tries half the step, until a step would add less than **Precision** (`0.02`). Then it holds the count for **Hold Seconds** (`10`, after two to settle), timing every frame the screen shows: the frame rate, its 1% low (the frame rate of the slowest frames, from the time 99 frames in 100 came within: a frame that misses the screen's beat takes twice as long, so a low under the frame rate means some did) and the garbage collections meanwhile. It shows *Holds 1,015 bunnies at 60 fps (best 1,147); held for 10 s: 60 fps, 1% low 60 fps, 0 garbage collections* and saves them (`held`, `best`, `fps`, `low` and `collections`). **Hold Count** holds that many bunnies straight away rather than finding the count, to check a count (*Held 50,000 bunnies for 10 s: ...*). The frame rate and the count show in the top left all the while, and Space runs it again. It's silent, so it times nothing but itself. While it plays, `entity_get` shows its `count`, `held`, `fps`, `low`, `collections` and `isDone`.

## Painting sprites and tilesets

**Art → New Sprite** opens the Sprite Painter, for pixel art. Paint with the **Pencil** (B), **Eraser** (E), **Fill** (G), **Line** (L), **Rectangle** (R) and **Ellipse** (O), or pick a color off the picture with the picker (I, or Alt-click); right-dragging erases. **Mirror** (M) paints both halves at once, and Ctrl+Z undoes a stroke at a time. Colors come from a palette (**Jam**, **Earth**, **Handheld** or **Grays**) or any `#RRGGBB`. Pick a starting point (**Creature**, **Ship**, **Item**, **Ground** or **Bricks**) for a fresh one made from scratch, and **Another** for a different one. A sprite is frames played in order: **Add Frame** copies the one you're on, **Onion skin** shows the frame before underneath, and the preview plays them at the **Speed** you set. **Art → New Tileset** (or the **Tileset** button) paints a grid of tiles instead, numbered from 0 as a tilemap's rows name them, with a preview that repeats the tile to check it joins up. **Save** puts it in `Sprites/` or `Tiles/` as a PNG that remembers its frames or tiles, so it opens again exactly as it was made. Every image field in the Inspector (a sprite's or tilemap's **Image**, particles, first-person weapons) shows a preview, a list of the project's pictures, and a **Paint** button that opens the painter on its picture (or a new one) and puts each save on it in one undo step: a sprite takes its **Columns** and **Rows** and an **Animation** to play several frames, a tilemap its tile grid, and a colored placeholder box loses its tint. **Art → Paint Selected** does the same for the selected sprite or tilemap. Double-click a picture in the Assets panel to paint over it; saved changes show in the scene and the running game straight away.

## Animation clips

**Art → Animation Clips** opens the Clip Editor beside the scene. It follows the selection, listing the selected entity's clips (an **Animation**'s **Clips**) with each one's frames, speed and whether it loops, and plays the one you pick in a preview, cut from the sprite sheet the way the game cuts it, with a strip of its frames underneath. Pause and step through them (Space, Left and Right, or click a frame in the strip); a clip that plays once stops on its last frame. **Add** makes a clip (the first takes the animation's own frames, later ones the frame after the last clip's), **Rename** (F2) and **Remove** (Delete) change the one picked, and **Start With This** makes it the clip the game starts playing (marked *starts*); each is one undo step, and the clip the game starts with follows a rename. Until it has clips, or when the game starts without one, the list shows the animation's *own frames* (its **First Frame** and **Frame Count**), which is what plays then. Selecting a sprite with no animation offers **Add Animation**, which plays every frame of its sheet. The whole sheet is shown under the preview with the picked clip's frames marked: drag across frames to make the clip play those (a click picks one), or Shift-click to stretch it to reach a frame. **Speed** (frames a second) and **Loops** set how it plays; a clip that leaves them out uses the animation's (the editor says so), and setting them here gives it its own. For the *own frames*, all three set the animation's **First Frame**, **Frame Count**, **Frames Per Second** and **Loop**. Each change is one undo step, and the **Clips** field in the Inspector shows the text it writes.

Beside the preview, **Boxes** lists the hitboxes and hurtboxes of the frame showing (a **Hitboxes**'s, for fighting games), drawn over the picture: **Hitbox** (H) or **Hurtbox** (U) and drag to draw one, snapped to the picture's pixels (**Draw circles**, C, for circles); **Select** (S) to pick one, move it and drag its corners to resize it, and set its **Damage**, **Knockback**, **Stun** and **Group** (a hurtbox's **Multiplier**); Delete removes it. **Copy From Previous Frame** (Ctrl+D) gives the frame the boxes of the one before, and **Onion skin** (O) shows that frame faintly underneath with its boxes dashed. Each change is one undo step (Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Y work in the Clip Editor too), the first box adds a **Hitboxes**, and renaming or removing a clip takes its boxes along. See [Fighting games](/docs/fighting-games/).

A fighter's moves have an editor of their own: select it and choose **Entity → Edit Moves...** for the Move List, its moves with their inputs drawn as arrows (`↓↘→ P`), each move's motion picked from a list of the common ones (quarter circles, dragon punches, charges) and its button, clip, frame data (drawn as a bar, with what it leaves on hit and on block), height and cancels. See [The Move List](/docs/fighting-games/#the-move-list).

A rhythm chart has a panel of its own: choose **Entity → Edit Chart** and the chart (or select Note Lanes, which shows theirs) for the **Chart** panel (at the bottom, beside Tables), the chart on a timeline in its lanes over the song's bars and shape. **Record** and tap the lanes' keys along to the song to put notes in, snapped to a grid and allowing for your audio offset, each take one undo step; click, drag and right-click the timeline to add, move, lengthen and take out notes; **Play** to hear it back with a tick on each note. See [Recording a chart](/docs/rhythm/#recording-a-chart).

## Making sounds and music

**Audio → New Sound** opens the Sound Maker. Pick a starting point (**Coin**, **Jump**, **Shoot**, **Explosion**, **Power Up**, **Hit**, **Blip** or **Random**) and it makes a fresh retro sound of that kind and plays it; click again for another. **Mutate** nudges every setting for "like that, but different", the **Wave** buttons change its tone, and the sliders shape it (its envelope, pitch slides, vibrato, a "ba-ding" change of pitch, repeats, a phaser and filters), playing it after every change. **Save** puts it in `Sounds/` as a WAV. **Audio → New Music** (or the **Music** button) writes a looping tune instead: pick a **Mood** (**Bright**, **Chill**, **Tense**, **Spooky** or **Heroic**: each has its own scale, chords, beat and accompaniment), then **New Tune** for another in the same mood, and set its **Tempo**, **Bars**, **Lead** sound and which **Parts** play (melody, bass, chords, drums). It's saved in `Music/`, and its end runs straight into its start, so it can play under a level forever. Sounds made here remember how they were made: double-click one in the Assets panel to play it, or right-click it and choose **Edit in Sound Maker** to tweak it and save over it (heard straight away, even in the running game). The Platformer, Top-down, Isometric, Arcade, Strategy, Shooter, Sandbox, Contraption, Multiplayer, Puzzle, Match-3, Falling Blocks, Physics and Pinball templates come with their sounds and a theme tune made this way, already hooked up: jumps, coins, swings, hits, explosions, checkpoints, doors, switches, springs, menu buttons, orders, digging, building, crafting, the tools, crates sliding onto their marks, gems clearing, blocks turning, locking and clearing, a slingshot stretching and twanging, wood cracking, glass shattering, stone crumbling, grubs popping and stars pinging in, and flippers, bumpers, a plunger, a bell, the multiplier going up, an extra ball, a new best and the game over.

## Adding entities

The **Entity** menu sorts what it makes into categories, a submenu each: **Basics** (an empty entity, a sprite, text, a camera), **Characters** (platformer, top-down and grid players, a grid enemy, an attack for the selected entity), **Gameplay** (a turn order), **Level** (a tilemap, a room, a ladder, a switch), **Physics** (a crate, a spring), **UI** (a button, toggle, slider, choice, bar and panel), **Story** (a dialogue box), **Audio** (music, a looping sound, a sound that plays on a message, a beat clock) and **Lights and Effects** (a light, particles, weather), then **Genres**, with one for each genre module that makes something (**Adventures**, **Cards**, **Dungeons**, **Falling Blocks**, **Match-3**, **Puzzles**, **Tactics**), so a new module never makes the menu taller. Each item has an icon, and hovering it says in a line what it makes. Right-clicking the Hierarchy has the same submenus. What's made goes inside the selected entity (on empty space in the Hierarchy, nothing is selected, so it goes at the top) and is selected.

**Entity → Add Entity...** (Ctrl+Shift+A, or the + at the top of the Hierarchy) opens a searchable list of all of it: type a few letters and the best match is picked (`enemy` finds **Grid Enemy**), and words find things by their category or by what they do too (`audio` lists the sounds, `waterfall` finds a **Sound Source**). Up and Down move through it, Enter (or a click) makes the picked one, and the line under the list says what it is. What you made lately, from the menus or the list, is listed first under **Recent**, kept between sessions. Something that needs a selection (**Add Attack** gives the selected entity one) is faded until there is one.

Where something is listed comes from the code: each command that makes something (and each module's `EntityPreset`) has a `[Category("...")]`, the way a component's does in the catalog, and its one line is the first sentence of its `///` summary, which is also what agents read about its tool. The commands keep their ids, so the tools are the same (`Entity_CreateGridEnemy`), and each tool's description says where it's listed ("Menu: Entity > Characters > Grid Enemy", "Menu: Entity > Genres > Match-3 > Match Board").

## Adding components

The Inspector's **Add Component** opens a list of every component by category with a search box ready: type a few letters (`patr`) and the one you mean is picked, Up and Down move through what's left, and Enter (or a click) adds it. Along with it come whatever it needs that the entity doesn't have yet, as one undo step: a **Patrol** brings a **BoxCollider** and a **Body**, and the line under the list says so before you press Enter. Components that need something show what in the list ("Needs Body, Collider"). A component whose entity lacks something it needs (a **Platformer Movement** left without its **Body**) is flagged under its name, "Needs a Body, which Player doesn't have", with a button that adds one; a need any kind will fill, like a collider, adds a **Box Collider**. Removing a component that another one there needs asks first and says which ("Platformer Movement needs it on Player, and won't work without it"), and the remove button's tooltip says it too; a second collider can go without asking, since either one will do. Agents see the same: `entity_get` says what each component needs and lacks, and removing a component with `entity_update` notes what that left without something it needs.

## Component catalog

Next to **Add Component** (the quick list), the Inspector's **Catalog** button (or **Browse Catalog...** at the bottom of the list, which opens it on what you'd typed) opens every component by category, searchable, with what it does, what it **Requires** on the same entity (checked against the selected entity, with what's missing marked), what it **Works well with**, what it's **Needed by**, and each property's kind, starting value and description. Names in the text are links to that component. **Add** puts it on the selected entity (on each of them, with several selected) along with any requirement it's missing (a Patrol brings a Body and a BoxCollider), as one undo step. Scripts are listed too, described by their `///` comments.

In the **Inspector**, components and their choices read as words (**Platformer Movement**, **Ping Pong**), and every field explains itself: hover its name for what it does and in what units (a component's name, and each choice in a list, explain themselves the same way). Drag a number's name sideways to change it, or a vector's X, Y and Z: the further from 0 it is, the faster it goes, so an **Acceleration** of 70 reaches 90 in a short flick while 0.5 still moves by hundredths (Shift for fine steps, Ctrl for coarse; a click without dragging puts you in the box to type). While typing in a number, Up and Down or the mouse wheel nudge it by a tenth of its size (1 for 70, 0.1 for 7), and a run of nudges is one undo step. Fold a component's section shut with the arrow by its name and it stays shut on every entity, and the next time the editor starts, until you open it again. Big components put their fields under subheadings that fold the same way (a **Platformer Movement**'s **Jump**, **Walls**, **Dash**, **Control** and **Sounds and Effects**; a **State** heading for what the game keeps count of, like a turn order's round), and fields only used while something else is on fade until it is: **Dash Speed** with **Dash** off, **Wall Slide Speed** with **Wall Slide** off, a button's **Panel** unless its **Action** opens one. A faded field's name says what it waits for, and it can still be set ahead of time. While the game is playing, typing in the Inspector doesn't reach the game; click the game to give it the keyboard back.

## Colors, Transforms and finding fields

Click a color field's swatch for a picker: drag in the square for how strong and how bright, along the strips for the hue and how see-through it is, or type a hex. Under them are the Sprite Painter's palette (choosing another palette there changes the painter's too) and the colors you picked lately in any color field, kept for next time. The color changes as you drag, on every selected entity, and each drag or pick is one undo step. The Transform shows in 2D: **Position** and **Scale** in X and Y, and **Rotation** as one angle (degrees, counterclockwise). The **3D** switch on its header shows Z as well, for every entity and next time; in 2D, a Z that isn't at rest is named under the Transform ("Also set in 3D: Position Z 2"), since it still counts (a position's Z orders what's drawn). Type in the filter at the top of the Inspector to show only the fields whose names have it in them (`speed` shows **Speed**, **Dash Speed** and **Wall Slide Speed** and nothing else), or all of a component's or subheading's when its name has it, folded or not; components with no match are left out. The filter stays as the selection changes, so you can step through entities comparing one field; Escape empties it.

## A component's menu

Right-click a component's header in the Inspector for **Reset to Defaults** (every value back to what a new one starts with), **Copy Values** and **Paste Values** (onto the same kind of component, on this entity or another), **Move Up** and **Move Down** (its place in the entity's list; the Transform stays first) and **Remove Component**. Each works on every selected entity at once, as one undo step.

## Naming entities

Fields that name another entity (a Follow's **Target**, a Dialogue's **Box**, a Wire's **From**, a door's **Prompt**) have a button listing the scene's entities, indented as in the Hierarchy: pick one to name it (type a few letters to narrow the list, Up and Down to move, Enter to pick), or drag an entity from the Hierarchy onto the field. Fields that name several, separated by commas (a Switch's **Targets** and **Hides**), add each one picked or dropped, and picking a ticked one takes it out again; with several switches selected each keeps its own other names. An attack's **Hitbox** and a panel's **First** control list only what's inside the entity. Typing a name still works, for something that isn't in the scene yet.

Renaming an entity (in the Hierarchy or the Inspector) renames it in every field that names it, as one undo step: rename the player to "Hero" and the camera still follows it, the enemies still chase it. A field keeps its name when it means another entity by it, as the game finds it: one inside its own entity (each lever's own **Hint**), or any of several with that name. A name that matches nothing in the scene (a typo, or something deleted) is shown in yellow with a warning sign by it, saying which names it can't find; a prefab's name counts as a match, since the game can bring one in.

## Naming prefabs, scenes, actions, layers and messages

Fields that name something else the project has are picked from a list of those too, flagged in yellow when they name something it hasn't: a Spawner's or Shooter's **Prefab** and the effects things leave behind (a Pickup's **Effect**, a Weapon's **Projectile**, a worker's **Builds**) list the project's prefabs, a Door's or Button's **Scene** its scenes, an Attack's or Shooter's **Action** and a Panel's **Open On** the input actions (every game's own and those added in **Game → Controls**), the **Layers** fields (who can collect a pickup, use a door, work a switch) the collision layers colliders and tilemaps are on, here or in any scene or prefab, and message fields the messages the project sends and listens for, each marked "sent", "heard" or both (a Pickup's **Message**, a Counter's, a Health's **Death Message**, a dialogue's `[send]` tags, and the names scripts give `Game.Send` and `On`). A counter listening for a message nothing sends is flagged, and so is a message sent that nothing listens for (unless it's still the component's own, like a Faction's "Defeated"). A collider's own **Layer** is where layers come from, so it's listed but never flagged; a new layer or message is typed in (the list offers to use it as typed). Fields naming several, like **Layers**, tick each one picked and take it out again when picked twice. Prefabs and scenes can be dropped on their fields from the Assets panel too. Every list, entities, images and sounds included, has a search box ready as it opens: type to narrow it. Agents open a field's list with `ui_set` (`IsPickerOpen`, its `Picker.Search`) and pick with `ui_invoke` (`Pick`), and `scene_tree` and `entity_get` report the names that match nothing, as `component_describe` says what each field names.

## Editing several at once

With several entities selected, the Inspector edits the components they all have, changing all of them together, as one undo step. Components only some of them have are listed after, greyed, with how many have them ("on 2 of 3", and which in the tooltip) and **Add to All**, which gives the rest a copy of the one shown, its values and whatever it needs included. A value they don't share shows as `—` (a check box half-filled, a list blank) until you set it. A vector's axes are separate, so typing an X lines them all up across while each keeps its own Y. The checkbox by the name enables or disables them all; **Add Component** and the remove button add and remove a component on every one of them. Names stay one per entity.

In the **Assets** panel:
- Drag an image or a prefab into the Scene view to place it there, or right-click it for **Add to Scene** (a prefab goes where it was saved from, an image at 0, 0). Either way it's one undo step, and inside a prefab's tab it goes inside the prefab.
- Right-click a file for **Open in Another Program** (programs and scripts aren't run), or anything for **Show in Explorer**.
- Drag any asset onto an Inspector text field to fill in its path.
- Double-click a scene to open it; right-click it to make it the start scene (or pick it under **File → Set as Start Scene**, which lists every scene).
- Rename a prefab from its right-click menu, or drag it onto a folder (or into empty space, for Assets itself) to move it there; **New Folder** on the right-click menu makes somewhere to put it. Every copy stays linked: in every scene, inside other prefabs and in open tabs (the open scene takes it as an undo step, and its file is relinked too). A prefab's name is what `Game.Spawn` and fields like a Spawner's **Prefab** use, so renaming one means changing those.
