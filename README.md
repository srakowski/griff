# Griff

**A game engine for game jams.** Griff is a 2D-first engine and editor (built so 3D can follow), written in C#. It aims to be the shortest path from an idea to something people can play by Sunday night, with an editor that just works.

## Why Griff

Most engines make you stop the game to change it, lose your changes when play mode ends, and break undo in creative ways. Griff is built around a few promises:

- **The game keeps running while you change it.** Save a C# script and every instance of it is swapped in the *running* game within a moment. Public properties carry over, and the game doesn't restart. A compile error leaves the last good version running and points at the exact line.
- **Rewind.** While playing, Griff keeps the last 20 seconds. Pause, drag the timeline back to just before the bug, tweak something, and resume from there. It keeps up with big scenes too: a scene of twenty thousand things plays in the editor at 60 frames a second, recorded ten times a second.
- **The same game on every machine.** The game moves in steps of a sixtieth of a second whatever the screen's frame rate (or as many a second as **Game → Step Rate** says), and the screen is drawn between the last two steps, so it's smooth on a 144 Hz screen and plays the same on a 30 Hz one: the same input gives the same result anywhere. Everything is drawn between steps, not just where things are: particles, trails, rain, fades and flashes, match-3 gems sliding and falling and falling-block pieces dropping, bars filling, lights flickering, the camera's zoom and shake, a first-person view's look up and down and its weapon's kick, debug shapes, and scripts' own drawing too. Slow motion, hit stop, rewind and network games all go by the steps.
- **Undo covers everything.** Every change goes through one undo history. That includes the history surviving a script reload: undo still targets the rebuilt components.
- **Play mode can't hurt your work.** Play runs on a copy of the scene. Inspect and tweak the live game freely; Stop puts everything back. Found the perfect jump height while playing? **Keep Play Changes** (Ctrl+K) copies the selected entity's live values into your scene.
- **A crash costs nothing.** Unsaved scenes are written to a recovery copy every 20 seconds and offered back next time you open them.
- **Readable files.** Scenes are small, stable JSON (`"position": [1.5, -2, 0]`) that diff and merge cleanly in git. A deleted or renamed script never destroys data; its values are kept and come back with it. Renamed components and properties keep their values too (`[FormerName]`), a component can version its data with steps that upgrade older scenes as they load, and values a component doesn't know (from a newer version) are kept rather than dropped.
- **Batteries included.** Sprites and sprite-sheet animation with named clips, tilemaps you paint right in the Scene view with a brush, rectangles and paint-bucket fills, from as many tilesets as you ## Using the editor

| Action | Shortcut |
| --- | --- |
| New project (pick a template) | Ctrl+Shift+N |
| Open project | Ctrl+O |
| Save scene | Ctrl+S |
| Undo / redo | Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y |
| Create empty entity | Ctrl+Alt+N |
| Rename, duplicate, delete | F2, Ctrl+D, Delete |
| Play / pause / step / stop | F5 / F6 / F10 / Shift+F5 |
| Play with 2 players (a window for player 2) | Ctrl+F5 |
| Cut / copy / paste entities | Ctrl+X / Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V |
| Build game / small game | Ctrl+B / Ctrl+Shift+B |

In the **Scene view**:
- Scroll to zoom.
- Middle-drag, right-drag or Alt-drag to pan.
- Click to select; Ctrl- or Shift-click adds one or takes it away.
- Drag from empty space to select everything inside the box (hold Ctrl or Shift to add to the selection).
- Drag to move: everything selected moves together, as one undo step (hold Ctrl to snap the one picked last to the grid).
- Drag the round handle above the selected entity to rotate it. With several selected, they all turn around the one picked last, as one undo step. Angles land on whole degrees; hold Ctrl to snap to 15 degrees.
- Drag the square handles on the selected entity's corners to scale it, or on its edges to stretch it one way. It grows or shrinks around its position, along its own axes; with several selected, they all scale around the one picked last, as one undo step. Hold Shift to keep its proportions, and Ctrl to snap to tenths; otherwise the scale lands on hundredths.
- Press R (or **View → Resize Handles**) and the square handles resize the selected entity's sprite instead, changing its **Size** rather than its scale, so a platform grows longer without stretching the coins on it. Like the scale handles it grows around its position: the opposite edge moves out as much, and its colliders and children stay where they are. Press C (**Collider Handles**) for green handles on its box and circle colliders: a box's edges and corners change its **Size** and **Offset** (the opposite side stays put), and a circle's edge changes its **Radius** around its middle. Hold Shift to keep proportions, and Ctrl to snap sizes to half units (a circle's radius to quarters); otherwise they land on hundredths. Each drag is one undo step. Press S to go back to scaling; the corner of the Scene view says which handles are out.
- The selected entity's distances show as dashed circles around it: how far its light reaches, its sound is heard, it notices the player or shoots, a unit sees. Press D (**View → Distance Handles**) for a round knob on each circle, named beside it: drag one to change how far it reaches, as one undo step. Hold Ctrl to snap to half units; otherwise it lands on hundredths. A distance of 0 (often "anywhere") has no circle; set it in the Inspector.
- A selected mover's path and a selected patrol's or grid patrol's route have knobs to drag, whichever handles are out. A **Mover**'s points are numbered from its start (where it's placed: move the entity to move the start); drag one to move it, drag the small plus between two points (or past the last, or on the way back to the start of a loop) to add one there, and double-click one to take it off. A **Grid Patrol**'s points work the same way, but each lands in the middle of the cell it's dropped on (counted in cells from the one it was placed on), and its pluses sit in cells too: the one halfway between two points, and the next along its last stretch. A **Patrol**'s **Left** and **Right** turns slide along its route; drag one back past where the patrol was placed to clear it, so it walks on until a wall or an edge on that side (a hollow knob at the end of a dotted arrow; drag it out to set it). Hold Ctrl to snap to half units; otherwise points land on hundredths (a grid patrol's always land on cells). Each drag is one undo step.
- Arrows link the selection to the entities its fields name, and to it from the fields elsewhere that name it, each labelled with its fields: what a switch turns on and hides, what a camera follows, where a wire comes from, where a door leads. A name several entities share links to each of them; one that matches nothing has no arrow (the Inspector flags it).
- Press F to frame the selection.
- Arrow keys nudge everything selected by half a unit (Shift for a tenth).
- Drop an image to create a sprite, or a prefab to place a copy of it (linked to the prefab: see Prefabs).

**Arranging panels.** Drag a panel's tab (Hierarchy, Inspector, Assets, Output) to move it; a box shows where it will go before you let go. Drop it right by an edge of the window to dock it along that whole side, near an edge of another panel (or of the Scene and Game tabs) to dock it beside that, or on another panel's tabs or middle to tab it with them. Drop it over the middle of the Scene view, or outside the editor, and it floats in a window of its own, which keeps the editor's shortcuts; hold Ctrl to float it wherever it's dropped, and press Escape to call a drag off. Drag the gutters between panels to resize them. Closing a floating window puts its panels back with the ones they start beside.

**Saving layouts.** The editor opens where you left it, with the panels where you left them, floating windows and all. **View → Save Layout...** saves where they are under a name (an art layout, a level-building one, one for recording), **View → Layouts** switches to one, **View → Delete Layout** forgets one, and **View → Reset Layout** puts every panel back where it starts out. Layouts are yours, not the project's: they're kept for every project you open. A floating window that was on a screen that's since gone opens in the middle of the editor.
- Lock a backdrop or a big tilemap so clicks and box selections pass through it to what's behind: click the lock by its row in the Hierarchy (it shows when you hover), or **Entity → Lock**. Everything inside a locked entity is locked with it. Locked entities can still be selected in the Hierarchy, and nudged or edited in the Inspector; the game ignores locking.
- Hide a pause menu, a dialogue box or a tilemap layer that covers the level while you work on what's under it: click the eye by its row in the Hierarchy (it shows when you hover), press H in the Scene view or the Hierarchy, or **Entity → Hide**. The Scene view doesn't draw it or anything inside it, and clicks and box selections go to what's underneath. Hidden is saved with the scene, so it stays out of the way next time; the Hierarchy still selects it (and the Scene view outlines it while it's selected), and the game shows it as usual.

In the **Hierarchy**:
- Type in the filter at the top to show only the entities whose names have it in them (`slime` finds **Slime** and **Big Slime**), with the entities they're inside shown faintly and opened to show them. Escape (or the ×) empties it and puts the tree back as it was; it stays while you work, so new slimes show up in it.
- Click to select; Ctrl-click adds or takes one away, Shift-click selects every row from the last one clicked (Ctrl+Shift-click adds them).
- Delete, duplicate, cut, copy and paste work on everything selected, as one undo step. Duplicates go right after their originals, pastes after the selected entity, and the copies end up selected.
- Drag a row onto another to put it inside, or above or below it.
- The lock by a row locks it in the Scene view (all of the selection, if the row is one of them). Locked rows keep their lock showing; rows a parent's lock covers show it faintly.
- The eye by a row hides it in the Scene view the same way, and shows it again. Hidden rows keep a shut eye and a faded name; rows a parent's hiding covers show it faintly.
- The Inspector edits them all at once, and the Scene view outlines them all.

**Tilemaps.** **Entity → Create Tilemap**, then set its **Image** to a tileset and its **Columns** and **Rows** to the tileset's layout. While a tilemap is selected, the Scene view paints:
- Pick a tool under the palette, or with a key: the **Brush** (B) paints the cells you drag over, the **Rectangle** (R) fills the box you drag out (shown as you drag; Esc lets go of it), and **Fill** (G) fills the area you click, like a paint bucket: the cell and every cell joined to it side by side holding the same tile, or all empty, within the map's grid. Hovering with Fill shows the area it would fill.
- Hold Shift with any of them to erase instead. Each stroke, rectangle or fill is one undo step.
- Pick a tile from the palette at the top left, with `[` and `]`, or Ctrl-click a painted tile. The mouse wheel scrolls a palette with more tiles than fit.
- The tabs along the palette's top switch between the map's tilesets. Its **+** tab (or **Art → Add Tileset to Tilemap**) adds another from the project's images: a picture painted in Griff keeps its own tiles across and down, and any other is cut into tiles the size of the first tileset's.
- The buttons under the tools switch between the map's layers, and **+ Layer** adds one (see **Layers** below). The layers not being painted fade back.
- Paint (or drag a rectangle) past the left or top edge and the map grows that way; its layers (and anything else inside it) stay where they are.
- Press Esc (or select something else in the Hierarchy) to stop painting.

**Several tilesets.** A map's **Image** is its first tileset, with tiles numbered from 0. **More Tilesets** adds others, one per line with their tiles across and down (`Tiles/props.png 4x4`), and each one's tiles are numbered on from the next hundred after the tiles before it: with a first tileset of 16 tiles, the second's are 100 on and a third's 200 on. A tileset that grows (up to the next hundred) doesn't renumber the ones after it, and every field that lists tile numbers (**Background Tiles**, **Auto Tiles** and the rest) uses these numbers, so `100-103` are the second tileset's first four tiles.

**Layers.** A map can have several layers, painted one at a time: a background of walls behind, the level's solid ground, and decoration in front. **+ Layer** under the palette (or **Art → Add Layer to Tilemap**) adds one on top of the others and switches painting to it; the buttons beside it switch back. A layer is an entity inside the map's, holding a tilemap with no **Image** of its own, so it shows in the Hierarchy where it can be renamed, reordered, hidden while you paint the others (its eye), turned off or deleted like anything else; switching to a hidden layer shows it again. It paints with the map's tilesets, tile size and tile settings (**Background Tiles**, **Auto Tiles** and the rest), and the Inspector leaves those out on it, but its tiles, **Tint**, **Order** and collision are its own: a new layer draws over the others and doesn't collide until you turn on its **Solid**, and its **Layer** and **Ignore Layers** set which bodies it stops, so a wall layer can block everyone while a ghost walks through. Giving a layer an **Image** makes it a map of its own again. Agents add a layer with **Art.AddTilemapLayer** (or `entity_create` with a `Tilemap` and the map as its `parent`) and switch with the Scene view's `ShowLayer`.

The map's tiles are also plain text in the Inspector, one row per line and one character per cell (`.` is empty, `0`–`9`, `a`–`z`, `A`–`Z` are tiles 0–61, and tiles from 62 on are accented letters from `À`, then CJK characters from `一`, which scenes save as they are), so you can type a level out or paste one in. With **Solid** on, tiles are walls and floors, except:
- **Background Tiles** never collide: grass, windows, signs. List tile numbers and ranges, e.g. `10-15, 20`.
- **One Way Tiles** are platforms you jump up through and land on.
- **Ladder Tiles** never collide, and players climb them.
- **Slope Up Tiles** (`/`) and **Slope Down Tiles** (`\`) are 45 degree slopes, solid below the line from corner to corner. Players and enemies walk up and down them without leaving the ground, land on them and jump from them; the tall side is a wall, and the flat bottom is a ceiling. Raycasts see them too, so patrolling enemies walk them.

**Autotiles.** Set **Auto Tiles** to a run of 16 tiles in the tileset (`16-31`, or just `16`) and paint with any of them: each cell shows the piece that joins up with its neighbors from the same run, in the Scene view and the game, so grass edges and cave walls take care of themselves. The run's tiles go in order of which sides join, counting up as 1, right 2, down 4 and left 8: the first is a lone block, the second joins only above, and the last joins on every side.

The Scene view outlines what collides: one-way runs get a dashed outline with a solid top edge, and slopes a triangle.

**Isometric maps.** Set a tilemap's **Shape** to **Isometric** and its cells become diamonds twice as wide as they're tall, the grid's columns running down to the right from the map's position (the top corner of its first cell) and its rows down to the left. **Tile Size** is a diamond's width. Painting follows the diamonds: the brush, rectangle and fill paint the cell under the pointer, the hover outline and the grid's edge are diamonds, and painting past the top edges grows the map up its diagonals. The tiles are drawn back to front, a diagonal at a time, so taller ones overlap those behind them. **Tile Height** says how tall each tile's picture is, in tile widths: 0.5 is a flat diamond (a floor), and 1 a square picture, a block or wall standing up from its diamond as tall again; a picture stands on its cell's bottom corner. A tile with no tileset is drawn as its diamond. A map's layers share its shape and tile height.

Floors and walls go on separate layers. A map draws as one, behind what's on it (its place is its top corner, above everything standing on it), which is right for floors. Turn on **Sort By Y** on a wall layer (and on the camera) and each of its rows is drawn in its own place among the sprites, so a character walks behind a wall and in front of it (sprites sort by the bottom of their pictures, their feet). Sort By Y works on square maps too, a row at a time, for trees and pillars a top-down hero passes behind. Scripts find cells with `CellAt` and `CellCenter` as on any map, `PointAt(x, y)` for a point along the grid (and `GridAt(point)` the other way, in columns and rows), `OutlineOf` for a box of cells' corners and `PictureOf` for where a tile is drawn.

**Walking on isometric maps.** A solid isometric map's tiles are walls shaped like their diamonds (turn **Solid** off on the floor, or make floor tiles **Background Tiles**, and keep it on for a layer of walls). A body meets them with the diamond under its collider: as wide as the collider, half as tall, round its middle, so give a character a flat box at its feet. It slides along a wall the way the wall runs on the screen, crates shoved into one stop there, and raycasts (an enemy's sight) and `OverlapPoint`, `OverlapBox` and `OverlapCircle` see the diamonds. The Scene view outlines the solid diamonds, and each body's footprint inside its collider. Tick **Isometric** on a **Top-down Movement** (the Inspector reminds you on an isometric map) and up and down go half as fast on the screen as left and right, as the ground is seen at a slant, so up and right together run along the diamonds' edges; with **Four Way** too, its four ways are the diamonds' edges (up goes up to the right, right down to the right, down down to the left, left up to the left). A **Grid Movement** steps from diamond to diamond the same way, and two ways held together step to the diamond that way on the screen; with **Diagonal**, every way pressed goes that way on the screen (up steps to the diamond straight above). Grid movers face the way they step, flipping their sprites and playing `Up` clips going up the screen and `Down` clips coming down it; chasers, patrols, pushed crates and pathfinding work on the diamonds as on squares.

**Hex maps.** Set a tilemap's **Shape** to **Hex** and its cells become hexagons pointing up and down, for board games, strategy and tactics. Each is a tile wide and a tile tall (**Tile Size**), so a tileset of square tiles with a hexagon in each fits: its points at the middle of the top and bottom edges, its sides straight down the left and right edges from a quarter of the way down to three quarters. Rows are three quarters of a tile apart, so each row's points fit between the row above's, and every odd row (the second, the fourth...) is shifted half a tile to the right; the map's position is the top left of its first cell's picture. Painting follows the hexagons: the brush, rectangle and fill paint the hexagon under the pointer, the hover outline, a dragged rectangle and the grid's edge follow the hexagons' sides, and a fill spreads to the six hexagons round each cell. Painting above the top row grows the map up two rows at a time, so the odd rows stay the shifted ones. Tiles are drawn a row at a time, each row over the points of the one above, and **Sort By Y** sorts the rows among the sprites as on a square map; a tile with no tileset is drawn as its hexagon. `Neighbors(x, y)` lists the six cells round a hexagon (the four round a square), and `CellAt`, `CellCenter` and `OutlineOf` work on hexagons. Hex maps are drawn and painted, but don't collide yet. A map's layers share its shape.

**Menus and HUDs.** **Entity → Create Button** makes a clickable button: a background sprite, a label and a **Button** component. Set its **Action** and a menu needs no code at all: **LoadScene** (with **Scene**, e.g. `Game`), **RestartScene** or **Quit**, with a **Fade** (in seconds) to fade the screen out and back in around the switch. A **Shortcut** key (Enter for Play, Esc for Back) clicks it too. Add a **ScreenAnchor** to pin anything to an edge or corner of the screen, like a score in the top-left: it stays put whatever the window's shape, while the camera follows the player or shakes. The Scene view outlines buttons' clickable areas in blue. Menus work without a mouse: the arrow keys, WASD, D-pad or stick pick a button (it lights up), and Enter, Space or the A button click it; with a controller, the top button is picked straight away, and B clicks whichever button Esc would. Untick a button's **Navigable** if it sits on a HUD during play, so moving the player doesn't pick it.

**Options.** **Entity → Create Slider** and **Create Toggle** make an options menu with no code. Set a slider's **Setting** to **Volume**, **MusicVolume** or **SoundVolume**, or a toggle's to **Sound** (on or off) or **Fullscreen**, and it changes that setting and always shows it. The game remembers the player's choices (whether made by a slider, a toggle or a script) and puts them back next time it runs. For anything else, like a difficulty, set **Save As** to a name instead and read it from scripts with `Game.SaveData.GetFloat("difficulty")`. A slider is dragged with the mouse, turned with the wheel, or moved by its **Step** with left and right once picked; a toggle is a button whose **On Text** and **Off Text** become its label ("Sound: On"). Built games go fullscreen with F11 too.

**Pause menus and panels.** **Entity → Create Panel** makes a panel: a backdrop pinned to the middle of the screen. Select it and create buttons, sliders and choices to put them on it. While the game runs it's hidden until it opens (tick **Open** to have it open from the start), so you can lay it out in the Scene view. Set its **Open On** to `Pause` and it's a pause menu with no code: Esc, P or Start opens it and stops the game, and pressing it again, Esc or the gamepad's B closes it (if the panel has a button with the Esc **Shortcut**, Esc clicks that instead). A button's **Action** opens and closes panels too: **OpenPanel** (with **Panel** naming one, like `Options`) and **ClosePanel** (Resume, Back). While a panel is open, the mouse, keyboard and gamepad only reach the controls on it, so the HUD and the menu underneath don't react; its top control (or its **First**) is picked straight away, and closing it gives the keyboard back to what was picked before. Panels stack: an options panel inside the pause menu closes with it. **Pause Game** (on by default) stops game time and runs only the scripts on open panels, and Interact doesn't reach levers, doors or dialogue behind it; untick it for a game over screen that the game goes on behind. **Close On Back** off makes the player choose their way out. **Open Message** opens it when that message is sent, with no code: `Solved` from a puzzle's goals, a `GameOver` from a player's **Death Message**, or anything a pickup, switch or script sends.

**Controls.** **Game → Controls...** lists every input action the game has and the keys, mouse buttons and gamepad buttons that do each. Pick an action, click **Press a Key** and press one (or click the button with a mouse button) to add it; controller buttons come from **Add Button** with no controller needed, or **Press on Controller** with one. A key that another action also uses is pointed out, the × on a key takes it off, and **Reset to Default** puts one of Griff's nine actions (Jump, Fire, Dash, Attack, Interact, Pause, EndTurn, Undo, Restart) back; they can be rebound but keep their names, since components use them. **Add Action** makes one of the game's own (`Shield`), which scripts read with `Input.WasPressed("Shield")` and button prompts and Rebind buttons work with. Changes are saved in `project.griff` as you make them (only what differs from Griff's defaults), reach a running game straight away, and go into exported games. A player's own Rebind choice still wins over them, and a script's `Input.Bind` over both; the window says when a choice you made while testing is in the way (**Game → Clear Save Data** clears it).

**Step rate.** **Game → Step Rate** sets how many steps a second the game takes: 60 unless you change it, with 30, 120 and 240 in its submenu (the one in use is ticked) and any rate from 10 to 240 when you run it from an agent or give it a number. A faster rate gives fast bodies finer steps for more work a second; the screen is drawn smoothly between steps whatever it is. It's saved in `project.griff` straight away (undo puts it back, without leaving the scene unsaved), reaches a running game, and goes into exported games; a script can still change `Time.StepRate` as the game runs.

**Button prompts.** Put an action's name in braces in any text and it shows the key or button that does it, on whatever the player is using: `Press {Interact}` reads "Press E" on the keyboard and "Press Y" on a gamepad, and follows the player's own bindings. Braces around anything else are left alone.

**Controls screens.** A button whose **Action** is **Rebind** and whose **Input Action** is `Jump` waits for the next key or gamepad button when clicked and makes it jump from then on (a key replaces Jump's keys, a gamepad button its buttons; Esc cancels). It's remembered next time. Label it `Jump: {Jump}` and it always shows the binding (`...` while it waits). **ResetControls** puts every action back. **Entity → Create Choice** makes a button that picks one of its **Options** (one per row): left and right change it while it's picked, a click or the mouse wheel moves to the next, and its **Format** (`Difficulty: < {0} >`) labels it. With **Save As** (`difficulty`) it's remembered, for scripts to read with `Game.SaveData.GetString("difficulty")`.

**Buttons that can't be used yet.** Untick a button's **Interactable** and it's drawn faded, ignores the mouse and its shortcut, and is skipped by the arrow keys. **Needs Saved** keeps it that way until something is saved under that name, for a Continue button that waits for a saved game.

**Panels.** Set a sprite's **Slice** to how much of its image is border (say 0.25 for a quarter at each edge) and its **Slice Size** to how big the border is drawn: its corners then stay crisp however you stretch its **Size**, for menu panels, dialogue boxes and buttons from one small image.

**HUD bars.** **Entity → Create Bar** makes a bar that fills to its **Value** out of **Max**, which way its **Direction** says. Set its **Track** to an entity's name (`Player`, `Boss`) and it shows that entity's health with no code. When it goes down, the part just lost lingers in the **Trail** color for a moment before draining away.

**Pickups and scores.** Add a **Pickup** to a coin (with a trigger collider) and it's collected when the player touches it: it sends its **Message** (`Score`) with its **Amount**, plays its **Sound**, spawns its **Effect** (a sparkle prefab, in the coin's color) and disappears. **Layers** says who can collect it (`Player`; empty is anything with a body), and **Spin** turns it while it waits. A **Counter** on a text adds up its **Message** by itself, whatever sends it (a pickup, `Game.Send("Score", 10)`, an enemy's **Death Message**), and shows it with its **Format** (`Score: {0}`, `{1}` is the best; a counter that counts goals, moves or a match board's gems has `{2}` for what it's out of and `{3}` for how many are left). With **Save Best As** (`best`) it keeps the best score between runs, so a counter on the menu with the format `Best: {1}` shows it.

The Arcade template shows all of this: a main menu, options (with a difficulty and a rebindable Fire button), a game with a pause menu, and a game over panel with Retry and Menu, fading between them. Its bullets use collision layers to fly out of the ship, and its enemy spawner is a coroutine. On a gamepad it plays twin-stick: move with the left stick, aim with the right, fire with the right trigger. The Platformer and Top-down templates' coins, gems and scores are pickups and counters, with no scripts.

**Players with no code.** **Entity → Create Platformer Player** makes a player that runs and jumps straight away: its **Platformer Movement** runs at **Speed**, gets up to it and stops at its **Acceleration** and **Deceleration** (less of both in the air, by **Air Control**), and jumps **Jump Height** high with the Jump action. Jumps are forgiving: one pressed a moment before landing (**Jump Buffer**) or a moment after running off a ledge (**Coyote Time**) still happens, and letting go early makes a short hop. Falling is a little heavier than rising (**Fall Multiplier**), up to **Max Fall Speed**. Tick what the game needs: **Air Jumps** (1 is a double jump), **Wall Slide** and **Wall Jump**, and **Dash** (the Dash action, Shift or the gamepad's B: **Dash Speed** for **Dash Time**, once in the air until landing). Down and Jump on a one-way platform drops through it. It flips its sprite to face where it's going, plays sounds and a **Dust Effect** if you give it some, and if it has an **Animation** with clips named `Idle`, `Run`, `Jump`, `Fall`, `Climb`, `Dash` or `WallSlide`, it plays them itself. **Entity → Create Top-down Player** makes one with **Top-down Movement** instead: eight directions (or **Four Way**), an optional dash, and `Idle`/`Run` clips (with `IdleUp`, `RunDown` and so on, facing that way). It ignores gravity. **Entity → Create Grid Player** makes one with **Grid Movement**, for puzzle games, roguelikes and old-school RPGs: each press steps it one cell of the scene's tilemap (or the one its **Map** names), gliding there in **Step Time** seconds, and holding a way keeps it walking (after **Repeat Delay** seconds, or untick **Repeat** to step once a press); a press during a step is taken when it ends. Solid tiles, solid colliders and other grid movers block it, and it turns to face them instead, playing its **Bump Sound** once a press; triggers (coins, doors), one-way and background tiles don't, and layers its collider ignores don't either. It starts on the middle of the cell it's placed in, moves four ways (the way pressed last wins) or **Diagonal**ly too (never cutting a wall's corner), stays on the map unless **Stay On Map** is unticked, and plays `Idle` and `Walk` (or `Run`) clips, with `IdleUp`, `WalkDown` and so on. With no tilemap it steps a world unit at a time, between whole-number positions. Untick **Controllable** for a cutscene. When a player is selected, the Scene view draws how far it jumps each way.

**Ladders, crates, springs and crumbling ledges.** **Entity → Create Ladder** makes a ladder (a trigger with a **Ladder**); up and down climb it without gravity, and Jump leaves it. Put a one-way platform at the top to stand on: climbing passes through it both ways, so down from the top climbs back down. **Entity → Create Crate** makes a crate with a **Pushable**: players walk into it to push it at its **Speed** (0.6 of theirs), along the ground in a platformer and any way in a top-down game, and it pushes crates in front of it until a wall stops them. It's on the `Crate` layer, so a pressure plate with `Crate` in its **Layers** can wait for it. In a scene with grid movers it's a grid crate instead (see Sokoban crates and goals). **Entity → Create Spring** makes a spring: a **Bouncer** sends whatever lands on it flying at its **Strength** in its **Direction** (sideways makes a bumper; on a trigger it's a boost pad), giving a player its air jumps and dash back, and the Scene view draws where it sends things. Add a **Crumble** to a platform and once the player stands on it, it shakes for **Delay** seconds, falls away and comes back **Respawn** seconds later, once nobody's in the way.

**Cameras.** Add a **Follow** to the camera and set its **Target** (`Player`) and it follows with no code. **Smoothing** sets how quickly it catches up (0 sticks exactly), **Offset** shifts the view (say `0, 2` to see more above the hero), **Dead Zone** is a box the target can move about in without the camera moving (a tall one keeps a platformer's camera still through jumps), and **Look Ahead** leads the way the target is moving. Set **Stay Inside** to the name of the level's tilemap, a big box collider or a backdrop sprite, and the camera never shows past its edges. Follow works on anything, not just cameras: a pet that trails its owner. For top-down games, tick the camera's **Sort By Y** so whatever is lower on screen draws in front. A sprite sorts by the bottom of its picture, where it stands, so a tall tree and the hero beside it sort by their feet.

**Rooms.** For a game that moves screen by screen (a dungeon, a Metroidvania, Celeste's screens), lay the level out in rooms: **Entity → Create Room** makes one a screen big, and its **Size** makes it bigger. While the camera's **Follow** target is in a room, the camera keeps inside it (in place of **Stay Inside**); when the target crosses into the next, the camera slides over in **Transition** seconds, with the game held still meanwhile (untick **Freeze** to keep it going). A room can start its **Music** (crossfading from what was playing; the same music carries on), set the camera's **Ambient** light (a dark cave inside a sunny level; transparent leaves the camera's own) and send its **Message** when it's entered, the room you start in included. Out of every room the camera keeps to the last one. The Scene view outlines rooms in blue with their names.

**Lights and darkness.** Set the camera's **Ambient** to a dark color and the scene goes dark: deep blue for a night, near black for a cave. Then **Entity → Create Light** (or add a **Light** to the player for a lantern, to a torch, to a glowing mushroom): it lights everything within its **Radius** in its **Color**, brightest in the middle and fading out by its **Softness** (0 is a hard-edged circle), as bright as its **Intensity**, from its **Offset** (a lantern held up). **Flicker** makes it waver like a flame (0.2 suits a torch). Lights that overlap add up. The HUD and menus are never darkened: anything pinned with a **ScreenAnchor** or riding inside the camera. The Scene view isn't darkened either, so you can see what you're placing; it shows each light as a soft glow, and a selected light's reach. `Game.LightAt(position)` says how lit a place is (0 to 1), for stealth games where guards can't see you in the shadows.

**Shadows.** Tick a light's **Shadows** and walls, solid tiles (slopes included) and solid colliders block it, casting shadows away from it. They're lit on the side facing the light and dark behind. What carries the light (the player holding a lantern) and anything the light is inside (a torch set into a wall) cast none, and triggers, one-way platforms and background, ladder and one-way tiles let light through. `Game.LightAt` sees shadows too, so a guard can't see a player hiding behind a pillar. The Top-down template's lantern and torches cast shadows.

**Weather.** **Entity → Create Weather** fills the view with **Rain**, **Snow**, **Leaves** or **Embers** (its **Kind**), wherever the camera goes. **Amount** is how heavy it is (scripts can change it as it plays, for a storm that builds), **Wind** blows it sideways (slanting rain, drifting snow), **Speed** makes it fall faster or slower and **Tint** colors it. Its **Order** says what it falls in front of, and in a dark scene it's lit like everything else. Tick **Storm** for lightning every few seconds, with its **Thunder Sound** a moment after each flash.

**Screen effects.** Add **Screen Effects** to the camera to change the look of the whole screen, HUD included: a **Vignette** darkening the edges (on at 0.4 to start), **Scanlines** and **Curvature** for an old TV, **Pixel Size** for big chunky pixels, **Aberration** for color fringes, **Grain** for film grain, **Saturation**, **Contrast**, **Brightness** and **Tint** to grade the colors, and a **Palette** to draw it all in a handheld's four greens (**GameBoy**), **Sepia** or black and white (**OneBit**). Scripts can change any of them while the game runs: tween the saturation down as the player's health runs low, or bulge the screen on a big hit.

**Scene transitions.** A camera's **Transition** says how its scene covers up and uncovers a scene change (a door, a button's **Fade**, `LoadScene` with a `fade:`): **Fade** (the whole screen), **Wipe** (the color sweeps across from the left, then carries on off the right) or **Iris** (a circle closes on the player and opens out again).

**Parallax.** Add a **Parallax** to a background entity and set its **Factor**: 1 moves like the rest of the world, 0 stays stuck to the camera like a distant sky, and in between is in between (far hills 0.2, near trees 0.7). It shifts everything the entity and its children draw. **Repeat X** and **Repeat Y** repeat its sprite to fill the view, so a short strip of hills never runs out. It's applied as the game is drawn, so the layer is exactly where its transform puts it when the camera is at the origin, and the Scene view shows it there.

**Health and hazards.** Add a **Health** to anything that can be hurt and set its **Max**. After a hit it flashes white and blinks, and can't be hurt again for its **Invincible Time**; at zero it dies, sends its **Death Message** (say `PlayerDied`) and is destroyed (untick **Destroy On Death** to play a death animation first). Add a **DamageOnTouch** to whatever hurts: spikes and lava (it keeps hurting while you stand in it), enemies, bullets. Its **Layers** says who it hurts (`Player` on spikes, `Enemy` on the player's bullets; empty hurts anything with health), **Knockback** pushes the target away, and **Destroy On Hit** spends a bullet. A collider on a child entity counts for the nearest Health above it, so a sword or a hurtbox can be its own child, and it never hurts its owner. The Arcade template's ship has three hearts, and its enemies and bullets use both.

**Moving platforms.** Add a **Mover** to a platform (or a saw, a lift, a gate) and give it **Points** to visit, as offsets from where it's placed: `4 0` goes four units right and back, `0 6` is a lift, `4 0; 4 3; 0 3` goes round a square. It moves at **Speed**, waits **Wait** seconds at each point and eases in and out of them (untick **Smooth** for a steady saw). **Mode** is **PingPong** (there and back the same way), **Loop** (straight back to the start and round again) or **Once** (a door sliding open). Whatever stands on it rides along. Its **Time** is how far into its trip it is: give a row of platforms different times so they don't move in step. Set its **Beats** and it steps from point to point on the music's beat instead (see Keeping the beat). The Scene view draws each mover's path; select it to drag its points, add more and take them off. Make a lift a one-way platform so players can walk through it at the bottom.

**Enemies.** A **Patrol** walks its body back and forth at **Speed**, turning at walls and, with **Turn At Edges**, before walking off a ledge. Give it a **Left** and a **Right** to keep it to a stretch of a long floor: it turns that far either side of where it's placed (0 walks on until a wall or an edge). The Scene view draws its route, and you can drag its ends. Its **Walked** is how far along from where it was placed it is: the game keeps it up to date (knock it back and it walks back to its route), and setting it starts it part of the way along. A **Chase** goes after its **Target** (`Player`) once it's within **Range**, and gives up once it's **Give Up** further away than that. With **Needs Sight** it has to see the target first, past any walls; with gravity it runs left and right after it; without, it heads straight there, or finds its way around a tilemap's walls if **Around** names it (`Level`). **Stop Distance** keeps it back, for an enemy that shoots. Give an entity both and it patrols until it spots you. Both flip the sprite to face where they're going (draw it facing right). Add a **DamageOnTouch** and they hurt.

**Grid enemies.** **Entity → Create Grid Enemy** makes an enemy for a grid game: a **Grid Movement** a little slower than the player's (its **Step Time**), a **Grid Chase**, and an enemy **Attack** whose `Hitbox` child hurts the `Player` layer. A **Grid Chase** steps after its **Target** (`Player`) once it's within **Range**, tile by tile and the shortest way round walls, solid things and other grid movers (it waits behind one blocking a corridor), and gives up once the target is **Give Up** further away than that; with **Needs Sight** it has to see the target first. It stops **Stop Distance** steps away (1 is next to it, 2 keeps a cell between them) and turns to face it, so an attack swings its way. At 0 it walks into its target's cell, and if the target is solid it bumps into it each step instead: a roguelike's bump attack (its **Bumped** event and **Bump Sound**). A **Grid Patrol** walks a grid mover round its **Points**, in cells from where it's placed (`4 0`, or `4 0; 4 -3; 0 -3` round a square), finding its way round walls between them, waiting for whatever stands in its way and pausing **Wait** seconds at each. Its **Mode** goes there and back, round in a loop or once, like a Mover's; switches start and stop it (**Running**), **Leg** starts it part of the way round, and the Scene view draws its route; select it to drag its points from cell to cell, add more and take them off. Give an entity both and it patrols until it spots you, then finds its way back to its route once it gives up. Either one steps on its own, never with the player's keys; in a network game the host moves it, after the nearest player. For a roguelike's fighting, give grid movers a **Bump Attack**: stepping into something with **Health** on its **Layers** (`Enemy`) hurts it by **Damage** instead of bumping, with a lunge toward it and back over a step's time, its **Sound**, an **Effect** prefab on what it hits and an optional **Hit Stop**; holding the way keeps hitting until it falls, and then the way is clear. On the player it attacks the monsters it walks into; on a monster whose **Grid Chase** has **Stop Distance** 0 (with **Layers** `Player`), it attacks the player it walks into. Taking turns, each hit is an action, so a hit and the monster's hit back go turn by turn. Give the things it hits an **Invincible Time** of 0 so each hit lands. Scripts hear its `Hit` event; in a network game the host does the damage.

**Sokoban crates and goals.** In a scene with a grid player, **Entity → Create Crate** makes a grid crate: a **Pushable** with a **Grid Movement** of its own (and no gravity). A grid mover on one of its **Layers** (`Player`) that steps into it pushes it a cell the same way, the crate gliding along just ahead of it, as long as the cell beyond is free; a wall or another crate stops the push (the pusher bumps instead), unless the crate **Pushes Row**, when the crates behind it slide along too. Pushes go straight, never diagonally, and a **Speed** of 0 won't budge. The crate never takes the player's keys, plays its grid movement's **Step Sound** as it slides, and raises `Pushed` with who pushed it and which way. **Entity → Create Goal** makes a goal: a flat square under the crates with a **Goal**, covered while a crate rests in its cell (once it has finished gliding there). A goal **Accepts** crates of the **Kind**s named (`Red, Blue`; empty takes any), turns the crate on it its **Covered Tint**, plays its **Sound**, and sends its **Cover Message** and **Uncover Message**; every goal with the same **All Covered Message** (`Solved`) sends it once they're all covered: every crate home, the level solved. A crate that starts on a goal covers it from the start, without a sound or message. A **Counter** with **Count Goals** shows how many goals are covered, with `{2}` for how many there are (`Crates: {0} / {2}`). Taking turns, a push is the pusher's step and uses its one action. In a network game a player's push goes to the host, which moves the crate.

**Undo and restart.** A **Move History** makes each step a grid player takes (with any crates it pushes) a move to take back: its **Undo Action** (`Undo`: Z, U or the gamepad's left shoulder) puts every grid mover back where it was before the last move, standing still and facing the way it was, and held down (**Hold To Repeat**) keeps going. Its **Restart Action** (`Restart`: R or the left trigger) puts them all back where the level started, as one more move Undo takes back; tick **Restart Reloads** to reload the scene from its file instead (fading over **Fade** seconds), so pickups, counters and scripts start over too. It puts back players, crates, and chasers and patrols (on the same stretch of their routes), but not what was collected or destroyed, nor counters, switches, inventories or whose turn it is. **Limit** caps how many moves it remembers (0 for all). Crates put back onto or off goals don't play their sounds or send messages, and solving the puzzle again sends All Covered again. It plays its **Undo Sound** and **Restart Sound** and sends its **Undo Message** (with the moves taken as its data) and **Restart Message**. A **Counter** with **Count Moves** shows how many moves have been taken (`Moves: {0}`), counting back with each undo. The first goal **Entity → Create Goal** makes in a scene brings a Move History along. Scripts call `Undo()`, `Restart()` and `Record()` (to count a move of their own, like a teleport) and read `Moves` and `CanUndo`. In a network game there's no undo, and anyone's Restart starts the scene over for everyone.

**The Puzzle template.** The **Puzzle** template is Sokoban made of these pieces, with no scripts: a menu of four levels, each button opening once the level before it is solved, and levels where a worker pushes crates onto the marks on a warehouse floor. Each level is its own scene (`Level1` to `Level4`): a tilemap whose walls are solid and whose floor is a background tile, a grid player, grid crates and goals (each with All Covered Message `Solved`), and a Move History. Its HUD counts crates on marks and moves, and a **Solved** panel opens on `Solved` (its **Open Message**) saying how many moves it took, with Next, Again and Menu. A **Progress** counter listening for `Solved` saves the level as solved (its **Save Best As**), which is what the next level's button **Needs Saved**. To add a level, duplicate a level scene, repaint its tilemap, move the crates and goals, have the last level's Solved panel go on to it (its Menu button's **Scene**), and add a button for it on the menu.

**Match-3 boards.** **Entity → Create Match Board** makes a board: an 8 by 8 tilemap with a **Match Board**, centered in the view and dealt already so you see it. The gems are the tilemap's tiles, one kind for each tile number in **Gems** (`0-5`: six kinds); without a tileset image each kind is a square of its own color, and with one they're its tiles. Any other tile on the board is a fixed block that gems stop on. Drag a gem onto its neighbor (or click one, then the other) to swap them, or with keys and a gamepad move the cursor with the arrows, pick a gem up with the **Pick Action** (`Jump`: Space or A) and press an arrow to swap it that way. Three or more of a kind in a row or column (its **Match Length**) shrink away over **Clear Time**, the gems above fall into the gaps (speeding up as they fall, **Fall Time** a cell), new ones drop in from the top (or grow under a block) with **Refill**, and whatever lines up as they land clears too: a chain. With **Only Matching Swaps**, a swap that lines nothing up only nudges the two gems into each other's cells and back. A board left with no swap to make is shuffled into one that has (**Shuffle When Stuck**). Each game it's **Deal**t afresh with nothing lined up and a move to make, from its **Seed** (0 for a new board each time); untick Deal to play the gems as painted, a level laid out by hand. It plays its **Swap Sound**, **Match Sound**, **No Match Sound** and **Shuffle Sound**, and sends its **Match Message** (`Match`, with how many gems cleared, so a **Counter** listening for it adds them up), **Chain Message** (with how long the chain is from the second clear on), **Settled Message** (as the board comes to rest after a swap) and **Shuffle Message**. **Swaps** counts the swaps made. Scripts call `Swap(x1, y1, x2, y2)`, `FindMove()` (a hint), `Shuffle()` and `DealInto(map, random)`, read `GemAt(x, y)`, `IsBusy`, `Chain`, `Picked` and `Cursor`, and hear `Matched` (each match's kind, cells and place in the chain), `Swapped` and `Settled`. The tiles and Swaps are saved with the scene, so a rewind goes back to how the board was; Deal unticks itself once the board is dealt, so rewinding doesn't deal a new one. In a network game the host runs the board, any player can swap with their own mouse or keys, and everyone sees the gems slide and fall.

**Match-3 levels.** Each gem cleared scores the board's **Points** (`10`), times how far along the chain it clears (the second clear after a swap scores double, the third triple), adding to its **Score** and sending its **Score Message** (`Score`, with the points), so a plain **Counter** shows the score. **Moves** limits the swaps (0 is no limit), **Target Score** is a score to reach, and **Collect** names gems to clear as kind:count pairs (`0:20, 3:15`: 20 of tile 0 and 15 of tile 3), counted in **Cleared**. Once the goals are met and the board comes to rest the level is won: it plays its **Win Sound** and sends its **Win Message** (`Level Complete`, so a panel with that **Open Message** shows the win). Use up the moves with the goals still to meet and it plays its **Out Of Moves Sound** and sends its **Out Of Moves Message** (`Out of Moves`), a board with no goals too, the end of a game played for score. Either way no more swaps are taken. Counters show the rest with no code: **Count Moves** counts a board's swaps (`{2}` its Moves, `{3}` how many are left: `Moves: {3}`), **Count Score** its score (`{2}` the Target Score: `Score: {0} / {2}`) and **Count Kind** the gems of one kind (that tile) cleared (`{2}` how many Collect asks for, `{3}` how many still to go: `{0} / {2}`). Scripts read `MovesLeft`, `GoalsMet`, `IsOver`, `ClearedOf(kind)` and `ToCollect(kind)`, and hear `Finished` (true for a win). Score, Swaps and Cleared are saved with the scene, so a rewind takes them back, and in a network game players' counters follow the host's board.

**The Match-3 template.** The **Match-3** template is four levels of these boards, with no scripts: a menu whose level buttons each open once the level before is won, and levels (`Level1` to `Level4`: First Sparkle, Ruby Hunt, Stone Garden and Grand Hoard) that each set a board's **Moves**, **Target Score** and **Collect**, and which gems are in play (five kinds or six); the later ones have stone blocks in the board. Down the left a HUD shows the level, the score (**Count Score**), the moves left (**Count Moves**) and, for each kind to collect, its gem with a **Count Kind** counter. A **Complete** panel opens on the board's `Level Complete` (its **Open Message**) with the score, Next, Again and Menu, and an **Out of Moves** panel on `Out of Moves` with Again and Menu. A **Progress** counter listening for `Level Complete` saves the level as won (**Save Best As**), which is what the next level's button **Needs Saved**. The gems are a tileset of six cut gems (each its own shape as well as color) and a stone. To add a level, duplicate a level scene, change its board's goals and moves (and paint stones on its tilemap), have the last level's Complete panel go on to it, and add a button for it on the menu.

**Falling blocks.** **Entity → Create Falling Board** makes a board: a tilemap with a **Falling Board** 10 cells across (**Columns**) and 20 down (**Rows**), centered in the view. As the game runs, pieces of four blocks come in at the top, one after another, and fall a row every **Fall Time** (`0.8` seconds). Left and right (the arrows, A and D, the d-pad or the stick) slide the piece, held down they keep sliding (after **Repeat Delay**, a cell every **Repeat Every**); down falls faster (**Soft Drop Time** a row); up or the **Rotate Action** (`Fire`: J, the gamepad's X or the right shoulder) turns it clockwise, stepping it aside off a wall or out of the floor if it has to; the **Drop Action** (`Jump`: Space or A) drops it all the way at once. A piece that rests on something for **Lock Time** locks into the board's tilemap (sliding or turning it starts that wait again, a few times over), and an outline in the **Ghost** color shows where it would land. A row filled from side to side flashes for **Clear Time** and clears, the rows above dropping down; rows cleared at once score **Points** (`100`) times the **Level**, three times as much for two rows, five for three and eight for four, adding to **Score** and **Lines**. Every **Lines Per Level** rows the **Level** goes up, and each level falls in **Speedup** (`0.8`) of the time the one before did. Once a new piece has no room to come in, the game is over (**Game Over**). Each shape lands as its own tile (the I as **First Tile**, then J, L, O, S, T and Z), so a tileset gives each its look; without one each is a square of its own color. **Pieces** picks the shapes that come (`IJLOSTZ`: each comes once in every round of them, shuffled, from the **Seed**; `IIO` would deal twice as many I's as O's), and **Preview** shows that many of the next ones in a box to the board's right. Tiles painted on the board to begin with are blocks already there. It plays its **Move Sound**, **Rotate Sound**, **Lock Sound**, **Clear Sound**, **Level Up Sound** and **Game Over Sound**, and sends its **Lines Message** (`Lines`, with how many rows cleared), **Score Message** (`Score`, with the points), **Level Message** (`Level Up`, with how many levels it went up, so a **Counter** that starts at 1 shows the level) and **Game Over Message** (`Game Over`, so a panel with that **Open Message** shows it). A **Counter** with **Count Score** shows its score too. Scripts call `Move(direction)`, `Rotate()` and `Drop()`, read `Piece`, `PieceAt`, `Rotation`, `PieceCells()`, `LandingCells()`, `NextPieces`, `BlockAt(x, y)` and `IsClearing`, and hear `Locked`, `Cleared`, `LeveledUp` and `Ended`. The tiles, Score, Lines, Level and Game Over are saved with the scene, so a rewind goes back to them (with a new piece coming in). In a network game the host runs the board, any player can move the piece, and everyone sees it fall.

**The Falling Blocks template.** The **Falling Blocks** template is a menu showing the best score with Play and Quit, and a `Game` scene with a Falling Board of seven bevelled block tiles, the next three pieces beside it, and a HUD of counters: the score (**Count Score**), the rows (listening for `Lines`), the level (listening for `Level Up`, starting at 1) and the best score (**Count Score** with **Save Best As** `best`, which the menu's counter shows). A **Game Over** panel opens on `Game Over` with the score, the best, Again and Menu, and the pause menu is there too, with no scripts.

**The Roguelike template.** The **Roguelike** template is a menu showing the deepest floor reached, with Play and Quit, and a `Crypt` scene made anew every game by a **Dungeon Generator** (Seed 0) on a tilemap of stone walls and flagstone floors: the hero starts in the first room and the stairs wait in the furthest, with three or four slimes, a bat or two, a potion or two and some gold scattered through the other rooms. It's turn-based: the hero steps a cell a turn (Tab waits one), and every monster steps or hits once after. The hero walks into monsters to fight them (a **Bump Attack**, two hits for a slime, one for a bat); slimes (`Prefabs/Slime.prefab`) notice the hero within five cells if they can see them, bats (`Prefabs/Bat.prefab`) anywhere within eight, flying diagonally, and either hits the hero by walking into them. Gold counts up, potions go in the hero's inventory and 1 drinks one (four hit points back). The stairs make the next floor, keeping the hero's health and potions. The HUD shows the hero's health bar, the floor (a counter with **Count Floor**, keeping the deepest), gold and potions; it's dark but for the hero's lantern. A **You Died** panel opens on the hero's Death Message `Game Over` with the floor reached and the deepest, Again and Menu, and the pause menu is there too, with no scripts.

**Swords and close-up attacks.** Select the player and choose **Entity → Add Attack**: it gets an **Attack** and a `Hitbox` child to its right, a trigger on the `Attack` layer with a **DamageOnTouch** that hurts enemies. Pressing the Attack action (X, L, the right mouse button or the gamepad's X) winds up for **Windup** seconds, brings the hitbox out for **Active Time** seconds (it's hidden the rest of the time), then rests for **Cooldown**; a press meanwhile swings as soon as it can. Each swing hurts each thing once, however long it lingers on it, and a hit freezes the game for **Hit Stop** seconds so it lands hard. The hitbox swings the way its owner faces (laid out facing right, it's mirrored to face left, and turned up and down in a top-down game). **Lunge** steps forward as it strikes, a **Sound** plays, and an **Animation** with an `Attack` clip (`AttackUp` and `AttackDown` too, top-down) plays it. An enemy's attack has no **Action**: it swings at its **Target** (`Player`) once it's within **Range**, turning to face it first, so a knight is a **Patrol**, a **Chase** and an **Attack** with a hitbox that hurts the `Player` layer.

**Turrets and guns.** A **Shooter** fires its **Prefab** (`Bullet`, which needs a Body) every **Interval** seconds at **Speed**: at its **Target** while it's within **Range**, or in its **Direction**. **Count** and **Spread** fire a fan of shots (or with one shot, wobble its aim), **Offset** is where shots appear, and a **Sound** plays with each volley. Set its **Action** to `Fire` and it's the player's gun instead: it fires while Fire is held, towards the right stick or the mouse. Shots are turned to face the way they fly. Set its **Beats** to fire on the music's beat instead of every **Interval** (see Keeping the beat).

**Turns.** **Entity → Create Turn Order** makes a game turn-based: a **Turn Order** whose text in the top-right corner says whose turn it is, and an **End Turn** button in the bottom-right corner. Its **Sides** take turns in order (`Player, Enemy`, or `Blue, Red` for two people at one keyboard), and whatever acts gets a **Turn Taker** naming its **Side**. Off its side's turn, a turn taker waits: the player's keys don't reach it (nor its scripts' `Input`), and its Patrol, Chase, Grid Chase, Grid Patrol, Attack and Shooter hold still. It still falls, gets pushed and gets hurt, and everything with no turn taker (moving platforms, particles, a ball still rolling) runs in real time as ever, so a turn can be a stretch of real-time play, as in Worms. A person's turn ends when they press End Turn (Tab or the gamepad's Back: its **End Action**) or click a button whose **Action** is End Turn (faded while they can't), or when its **Turn Time** runs out (0 waits for them). The **Computer Sides** (`Enemy`) can't be ended that way: their turn lasts **Computer Turn Time** seconds, long enough for patrols to walk and turrets to fire. Give a turn taker **Actions** to limit what it does in a turn: each attack swing and each shot uses one (scripts use them with `Act()`), it waits once it has used them, and once everyone on a side has, the turn passes by itself. **Between** turns nobody acts for a moment, so a last shot lands. A side with nobody left is skipped, and once a side that had turn takers loses the last of them it's beaten: its **Defeat Message** goes out once (`{0}Defeated`: `RedDefeated`, with the side as its data), for a Victory or Defeat panel to open on. Each turn sends its **Message** (`{0}Turn`: `PlayerTurn`, then `EnemyTurn`, with the side as its data, so a panel or a sound player can greet it) and plays its **Sound**; its **Format** fills its text (`{0}'s turn`, with `{1}` the **Round** and `{2}` the seconds left). **Current**, **Round**, **Time Left** and each turn taker's **Actions Used** are saved with the scene, so rewinding goes back to whose turn it was. In a network game the host runs the turns, and with the Multiplayer's **Teams** each player's character is on its player's team (sides `Blue, Red` for teams `Blue, Red`); only the players on that side can end its turn.

**Roguelike turns.** On a grid, a turn taker with **Actions** takes turns a step at a time: each step of its **Grid Movement** uses an action (bumping a wall doesn't), and a **Grid Chase** or **Grid Patrol** steps once an action, passing when it has nowhere to go (out of range, waiting at a point, blocked) and nothing to swing at, so its side's turn ends straight away rather than after Computer Turn Time. With one action each and **Between** at 0, everything moves when the player moves: the player steps, every enemy steps (or swings) once, and it's the player's turn again, the steps gliding at the same time. A swing lands before its side's turn passes, so a monster's blow can't be dodged by stepping away; the next turn also waits for a mover still gliding from its last step, so play goes at the pace of the slowest mover. Holding a way walks a step a turn, a way pressed while the computer takes its turn is taken as the turn comes back, and Tab (End Turn) waits a turn. A grid patrol waits whole turns at its points (its **Wait**, rounded up). **Create Turn Order** in a scene with grid movers sets all this up: each grid mover gets a turn taker with one action (players on the first side a person plays, chasers and patrols on the first the computer plays), Between is 0, the text counts turns (`Turn {1}`) and the button says **Wait**; grid players and enemies made after it take turns too. Online, a player's step on their own machine is their action on the host.

**Tactics.** **Entity → Create Tactics Unit** makes a unit for a tactics game: a box in its side's color with a **Grid Movement**, a **Health** of 3, a **Tactics Unit** and a **Turn Taker** with one action, on whichever side has the fewest units so far (so making four gives two even teams, Blue down the left and Red down the right). In a scene with no turn order it also makes one (`Blue, Red`, with the computer playing Red, and an End Turn button), and a **Tactics Controls** to pick units with. On your side's turn, click one of your units: the cells it can walk to light up blue (up to its **Move**, `4`, around walls and other units, which block friend and foe alike), and the cells it could hit from them red (things from **Min Range** to **Range** steps away, `1` for a sword; an archer is Range `3`, Min Range `2`; ranges reach over walls). Hover a cell to see the way it would go, and click it to walk there. Then click a foe in reach to attack it (its **Damage**, with an **Attack Sound**, a **Hit Effect** and a **Hit Stop**), or the unit itself to wait; with nothing in reach it's done as soon as it gets there. Clicking a foe straight away walks up to the nearest cell it can hit it from and attacks. A right click (or Cancel, Shift or the gamepad's B) takes the walk back, or lets the unit go. Attacking or waiting uses the unit's action, it's dimmed (**Done Tint**) until its side's next turn, and once every unit on the side has had its go the turn passes. Hovering any unit shows faintly how far it reaches, health bars sit under the units, and the arrow keys or the stick move a cursor that Space (or the gamepad's A) clicks with (**Confirm Action** and **Cancel Action**), all colored to taste. A unit's foes are the units on other sides, or with **Layers** set, whatever has health on them (a gate to break down). Scripts read a unit's **Reachable**, **Targets**, **Can Act** and **Is Done**, give it orders with `MoveTo((x, y))`, `Attack(entity)`, `Wait()` and `UndoMove()`, hear **Moved**, **Attacked** and **Done**, and pick a unit with the controls' **Selected**. Whether a unit has moved is saved, so rewinds keep its go; online the host moves the units and each player picks on their own screen, their clicks sent to the host.

**The computer's side in tactics.** On a side the turn order's **Computer Sides** names (Red, from Create Tactics Unit; clear it for two people at one keyboard), the computer takes each unit's go in turn, the unit nearest a foe first: its reach lights up for the controls' **Computer Pause** (`0.5` seconds) so you can see what's coming, then it attacks the best foe it can reach with a walk and a hit (one it can finish off first, then the weakest, then the nearest), walking up to it, or with nothing in reach walks as far as it can toward the nearest foe, around walls. Each unit's **Stance** says how it plays: **Charge** (always comes at you), **Guard** (stays put until you come within a walk and a hit of it) or **Hold** (never walks, only attacking what's already in range: a tower, an archer on a wall). Set the turn order's **Computer Turn Time** to `0` (as Create Tactics Unit does) so its turn lasts as long as its units need. The host plays the computer's side in a network game.

**The Tactics template.** The **Tactics** template is a menu (Play and Quit) and a `Battle` on a field of grass with trees and rocks in the way, painted on a tilemap: your two knights (Move 4, 6 hit points, 2 damage up close) and two archers (Move 3, shooting two or three cells off) start on the left, and the computer's three orcs (charging), a goblin archer guarding the far side and another holding the rocks start on the right. It's played as the Tactics section above says, Blue first; the turn text counts the rounds (`{0}'s turn · Round {1}`), health bars show who's hurt, and hits throw sparks with swings and shots to hear. Beat every orc and goblin and **Victory** opens (on `RedDefeated`, the turn order's Defeat Message); lose all four of yours and it's **Defeat** (`BlueDefeated`). Both offer Again and Menu, and the pause menu is there too, with no scripts.

**The BunnyMark template.** The **BunnyMark** template is a benchmark rather than a game: bunnies pour in from the top left and bounce about the view, more and more while the game keeps up 60 frames a second, until it has found how many this computer holds there. Each bunny is an ordinary entity, a **Sprite** and a `Bunny` script (falling, bouncing off the floor and the sides), copied from the `Bunny` prefab, so the count says how much an ordinary game can put on screen. The `BunnyMark` script starts with **Start Count** (`100`) and times each count for **Window** (`1`) second: while the frame rate (the frames the screen shows a second, from `Time.DrawnFrames` and `Time.RealTotal`; the game itself takes 60 steps a second however many frames are drawn) keeps up **Target Fps** (a frame missed in the second still counts), it adds **Growth** (`0.5`, half as many again); when it falls behind, it takes the last lot away and tries half the step, until a step would add less than **Precision** (`0.02`). Then it holds the count for **Hold Seconds** (`10`, after two to settle), timing every frame the screen shows: the frame rate, its 1% low (the frame rate of the slowest frames, from the time 99 frames in 100 came within: a frame that misses the screen's beat takes twice as long, so a low under the frame rate means some did) and the garbage collections meanwhile. It shows *Holds 1,015 bunnies at 60 fps (best 1,147); held for 10 s: 60 fps, 1% low 60 fps, 0 garbage collections* and saves them (`held`, `best`, `fps`, `low` and `collections`). **Hold Count** holds that many bunnies straight away rather than finding the count, to check a count (*Held 50,000 bunnies for 10 s: ...*). The frame rate and the count show in the top left all the while, and Space runs it again. It's silent, so it times nothing but itself. While it plays, `entity_get` shows its `count`, `held`, `fps`, `low`, `collections` and `isDone`.

**Dungeons.** **Entity → Create Dungeon** makes a dungeon: a tilemap with a **Dungeon Generator**, 48 cells across (**Width**) and 32 down (**Height**), centered in the view and showing a dungeon made from a new seed, its rooms and corridors cut from solid wall so they show without a tileset. As the game starts it fills its tilemap with a new floor: up to **Rooms** (`9`) rooms, each **Min Room Size** to **Max Room Size** cells across and down, a wall apart, joined by corridors **Corridor Width** wide (each room to its nearest, so they're short), plus **Loops** more between rooms already joined, so there's more than one way round. Floors are **Floor Tile** (-1 leaves them empty; list a floor tile in the tilemap's **Background Tiles** so nothing collides with it) and the cells round them **Wall Tile**, a row deep, or everything else with **Fill Walls**. The entities named in **Players** (`Player`) are put in the middle of the first room, the **Exit** (a staircase, say) in the room the longest walk from it, and **Spawns** are scattered through the other rooms, one per line: a prefab and how many (`Enemy 4`, `Potion 1-2` picks a number between, `Gold` is one), never on the exit or each other. A player stepping onto the exit's cell (once a grid mover's step ends) goes down to the next floor (untick **Descend On Exit** to go only when told): a new dungeon, the last floor's spawns cleared away, the players in the new first room. So does sending its **Next Floor Message** (say `Next Floor`, from a button or a script). **Floor** counts the floors, and each one after the first plays **Floor Sound** and sends **Floor Message** (`Floor`, with 1). A **Counter** with **Count Floor** shows the floor (`Floor {0}`), even in a panel that was closed as the floors went by, and with **Save Best As** keeps the deepest. The same **Seed** always makes the same dungeon, each floor its own; 0 (to begin with) makes a new one every game. **Entity → Generate Dungeon** makes the selected dungeon in the editor to see it, putting the players and exit in place as one undo step (with Seed 0, a new one each time; the game still makes its own). Untick **Generate On Play** to play the map as painted, with the floors after it made. Spawns go inside a `Spawns` entity in the dungeon's. Scripts call `NextFloor()` and `Generate()` (this floor again), read `RoomList`, `RoomAt(x, y)`, `ExitCell` and `UsedSeed`, and hear `Generated`. The tiles and Floor are saved with the scene, so a rewind goes back to the floor it was on. In a network game the host makes the floors and everyone sees them.

**Checkpoints.** Tick a player's **Health**'s **Respawn** and losing its hit points doesn't destroy it: it disappears for **Respawn Delay** seconds, then comes back with full health (and a moment of invincibility) at the last **Checkpoint** it touched, or where it started. A checkpoint is a trigger with a **Checkpoint**: touching it makes it the **Active** one, turns its sprite its **Active Tint**, plays its **Sound** and sends its **Message**. Bottomless pits and lava are a **DamageOnTouch** with **Kill** ticked, which kills straight through invincibility. Cameras following the player jump straight to where it comes back.

**Doors.** A **Door** on a trigger takes whoever touches it (on its **Layers**, `Player`) to its **Scene** (the level's exit) or, with no scene, to the entity named in its **Target** (a teleporter, a door into a house), fading out and in over **Fade** seconds. Tick **Needs Interact** and it waits for the Interact action (E, Enter or the gamepad's Y). Having arrived through one, you have to step off before a door takes you anywhere again, so a pair of portals never sends you back and forth. **Prompt** names an entity (a "Press E" text, its child) that shows only while someone can use it.

**Dialogue with characters.** **Entity → Create Dialogue Box** makes a panel at the bottom of the screen whose text types itself out, with a portrait and a name tag above it for whoever is talking (see Speakers and portraits). Give a sign or a character a trigger collider and a **Dialogue**: its **Lines** (one per row) are said in the box when the player presses Interact beside it, or straight away with **Start** set to **Touch**. Interact finishes the line being typed, then shows the next; after the last the box hides itself and it sends its **Message** (`TalkedToKing`). **Pause Game** stops everything while it's said, **Once** says it only the first time, and **Prompt** works like a door's. The box is hidden while the game runs until someone talks.

**Dialogue choices.** A row of **Lines** starting with `>` is a choice, offered under the line before it; several in a row are picked between. The player moves between them with the arrow keys, W and S, the D-pad or the stick (the picked one has an arrow by it), or points at one, and picks with Interact, a click or its number key. A choice ending in `-> Name` goes to the row `# Name`, which starts a part of the talk that's only reached that way: the lines before a part end where it starts. A choice with nowhere to go goes on after the choices, a row `-> Name` goes there by itself, and `-> End` ends the talk. So a shopkeeper is:

```
Want a sword?
> Yes -> Buy
> No -> Bye
# Buy
That'll be ten gold.
-> End
# Bye
Safe travels!
```

Parts can go back to earlier ones, to ask again (the Top-down template's guide does). Going to a part that isn't there stops the game as soon as the talk starts, naming it. Start a row with `\` to say it as it is, `>` or `#` and all.

**Dialogue flags.** Flags remember what was said and picked. A row can start with tags in square brackets: `[set MetKing]` sets a flag and `[clear HasKey]` clears one when the line is said (or the choice picked), `[if MetKing]` says the line only when the flag is set and `[if not MetKing]` only when it isn't, and `[send OpenGate]` sends a message, for a gate that opens on one answer and not another. A choice's tags go after its `>` (`> [if HasKey] Open the vault -> Vault`), and it's only offered while they hold; a go-to with an `[if]` is only followed while it holds, and a row of tags alone just does them. Choices go under the last line said of those just above them, so two greetings can share them (and when none of those lines is said, neither are the choices). So a king who remembers you is:

```
[if MetKing] Back again, are we?
[if not MetKing] [set MetKing] Welcome, stranger.
> [if HasKey] I found the key! -> Key
> Farewell -> End
# Key
[clear HasKey] [send OpenVault] Splendid! The vault is yours.
```

Flags are kept in the game's save data under their own names, so they're still set the next time the game runs (**Game → Clear Save Data** forgets them while you test). Capitals don't matter. Scripts read and change them with `Game.HasFlag("MetKing")` and `Game.SetFlag("HasKey")` (or `SetFlag("HasKey", false)`), and a toggle's **Save As** is a flag too. Anything else in brackets (`[sighs] Fine.`) is said as it is.

**Speakers and portraits.** Give someone a **Speaker** and their name (its **Name**, or the entity's) and **Portrait Image** show over what their **Dialogue** says, in the box's **Name Tag** and **Portrait** (named by the box's **Dialogue Box** component; each hides when there's nothing to show, like a sign's lines). A line starting with a speaker's name, `You: I'll help!`, is said by them instead, so the player answers back (give the player a Speaker called `You`), and a Speaker on an empty entity, even a hidden one, is a narrator or a voice on the radio. A line whose start matches no speaker (`Note: ...`) is said as it is.

**Switches.** **Entity → Create Switch** makes a lever. Name what it opens in its **Targets** (commas between names): while it's on, a **Mover** moves (a gate set to **Once** slides open, and closes again when it's switched off), a **Spawner** or **Shooter** runs, particles emit, an animation plays and a **Door** unlocks; anything else simply appears. **Hides** names what disappears while it's on: a barrier, a wall of spikes. It all starts switched off, so a bridge a lever brings out isn't there until it's pulled. Its **Mode** is **Lever** (Interact flips it, with a **Prompt** like a door's), **Plate** (on while something on its **Layers** stands on it: `Player, Crate` for crates too) or **Button** (on for good once touched; shoot it by setting **Layers** to `Bullet`). **On Time** turns it off again after that many seconds, for a timed button. It turns its sprite its **On Tint** (and its **On Frame**, for a lever drawn both ways), plays its **Sound** and sends its **On Message** and **Off Message**. Set its **Beats** and it flips itself on the music's beat as well (see Keeping the beat). If several switches name the same thing, it's on only while all of them are, so a gate can need two plates held down. Names are looked for inside the switch first, so a prefab can switch its own parts; the Scene view draws a selected switch's links.

**Keys and inventory.** Give a **Pickup** an **Item** (`Key`) and whoever collects it carries it in an **Inventory** (one is added to them if they have none). A **Door** with a **Key** stays shut to anyone without one, and the first trip through uses it up; a switch with a **Key** works the same, so a locked block is a solid box with a **Button** switch that needs a key and **Hides** itself. A **Counter** with an **Item** shows how many the player holds (`Keys: {0}`). Inventories are saved with the scene, so rewinding gives back the keys picked up since, and you can start a playtest already holding a key by typing `Key` into the player's **Items**. Items can be used, too: the inventory's **Item Uses** say what each does, one per line (`Potion: heal 3`, `Bomb: spawn Explosion`, `Scroll: send Teleport`; several separated by commas): `heal` gives back hit points, `spawn` puts a prefab where the holder stands, and `send` sends a message with the holder as its data. With **Number Keys** (on to begin with) the number keys use them, 1 the first item it holds that has a use, 2 the second, and so on, each use taking one out and playing its **Use Sound**; a potion that only heals is kept while its holder's health is full. Taking turns, using one is an action and only happens on the holder's turn. Scripts call `Use("Potion")`, read `Usable` and `EffectsOf(item)`, and hear `Used`.

**Drag and drop.** Give something a **Draggable** and the player picks it up with the mouse: press on it (its collider, or its sprite if it has none), carry it, and let go. It's drawn over everything while it's held, and keeps the spot it was grabbed by. Give a slot, a hole or a place on the table a **Drop Target**: something dropped on it (by its middle) lands in its middle, sliding there over its **Slide Time**, and the target lights up in its **Hover Tint** while something it would take is held over it. A target **Accepts** the **Kind**s named (`Red Gem, Blue Gem`; empty takes anything) up to its **Capacity** at a time (0 for any number: a bin, a discard pile); anything else dropped on it slides back where it came from. Untick **Snap** for an area things stay where they're let go in, and tick **Swap** on inventory slots so dropping on a full one swaps the two. Off a target, a draggable stays where it's let go, lands in the middle of a square of its **Grid** size (1 lines it up with a tilemap's cells), or with **Only On Targets** goes back. **Stays On Target** fixes a jigsaw piece once it's in place. Draggables play their **Pick Up Sound** and **Drop Sound** and send their **Pick Up Message** and **Drop Message**; targets send a **Drop Message** when something lands and a **Take Message** when it's taken off, and every target with the same **All Full Message** sends it once they're all full: every piece in place, the puzzle solved. What's on a target is whatever rests with its middle inside it, so things placed on a target in the Scene view start on it. In a network game the host carries things for each player by their own mouse, and two can't hold the same thing.

**Drag and drop with buttons.** For a game played with a controller or keys, give a cursor (or a character) a **Grabber**. Press its **Action** (`Interact`: E, Enter or the gamepad's Y) and it picks up the draggable under its hand, or else the nearest one whose middle is within its **Reach**; press again and it puts it down where it's held, onto a drop target as the mouse would. What a press would pick up lights up in its **Highlight**. A put-down the mouse would send back (on a full target, one for other kinds, or off targets for something that only goes on them) does nothing instead, so it keeps holding the thing and plays its **Refuse Sound**; put down on a full target that swaps, it picks up what was there, so a controller can shuffle an inventory around. Tick **Hold To Carry** to carry only while the button is held. Its **Hold Offset** is where it holds things: its X is in front of it (the side it faces, turning as it does) and its Y up, so `0, 1` carries a crate over a character's head and `0.8, 0` in front of it; a cursor keeps `0, 0`. Move a cursor with a **Top-down Movement** (or a **Grid Movement**, to step from slot to slot), which ignores gravity, so a cursor works in a game that has some. Grabbers send a **Pick Up Message** and **Drop Message** with what they moved, and scripts read `Holding` and `InReach`, hear `PickedUp` and `Dropped`, and call `Press()`. In a network game each player's grabber answers to their own controller.

**Animated tiles.** Set a tilemap's **Animated Tiles** to runs of tiles side by side in its tileset and how many frames a second, `16-19 @6`, and every cell painted with one of them plays through the run while the game runs: water, lava, torches. Paint neighbors with different frames of the run and they won't move in step.

**Trails.** Add a **Trail** next to a sprite and it leaves copies of itself that fade away over **Fade** seconds while it moves faster than **Min Speed**: dashes, speed lines, ghosts, bullets with streaks. **Color** paints the copies one color, and scripts can turn **Emitting** off and on (only during a dash, say).

Every template has a pause menu. Both the Platformer and Top-down players carry a sword (X). The Platformer template has a lift up to a ledge where a slime patrols (two swings defeat it) and a lever brings out a bridge with more coins, a spring up to two ledges that crumble under you, a checkpoint flag, a sign to read, and a player with three hearts who comes back at the flag. The Top-down template is a dim dungeon lit by flickering torches and the player's lantern; in it a bat chases you once it sees you, portals take you across the room, there's someone to talk to, a key across the room opens a vault, and a crate pushed onto a plate brings out a bonus gem. The Isometric template is a sunny ruined courtyard seen at a slant, on an isometric map: a floor that doesn't collide and a layer of stone walls, a hedge and low walls along the front that's solid and sorts each diagonal among the sprites, so the hero walks behind the old shed's walls and in front of them. Its people stand on the diamonds (their colliders are flat boxes at their feet), gems float over their cells (one in the shed, one behind the hedge, three in the corners), a slime chases you on sight and finds its way round the walls, a crate pushed up into the hedge's corner presses a plate that brings out a bonus gem, and the gardener talks to you from the diamonds round them. All three templates' players move with no script and dash with Shift, leaving streaks; the Arcade template's bullets leave streaks too, and its scripts are the place to see how a game is coded.

**Real-time strategy.** The **Strategy** template is a whole real-time strategy game made of no-code components: blue against the computer's red, each with a town hall, workers and soldiers, on a map with gold mines, trees, rocks, fog of war and a pause menu. The pieces:
- **Faction**, one per side: its **Team** name (everything with that Team belongs to it; different names are enemies), its **Color**, and the **Resources** it has to spend, kept as text (`Gold 400, Wood 150`) so they show and rewind like anything else. A team with no buildings left is beaten; it sends its **Defeat Message** with the team's name.
- **Unit**, anything that can be selected and sent about: its **Team**, **Speed**, **Radius** and **Sight**, and, for training it, its **Cost** (`50 Gold, 25 Wood`), **Train Time**, **Supply** and **Hotkey**. Units find their way around buildings, resources, rocks and solid tiles on their own, keep apart rather than stack, spread out when sent together, and fight back when something armed shoots at them.
- **Fighter**, for units and towers: **Damage**, **Range** (edge to edge, so a melee soldier's is small), **Cooldown**, and a **Projectile** prefab (an arrow) with its **Projectile Speed**, or none to hit straight away. Fighters attack enemies that come within their unit's sight by themselves, the closest first, armed ones (and towers) a little before the rest.
- **Worker**, which makes a unit gather and build: its **Carry** and **Gather Time**, and the building prefabs it **Builds** (`Farm, Barracks, Tower`). Sent to a resource it fetches load after load to the nearest depot, moves on to more of the same when that runs out, and goes back to work after putting up a building.
- **Resource**, something to gather: its **Kind** (`Gold`, `Wood`, or anything else; a Faction's Resources use the same names) and the **Amount** left. It's gone when it's empty.
- **Building**, which stands still and takes up its **Size**: **Team**, **Sight**, **Cost**, **Build Time**, the **Supply** it provides, **Depot** (workers bring resources here) and **Hotkey**. Placed by a worker, it starts at **Progress** 0, see-through, and only trains, takes resources, provides supply or shoots once it's finished. A **Fighter** makes it a tower.
- **Producer**, on a building, **Trains** units (`Soldier, Archer`, prefabs with a Unit), up to **Queue Limit** at once, paying for each as it's queued (Esc or X cancels the last one, giving the cost back). Trained units walk out to the rally point, or start gathering if it was set on a resource. Sprites on entities called `Team Color` inside a unit or building take on its team's color, so one prefab serves every side.
- **Commander**, on the camera, is the player's controls and HUD: a bar of resources and supply along the top, and along the bottom a **minimap**, what's selected (with health, what it's doing and what it's training) and a **command card** of buttons with hotkeys. Its **Map Center** and **Map Size** bound the camera, the minimap and pathfinding. **Victory Panel** and **Defeat Panel** name panels it opens when the game is won or lost.
- **FogOfWar**, next to the Commander, hides what the player's team can't see: black where it has never been, dimmed where it has been but can't see now, with enemy units there hidden.
- **Opponent**, next to a Faction, lets the computer play it: it keeps its **Workers** gathering, trains more, puts up what it **Builds** (and farms when short of supply), trains its **Army**, one every **Train Every** seconds (0 as fast as it can pay; higher is an easier game), and once it's past **First Attack** and has **Attack With** soldiers idle, sends them to the nearest enemy building, waiting for **Wave Growth** more each time.

**First-person shooters.** The **Shooter** template is a first-person shooter made of no-code components: fight out of a dark base past grunts that charge and robots that throw fireballs, with a pistol, a shotgun to find in the armory, medkits, doors that slide open as you come near, a blue key for the locked way out, lamps lighting the corridors, a minimap and a pause menu. The level is an ordinary tilemap painted top-down in the Scene view; in play mode you stand in it. The pieces:
- **First Person View**, on the camera, draws the game through the **Viewer**'s eyes (the entity called `Player` by default). Solid tilemaps are walls, textured with their tiles (and shaded by side), as tall as **Wall Height**; sprites stand on the floor and always face the viewer (a pickup with **Spin** turns in place), hidden behind any wall that's nearer; particles hang in the air. **Field Of View**, **Eye Height** and **Columns** (strips across the screen: fewer is chunkier) shape the view, **Floor** and **Ceiling** color it, and everything fades into the **Fog** color by **Fog Distance**. In a dark scene (the camera's **Ambient**), lights light the walls, floor and sprites near them. It also draws the weapon in hand, a **Crosshair**, a **Minimap** of the walls, enemies and pickups, and a **Hurt Flash** when the viewer loses health. The HUD (anything with a **Screen Anchor**) goes on top as usual. The viewer looks the way its Rotation Z points: 0 is to the right on the map.
- **First Person Movement** walks (W and S, or up and down) and strafes (A and D) at **Speed**, or **Run Speed** with Shift, and turns with the left and right arrows at **Turn Speed**. With **Mouse Look**, clicking the game hands it the mouse (hidden, and kept inside the game) to turn with, **Mouse Sensitivity** degrees a pixel, and to look up and down a little with **Look Up Down**; Esc, pausing or switching away gives it back. The controller's left stick walks and strafes and the right stick turns. **Step Sound** plays every **Step Length** units walked.
- **Weapon**, on the player, shoots where it looks when **Action** (Fire) is pressed, or held for an **Automatic** one, up to **Fire Rate** times a second: **Pellets** bullets a shot, scattered across **Spread** degrees and reaching **Range**, each taking **Damage** from whatever with Health they hit first (every pellet that hits one thing lands as one blow) and pushing it back by **Knockback**, with an **Impact Effect** where they land. A **Projectile** prefab is launched at **Projectile Speed** instead. Each shot uses up one of the player's **Ammo** item (`Bullets`), so a **Pickup** with that Item refills it and a **Counter** with that Item shows it; with none left it plays its **Empty Sound**. A weapon with **Needs Item** (`Shotgun`) can't be used until a pickup gives that item, and is taken out the moment one does. Several weapons on the player are picked with the number keys (their **Slot**), the mouse wheel or the controller's Y. In hand it's its **Image** (or a simple gun in its **Color**) at **Size**, swapping to **Fire Image** for a moment as it fires, with a **Muzzle Flash**, a **Kick** and a screen **Shake**.
- **Billboard**, on a sprite, lifts it off the floor in first person (**Lift**: a lamp on the ceiling, a fireball at chest height), **Bob**s it up and down, or **Scale**s it.
- **Block**, on an entity with a Box Collider, draws it as a box of wall instead of a sprite, covered with its sprite's image (or color): doors, gates, pillars. Give it a **Mover** and a **Switch** to slide it open, or let a switch hide it.

Three smaller additions came with the shooters: a **Pickup**'s **Heal** gives back health (a medkit is left alone while health is full), **Damage On Touch**'s **Destroy On Wall** breaks a shot on whatever solid it hits, and a **Shooter**'s **Needs Sight** holds fire while a wall is in the way.

The controls: click a unit or building to select it (double-click picks every unit of its kind on screen), drag a box round units, and hold Shift to add to the selection. Right-click to send what's selected: on the ground to walk there, on an enemy to attack it, on a resource to gather it, on a building going up to help build, and with a building selected, to set its rally point. Hotkeys follow the command card: **A** then a click attack-moves (fighting whatever's met on the way), **M** then a click moves, **S** stops, **H** holds position; with workers, a building's hotkey and a click places it (Shift places more); with a building, a unit's hotkey trains it. Ctrl and a number keeps the selection as a group, the number picks it again, and twice brings the camera to it. The arrow keys, the screen's edges and the minimap move the camera, the mouse wheel zooms, and Esc cancels placing or targeting before it opens the pause menu.

**Block worlds.** The **Sandbox** template is a world to dig and build in, Terraria style: rolling hills over caves full of coal, iron and gold, trees, sand, and a floor of bedrock, with a hotbar, crafting, and torches to light the dark underground. All of it is no-code components on a tilemap and the player:
- **World Generator**, on a tilemap, fills it with a world made from its **Seed** each time the game starts (**Generate On Play**; untick it to keep and paint on the map as it is). The same seed always makes the same world, and 0 makes a new one every time. **Width** and **Height** size it; **Surface**, **Hills** and **Hill Width** shape the ground; **Grass Tile**, **Dirt Tile** (**Dirt Depth** deep) and **Stone Tile** fill it; **Caves** hollows out tunnels and caverns, more of them deeper down; **Ores** lists ore tiles and how much of the stone they make up (`3 6%, 4 3%`), each later one deeper; **Sand** and **Trees** (**Trunk Tile**, **Leaves Tile**) dress the surface, and **Bottom Tile** floors it. **Walls** names a second, non-solid tilemap to fill with the underground's back walls, which caves show instead of the sky. **Spawn** stands the player on the ground in the middle.
- **Blocks**, on the same tilemap, makes it something to dig and build with. **Items** says which item puts down which tile (`Dirt 1`), and mining a tile gives its item, unless **Drops** says otherwise (`0 Dirt` for grass, `6 nothing` for leaves). **Mine Time** and **Hardness** (`2 0.8, 3-4 1.2`) are how long blocks take to break; **Unbreakable** tiles never do. **Falling Tiles** drop while there's nothing under them, like sand. **Glowing Tiles** light the dark like a Light (**Glow Radius**, **Glow Color**), and **Underground** is the light the camera fades to below the ground, **Dark Depth** down. **Save As** keeps the world in the game's save data, so what's dug and built is still there next time. **Break Effect**, **Dig Sound**, **Break Sound** and **Place Sound** give it feel.
- **Block Builder**, on the player, digs with the left mouse button held on a block within **Reach** (faster with **Mine Speed**), the block cracking as it goes, and puts down its **Item** with the right button, next to another block and never on top of anyone. In first person it works on the block straight ahead, Minecraft style. What's dug goes into the player's **Inventory**.
- **Hotbar** shows the inventory in **Slots** along the bottom of the screen, each block drawn with its own tile, and picks one with the number keys, the mouse wheel or a click: that's what the builder puts down. Items keep their slot until they run out. **Colors** colors items that aren't blocks.
- **Crafting** lists **Recipes** like `Torch 4 = Wood + Coal`, opened and closed with its **Key** (C); click one to make it, lit up when there's enough. `@ Workbench` needs a workbench within **Station Reach**: a placed block of that item, or an entity with that name.

Tilemaps now only draw the tiles in view, so worlds can be big.

**Contraptions and wiring.** The **Contraption** template is a room to build machines in, Garry's Mod and Wiremod style: a lamp on a rope with a button, a lift of thrusters welded under a plank, a door held shut by logic until a crate is dropped on its plate, and a clock blinking a lamp and counting on a display. The pieces:
- **Sandbox Tools**, on the player, are tools to build with while the game runs, picked along the top of the screen, with the number keys or the mouse wheel. **Grab** carries things to the mouse (**Grab Strength**) and throws them when let go; right-click to freeze one in the air, or free a frozen one. **Spawn** puts down the prop picked from **Props** (prefab names; each gets a name of its own). **Weld** and **Rope** join the two things clicked; **Wire** joins an output of one to an input of another, asking which when there's a choice; **Remove** takes something away with its welds, ropes and wires. **Undo Key** (Z) undoes what was made, newest first. What's made is part of the scene, so **Keep Play Changes** brings a contraption back to the editor.
- **Body** has three new settings: **Frozen** holds it still where it is (others still bump into it), **Mass** says how heavy it is, and **Friction** stops it sliding along the ground.
- **Weld** sticks its body **To** another entity, by name: they move as one, sharing their speed by weight (so a balloon welded to a crate lifts it), and don't bump into each other. Welded to something without a body, it holds still. **Rope** ties it to another entity no further than **Length** apart (0 is as far as they start), from its **Offset** to the other's **Other Offset**, drawn in its **Color**.
- **Thruster** pushes its body (and what it's welded to) along its **Direction**, turned with the entity, with **Force** times its **Power** input; a particle emitter on it fires while it pushes.
- **Wire**, on the entity it drives, carries the **Output** of the entity named in **From** into this one's **Input**, drawn from one to the other (brighter while it's on, unless it isn't **Visible**). Every entity has the inputs **Power** (switching it on and off as a Switch does: movers, spawners, doors) and **Show**; outputs include a button's or switch's **On**, a gate's **Out**, a sensor's **Out** and **Count**, a body's **Speed** and **Height**, health's **Health** and a counter's **Count**. Several wires into one input add up.
- **Wire Gate** works out **Out** from its **A** and **B** inputs (an input with no wire keeps its value) as its **Kind** says: And, Or, Not, Xor, Nand, Nor, Add, Subtract, Multiply, Divide, Greater, Less, Equal, a Constant **Value**, a Toggle (flips each time A turns on), a Latch (A sets, B resets), a Delay of **Value** seconds, a Clock ticking every **Value** seconds, or a Counter. Chains of gates answer in the same frame, and each is labeled with what it does.
- **Wire Button** is pressed with Interact (E) with the mouse over it, or while touching it: a **Toggle**, or on only while held. **Wire Sensor** notices bodies inside its trigger (on its **Layers**). **Wire Lamp** lights from **Off Color** to **On Color** (and turns up a Light on it) with its **On** input, and **Wire Display** shows its **Value** input on its Text, or over itself.

**Multiplayer.** Any scene becomes a network game with a **Multiplayer** component, and the **Multiplayer** template is one to start from: a menu to type your name and a host's address into, and an arena for up to eight players with slimes, hearts, crates to hide behind and a scoreboard. One machine hosts and runs the game; everyone else's shows the host's world, sends the host their keys, and moves their own character itself, so it never waits on the connection. Cameras, the HUD, menus and music stay on each machine and follow that player's own character. The pieces:
- **Multiplayer**, on any entity in the scene: **Max Players**, the **Port** to host on, the **Player** each one plays (an entity in the scene, which the host plays and everyone else gets a copy of, or a prefab; empty for a strategy game, where players command teams), **Teams** for team games ("Blue, Red": players are shared out between them, appear at their team's spawns and can't hurt each other unless **Friendly Fire** is on; in a strategy game they're the factions players take, and a computer **Opponent** stands aside for a person playing its team), **Color Players** (each player's character in their own color, or their team's, and their shots too), **Score Kills** (a point for defeating another player or an enemy), **Send Rate**, **Smoothing** (how far behind the host everyone else's characters are shown, so they glide), **View Distance** (for a big world, like an MMO: only what's within it is sent to each player), **Remember Players** (a player who leaves gets their character back as it was when they join again, even in a later game) and **Exit Scene** (where to go when the host leaves).
- **Player Spawn** marks where players appear (for a **Team**, or anyone). **Name Tag** shows the name of the player something belongs to in its Text: put it on a child above the player. **Scoreboard** lists who's playing, with their score and ping, and how the game's going ("Hosting on 192.168.1.5:7777", "Joining...", why it ended), optionally only while a key like Tab is held. **Text Field** is a box to type into: a player's name, or the address to join.
- Buttons have **Host Game** (then go to their **Scene**), **Join Game** (the address typed into a Text Field, or with none, the first game found on the local network) and **Leave Game**. A pause menu doesn't stop a network game: the game goes on, and that player's keys just don't reach it until it's closed.
- What's sent is every saved property of every component, a script's as much as Griff's, whenever it changes. A component says otherwise for itself: `[NetLocal]` on its class keeps its entity on each machine (as cameras, HUDs, menus and music are), `[PlayerMovement]` marks no-code movement the player's own machine runs, `[Sent]` sends a value kept only while playing (`[Sent] public float Charge { get; private set; }`; `[Sent(ByMover = true)]` lets the player moving the entity decide it), `[NotSent]` keeps a saved one to each machine, and `INetState` adds values that need turning into something sendable (a match-3 board sends its gems as lines) or skips a property just now.
- **Networked** changes how one entity takes part: **Sync** shares it with everyone (the default for everything but cameras, HUDs, menus and music) or keeps it **Local** to each machine; **Movement** says whether a player's own machine moves their character (the default for characters with a movement component: instant to control) or the host does from their keys (nobody can cheat); **Always Sent** ignores the view distance; **Owner** is the player it belongs to, set by the game.
- What works for every player with no code: platformer, top-down, grid and first-person movement (each player turns and walks on their own screen), weapons, turrets and attacks driven by their own keys and aim, health, damage and respawning (the host's spikes and checkpoints count for every player), pickups, doors, levers, buttons and dialogue (read on each player's own screen), enemies that chase or shoot at the nearest player, a strategy game's commander (each player selects and orders their own team; the orders go to the host), and a block world's digging, building, hotbar, crafting and sandbox tools (everyone in the same world, with their own tools and undo). Messages sent on the host reach everyone, so their HUD counters and sound players work too, and the host's sounds play for everyone.
- **Game > Play with 2 Players** (Ctrl+F5, or 3 or 4) plays as several players at once: the Game tab hosts and each other player gets a window of their own, joined over a pretend network inside the editor. Click a window to play as that player. **Game > Pretend a Slow Connection** adds lag and a few lost packets to see how the game feels on a bad connection.
- Exported games host and join from their own menus. On the command line, `--host [port]` hosts straight away, `--join <address>` joins, `--name <name>` sets the player's name, and `--server [port]` runs a dedicated server: the start scene, hosted for everyone and played by nobody, with no window.

**Painting sprites and tilesets.** **Art → New Sprite** opens the Sprite Painter, for pixel art. Paint with the **Pencil** (B), **Eraser** (E), **Fill** (G), **Line** (L), **Rectangle** (R) and **Ellipse** (O), or pick a color off the picture with the picker (I, or Alt-click); right-dragging erases. **Mirror** (M) paints both halves at once, and Ctrl+Z undoes a stroke at a time. Colors come from a palette (**Jam**, **Earth**, **Handheld** or **Grays**) or any `#RRGGBB`. Pick a starting point (**Creature**, **Ship**, **Item**, **Ground** or **Bricks**) for a fresh one made from scratch, and **Another** for a different one. A sprite is frames played in order: **Add Frame** copies the one you're on, **Onion skin** shows the frame before underneath, and the preview plays them at the **Speed** you set. **Art → New Tileset** (or the **Tileset** button) paints a grid of tiles instead, numbered from 0 as a tilemap's rows name them, with a preview that repeats the tile to check it joins up. **Save** puts it in `Sprites/` or `Tiles/` as a PNG that remembers its frames or tiles, so it opens again exactly as it was made. Every image field in the Inspector (a sprite's or tilemap's **Image**, particles, first-person weapons) shows a preview, a list of the project's pictures, and a **Paint** button that opens the painter on its picture (or a new one) and puts each save on it in one undo step: a sprite takes its **Columns** and **Rows** and an **Animation** to play several frames, a tilemap its tile grid, and a colored placeholder box loses its tint. **Art → Paint Selected** does the same for the selected sprite or tilemap. Double-click a picture in the Assets panel to paint over it; saved changes show in the scene and the running game straight away.

**Animation clips.** **Art → Animation Clips** opens the Clip Editor beside the scene. It follows the selection, listing the selected entity's clips (an **Animation**'s **Clips**) with each one's frames, speed and whether it loops, and plays the one you pick in a preview, cut from the sprite sheet the way the game cuts it, with a strip of its frames underneath. Pause and step through them (Space, Left and Right, or click a frame in the strip); a clip that plays once stops on its last frame. **Add** makes a clip (the first takes the animation's own frames, later ones the frame after the last clip's), **Rename** (F2) and **Remove** (Delete) change the one picked, and **Start With This** makes it the clip the game starts playing (marked *starts*); each is one undo step, and the clip the game starts with follows a rename. Until it has clips, or when the game starts without one, the list shows the animation's *own frames* (its **First Frame** and **Frame Count**), which is what plays then. Selecting a sprite with no animation offers **Add Animation**, which plays every frame of its sheet. The whole sheet is shown under the preview with the picked clip's frames marked: drag across frames to make the clip play those (a click picks one), or Shift-click to stretch it to reach a frame. **Speed** (frames a second) and **Loops** set how it plays; a clip that leaves them out uses the animation's (the editor says so), and setting them here gives it its own. For the *own frames*, all three set the animation's **First Frame**, **Frame Count**, **Frames Per Second** and **Loop**. Each change is one undo step, and the **Clips** field in the Inspector shows the text it writes.

**Making sounds and music.** **Audio → New Sound** opens the Sound Maker. Pick a starting point (**Coin**, **Jump**, **Shoot**, **Explosion**, **Power Up**, **Hit**, **Blip** or **Random**) and it makes a fresh retro sound of that kind and plays it; click again for another. **Mutate** nudges every setting for "like that, but different", the **Wave** buttons change its tone, and the sliders shape it (its envelope, pitch slides, vibrato, a "ba-ding" change of pitch, repeats, a phaser and filters), playing it after every change. **Save** puts it in `Sounds/` as a WAV. **Audio → New Music** (or the **Music** button) writes a looping tune instead: pick a **Mood** (**Bright**, **Chill**, **Tense**, **Spooky** or **Heroic**: each has its own scale, chords, beat and accompaniment), then **New Tune** for another in the same mood, and set its **Tempo**, **Bars**, **Lead** sound and which **Parts** play (melody, bass, chords, drums). It's saved in `Music/`, and its end runs straight into its start, so it can play under a level forever. Sounds made here remember how they were made: double-click one in the Assets panel to play it, or right-click it and choose **Edit in Sound Maker** to tweak it and save over it (heard straight away, even in the running game). The Platformer, Top-down, Isometric, Arcade, Strategy, Shooter, Sandbox, Contraption, Multiplayer, Puzzle, Match-3 and Falling Blocks templates come with their sounds and a theme tune made this way, already hooked up: jumps, coins, swings, hits, explosions, checkpoints, doors, switches, springs, menu buttons, orders, digging, building, crafting, the tools, crates sliding onto their marks, gems clearing, and blocks turning, locking and clearing.

**Choosing sounds.** Every sound field in the Inspector (**Sound**, **Jump Sound**, **Music**, a script's `HitSound`) has three buttons: **Play** to hear it, a list of every sound in the project to pick from, and the Sound Maker, which opens on the field's sound if it was made there (or on a new one) and sets whatever it saves. Dragging a sound from the Assets panel onto a field sets it too. A field naming a file that isn't there turns red. Griff plays WAV, MP3 and OGG files.

**Music and sound players.** Add a **Background Music** to a scene and its **Music** starts with the scene, crossfading over **Fade** seconds from whatever was playing (the same music just carries on, so every level can name it). A **Sound Player** plays its **Sound** whenever its **Message** is sent (`Score` from a pickup, a switch's **On Message**, an enemy's **Death Message**) and, with **On Start**, when it appears; **Pitch Variation** changes its pitch a little at random each time, so a sound heard over and over doesn't grate, and **Positional** makes it come from where it is. A **Health** plays its **Hurt Sound** when damage lands and its **Death Sound** when it dies, and a **Button** plays its **Sound** when clicked.

**Sounds in the level.** Add a **Sound Source** to a waterfall, a fire or a machine and its **Sound** plays over and over, loudest near the middle of the screen and fading out by its **Range** (0 is heard everywhere at full volume: rain, wind), from the left or right speaker as it's to that side. Untick **Playing** to start it silent; a switch's **Targets** turn it on and off. Its **Pitch** can change as it plays: an engine that revs. Scripts can play a one-off sound where something is with `Game.PlaySoundAt`.

**Keeping the beat.** Add a **Beat Clock** (next to the scene's **Background Music** is a good place) and it keeps time with whatever music is playing: a scene's, a room's or a script's. It counts what's been *heard*, not what's been sent to the sound card, which holds a few hundredths of a second of sound before it reaches the speakers; so a beat lands with the drum you hear. On every beat it sends its **Beat Message** (`Beat`, with the beat's number, 0 for the first) and on the first beat of every bar its **Bar Message** (`Bar`, with the bar's number), so a **Sound Player** can tick along and a script do anything on the beat with `On<int>("Beat", beat => ...)`. Music made with **Audio → New Music** says its own tempo, so the clock needs no setting up; for other music set its **Tempo** (beats a minute), and **Offset** (seconds into the music of its first beat) for music with a lead-in, or to nudge the beat until it sits right. **Beats Per Bar** is 4 (3 for a waltz). As the music loops the count carries on (beat 33 after a 32-beat tune, not 0 again), each loop starting on a beat of its own. With no music it waits. While it plays, its **Beat**, **Bar**, **Beat In Bar** (0 is the downbeat), **Phase** (how far through the beat, 0 to 1), **Beats** (the count with the fraction), **Position** (seconds into the music) and **Current Tempo** say where it is, and scripts can time a press to the music with `SecondsFromBeat` (negative just before a beat, positive just after) or follow its `BeatStarted` and `BarStarted` events. Give anything a **Beat Pulse** and it bops on the beat: drawn bigger by its **Size** as each beat lands, shrinking back before the next, **Every** that many beats (4 for once a bar), with its children; only how it's drawn changes, not its transform or collider. A pulse follows the scene's first clock, or the one on the entity its **Clock** names. **Spawners**, **Shooters**, **Movers** and **Switches** step to the beat too: set their **Beats** and they spawn, fire, set off for their next point or flip on or off on every that many beats instead of counting seconds. A spawner or shooter still waits out its **Delay** first, then goes on the next beat it steps on (a spawner with no room waits for the next one after that; a shooter with an **Action** fires on the beat while it's held). A mover sets off on a step, gets to the next point at its **Speed** (faster if that wouldn't get it there by the next step), and waits there for the step after rather than for its **Wait**; it waits at its start for the first. A switch flips whoever has worked it, so with **Targets** it brings out spikes every other bar, or opens a gate on the beat (a plate goes back to how it's held). **Beat Offset** shifts each one later by that many beats, so two turrets on every 2 beats with offsets 0 and 1 take turns, and **Clock** names the clock to step to (empty is the scene's first). Scripts can step the same way with `clock.OnBeat(every, offset)`, true in the frame a beat it steps on lands. Scripts can read the music's time themselves: `Audio.MusicTime` is the seconds heard, `Audio.MusicLength` how long it lasts and `Audio.MusicTempo` the tempo it says. In a network game every machine keeps time with its own music, and the host's clock sends the messages to everyone.

**Spawners and lifetimes.** A **Spawner** spawns its **Prefab** every **Interval** seconds where it stands, after a **Delay**, within a **Radius**, up to **Count** in all and **Max Alive** at once; with **Beats** set, it spawns on the music's beat instead (see Keeping the beat). A **Lifetime** destroys its entity after **Seconds**, fading it out over the last **Fade Out** seconds: bullets, effects, score popups.

**Typewriter text.** Add a **Typewriter** next to a **Text** and the text types itself out, **Speed** letters a second, pausing briefly at punctuation, with an optional blip **Sound**. Whenever a script changes the text, the new line types out from the start. It runs on real time, so it works in a paused game.

**Prefabs.** Select an entity and choose **Entity → Save as Prefab** to save it (with its children) to `Assets/Prefabs`. Drag a prefab into the Scene view to place it, or spawn it from a script. Save as Prefab works while playing too, to keep a live entity you've tweaked.

Placed copies stay linked to their prefab: the Hierarchy marks them with a package, and the Inspector says which prefab each is a copy of. Change one copy and choose **Save as Prefab** again: it saves over its own prefab (whatever the copy is called), and every other copy, in this scene and in every other scene, is brought up to date. Each copy takes the prefab's components, scale and children, and keeps its own name and place, so the fields that name it still find it. In the open scene that's one undo step; other scenes are saved straight away. Copies placed inside other prefabs (coins in a chest) are updated too, keeping what that prefab changed on them, so every chest's coins follow the coin (two prefabs that hold copies of each other are left alone). Saving an ordinary entity over an existing prefab links it and updates that prefab's copies the same way. **Entity → Unlink from Prefab** turns copies back into ordinary entities that saving the prefab leaves alone.

What a copy changes is its own. Make one gem red and the Inspector marks its **Tint** (an orange mark by the field, its name in bold, and the prefab's value in its tooltip) and says "Copy of the Gem prefab, with 1 change"; saving the prefab from another copy gives the red gem everything new except its colour. Click the mark (or right-click the field's name) for **Revert to Prefab**, which puts the prefab's value back, and **Apply to Prefab**, which saves this one field into the prefab, so every copy that hasn't changed it takes it too. Components a copy adds show **Added** on their header and come along when the prefab is saved, and components or children it takes away stay away. Things inside a copy count against the matching part of the prefab (children are matched by name), so a brighter lantern on one torch is a change too. **Entity → Revert to Prefab** puts whole copies back as their prefab has them, keeping their name and place, as one undo step. A change is simply whatever differs from the prefab, so setting a field back by hand reverts it as well.

To change the prefab itself, open it on its own: double-click it in Assets (or right-click it and choose **Edit Prefab**), select a copy and choose **Entity → Edit Prefab**, or use **File → Open Prefab**. It opens in a tab of its own beside the scene's, with the prefab selected, and the Hierarchy, Inspector and Scene view edit it there, with its own undo history and a reminder at the top of the Scene view. Whatever you add goes inside it (a prefab is one entity), and fields naming things in the scene, like a chaser's **Target**, aren't flagged, since they're found wherever it's placed. **Save** (Ctrl+S) saves it and brings every copy, in every scene, up to date, each keeping what it changed. Pick the Scene tab to go back (each tab keeps its own selection and view), and close the prefab's tab with its ×, a middle-click or **File → Close Prefab**, which asks to save any changes. Playing plays the scene. If the prefab is saved from a copy while its tab is open, the tab shows the new version too.

**Adding components.** The Inspector's **Add Component** opens a list of every component by category with a search box ready: type a few letters (`patr`) and the one you mean is picked, Up and Down move through what's left, and Enter (or a click) adds it. Along with it come whatever it needs that the entity doesn't have yet, as one undo step: a **Patrol** brings a **BoxCollider** and a **Body**, and the line under the list says so before you press Enter. Components that need something show what in the list ("Needs Body, Collider"). A component whose entity lacks something it needs (a **Platformer Movement** left without its **Body**) is flagged under its name, "Needs a Body, which Player doesn't have", with a button that adds one; a need any kind will fill, like a collider, adds a **Box Collider**. Removing a component that another one there needs asks first and says which ("Platformer Movement needs it on Player, and won't work without it"), and the remove button's tooltip says it too; a second collider can go without asking, since either one will do. Agents see the same: `entity_get` says what each component needs and lacks, and removing a component with `entity_update` notes what that left without something it needs.

**Component catalog.** Next to **Add Component** (the quick list), the Inspector's **Catalog** button (or **Browse Catalog...** at the bottom of the list, which opens it on what you'd typed) opens every component by category, searchable, with what it does, what it **Requires** on the same entity (checked against the selected entity, with what's missing marked), what it **Works well with**, what it's **Needed by**, and each property's kind, starting value and description. Names in the text are links to that component. **Add** puts it on the selected entity (on each of them, with several selected) along with any requirement it's missing (a Patrol brings a Body and a BoxCollider), as one undo step. Scripts are listed too, described by their `///` comments.

In the **Inspector**, components and their choices read as words (**Platformer Movement**, **Ping Pong**), and every field explains itself: hover its name for what it does and in what units (a component's name, and each choice in a list, explain themselves the same way). Drag a number's name sideways to change it, or a vector's X, Y and Z: the further from 0 it is, the faster it goes, so an **Acceleration** of 70 reaches 90 in a short flick while 0.5 still moves by hundredths (Shift for fine steps, Ctrl for coarse; a click without dragging puts you in the box to type). While typing in a number, Up and Down or the mouse wheel nudge it by a tenth of its size (1 for 70, 0.1 for 7), and a run of nudges is one undo step. Fold a component's section shut with the arrow by its name and it stays shut on every entity, and the next time the editor starts, until you open it again. Big components put their fields under subheadings that fold the same way (a **Platformer Movement**'s **Jump**, **Walls**, **Dash**, **Control** and **Sounds and Effects**; a **State** heading for what the game keeps count of, like a turn order's round), and fields only used while something else is on fade until it is: **Dash Speed** with **Dash** off, **Wall Slide Speed** with **Wall Slide** off, a button's **Panel** unless its **Action** opens one. A faded field's name says what it waits for, and it can still be set ahead of time. While the game is playing, typing in the Inspector doesn't reach the game; click the game to give it the keyboard back.

**Colors, Transforms and finding fields.** Click a color field's swatch for a picker: drag in the square for how strong and how bright, along the strips for the hue and how see-through it is, or type a hex. Under them are the Sprite Painter's palette (choosing another palette there changes the painter's too) and the colors you picked lately in any color field, kept for next time. The color changes as you drag, on every selected entity, and each drag or pick is one undo step. The Transform shows in 2D: **Position** and **Scale** in X and Y, and **Rotation** as one angle (degrees, counterclockwise). The **3D** switch on its header shows Z as well, for every entity and next time; in 2D, a Z that isn't at rest is named under the Transform ("Also set in 3D: Position Z 2"), since it still counts (a position's Z orders what's drawn). Type in the filter at the top of the Inspector to show only the fields whose names have it in them (`speed` shows **Speed**, **Dash Speed** and **Wall Slide Speed** and nothing else), or all of a component's or subheading's when its name has it, folded or not; components with no match are left out. The filter stays as the selection changes, so you can step through entities comparing one field; Escape empties it.

**A component's menu.** Right-click a component's header in the Inspector for **Reset to Defaults** (every value back to what a new one starts with), **Copy Values** and **Paste Values** (onto the same kind of component, on this entity or another), **Move Up** and **Move Down** (its place in the entity's list; the Transform stays first) and **Remove Component**. Each works on every selected entity at once, as one undo step.

**Naming entities.** Fields that name another entity (a Follow's **Target**, a Dialogue's **Box**, a Wire's **From**, a door's **Prompt**) have a button listing the scene's entities, indented as in the Hierarchy: pick one to name it (type a few letters to narrow the list, Up and Down to move, Enter to pick), or drag an entity from the Hierarchy onto the field. Fields that name several, separated by commas (a Switch's **Targets** and **Hides**), add each one picked or dropped, and picking a ticked one takes it out again; with several switches selected each keeps its own other names. An attack's **Hitbox** and a panel's **First** control list only what's inside the entity. Typing a name still works, for something that isn't in the scene yet.

Renaming an entity (in the Hierarchy or the Inspector) renames it in every field that names it, as one undo step: rename the player to "Hero" and the camera still follows it, the enemies still chase it. A field keeps its name when it means another entity by it, as the game finds it: one inside its own entity (each lever's own **Hint**), or any of several with that name. A name that matches nothing in the scene (a typo, or something deleted) is shown in yellow with a warning sign by it, saying which names it can't find; a prefab's name counts as a match, since the game can bring one in.

**Naming prefabs, scenes, actions, layers and messages.** Fields that name something else the project has are picked from a list of those too, flagged in yellow when they name something it hasn't: a Spawner's or Shooter's **Prefab** and the effects things leave behind (a Pickup's **Effect**, a Weapon's **Projectile**, a worker's **Builds**) list the project's prefabs, a Door's or Button's **Scene** its scenes, an Attack's or Shooter's **Action** and a Panel's **Open On** the input actions (every game's own and those added in **Game → Controls**), the **Layers** fields (who can collect a pickup, use a door, work a switch) the collision layers colliders and tilemaps are on, here or in any scene or prefab, and message fields the messages the project sends and listens for, each marked "sent", "heard" or both (a Pickup's **Message**, a Counter's, a Health's **Death Message**, a dialogue's `[send]` tags, and the names scripts give `Game.Send` and `On`). A counter listening for a message nothing sends is flagged, and so is a message sent that nothing listens for (unless it's still the component's own, like a Faction's "Defeated"). A collider's own **Layer** is where layers come from, so it's listed but never flagged; a new layer or message is typed in (the list offers to use it as typed). Fields naming several, like **Layers**, tick each one picked and take it out again when picked twice. Prefabs and scenes can be dropped on their fields from the Assets panel too. Every list, entities, images and sounds included, has a search box ready as it opens: type to narrow it. Agents open a field's list with `ui_set` (`IsPickerOpen`, its `Picker.Search`) and pick with `ui_invoke` (`Pick`), and `scene_tree` and `entity_get` report the names that match nothing, as `component_describe` says what each field names.

**Editing several at once.** With several entities selected, the Inspector edits the components they all have, changing all of them together, as one undo step. Components only some of them have are listed after, greyed, with how many have them ("on 2 of 3", and which in the tooltip) and **Add to All**, which gives the rest a copy of the one shown, its values and whatever it needs included. A value they don't share shows as `—` (a check box half-filled, a list blank) until you set it. A vector's axes are separate, so typing an X lines them all up across while each keeps its own Y. The checkbox by the name enables or disables them all; **Add Component** and the remove button add and remove a component on every one of them. Names stay one per entity.

In the **Assets** panel:
- Drag an image or a prefab into the Scene view to place it there, or right-click it for **Add to Scene** (a prefab goes where it was saved from, an image at 0, 0). Either way it's one undo step, and inside a prefab's tab it goes inside the prefab.
- Right-click a file for **Open in Another Program** (programs and scripts aren't run), or anything for **Show in Explorer**.
- Drag any asset onto an Inspector text field to fill in its path.
- Double-click a scene to open it; right-click it to make it the start scene (or pick it under **File → Set as Start Scene**, which lists every scene).
- Rename a prefab from its right-click menu, or drag it onto a folder (or into empty space, for Assets itself) to move it there; **New Folder** on the right-click menu makes somewhere to put it. Every copy stays linked: in every scene, inside other prefabs and in open tabs (the open scene takes it as an undo step, and its file is relinked too). A prefab's name is what `Game.Spawn` and fields like a Spawner's **Prefab** use, so renaming one means changing those.

Games can switch scenes with `Game.LoadScene("Level2")`. **Game → Clear Save Data** forgets what the game saved while playing in the editor.

## Agents (MCP)

While the editor is open it runs a [Model Context Protocol](https://modelcontextprotocol.io) server on this computer, so an agent like Claude Code can build games with it: `claude mcp add --transport http griff http://127.0.0.1:7650/mcp` (or **Help → Copy MCP Setup Command**; **Help → MCP Server** turns it off; `GRIFF_MCP_PORT` picks another port). The server lists no features of its own. Its tools are read from what the editor already has, so everything the editor can do is there, and anything added later shows up by itself:

- **Every command** in the menus and toolbar is a tool, named by its id (`Entity_CreatePlatformerPlayer`, `Game_Play`, `Scene_Save`) and described by its documentation. Commands that act on the selection take an `entity` (or `select` several first: Delete, Duplicate, Cut and Copy act on all of them); a question a command asks (a scene's name, saving changes) comes back to the agent, which answers it with `answers`. Commands that pick something take it as `choice`, and their description lists what there is: the scene to start in (`Scene_SetStart`), a prefab or image to add to the scene (`Asset_AddToScene`, as the Assets panel's right-click menu and dropping on the Scene view do), the Output line of a script error to open (`Output_GoToScriptError`), a saved layout. `Asset_OpenExternally` and `Asset_ShowInExplorer` hand a file in Assets to Windows. Commands that throw something away (Delete, Cut, Clear Save Data, Exit) are marked as destructive.
- **Every component**, Griff's and the project's scripts, can be added and set with `entity_create` and `entity_update` (values as scene files have them), described by `components_list` and `component_describe` (which lists properties under the Inspector's subheadings, says which are only used while another is on, and which module a genre's component is part of). `scene_tree`, `entity_get` and `entity_find` look at the scene, the running game included (while it plays, `entity_get` adds what each component says about itself as it runs and doesn't save, as `live`: a beat clock's `beat`, a spawner's `spawned`; `component_describe` lists what those mean); `scene_tree` ends with the fields naming entities that aren't there, and `entity_get` shows them as `unmatched`. Renaming with `entity_update` renames the entity in the fields that name it, and says which. Each call is one undo step. While a prefab's tab is showing (`Prefab_Open`, `Entity_EditPrefab`), they edit the prefab, `Scene_Save` saves it (updating its copies) and `Prefab_Close` goes back to the scene.
- **Every window, panel and editor service** (the Sprite Painter, the Sound Maker, the Controls window, the workspace, the Scene view's clicks, box selection, moving, rotating, scaling and resizing (its `Handles` mode, `ResizeShape`), dragging distances (`Distances`, `DragDistance`), editing paths (`PathPoints`, `DragPathPoint`, `InsertPathPoint`, `RemovePathPoint`), painting the selected tilemap (`TileTool`, `BrushTile`, `PaintTile`, `PaintRectangle`, `FillTiles`, `PickTile`, `CellAt`) and its layers (`PaintLayers`, `ShowLayer`, `AddLayer`), the links it draws (`Links`), where it looks (`SceneViewport`: `Pan`, `Zoom`, `FrameSelection`), its tile palette (`TilePalette`: `Tilesets`, `Shown`, `Tiles`, `VisibleTiles`, `Scroll`, `Tool`, `Layers`, `Hint`, `Pick`, `Step`, `ShowTileset`), whether script tabs show the component beside the code (`ScriptEditors`' `ShowComponent`), where the panels are (`PanelLayout`: `Dock`, `Tab`, `Float`, `CloseFloating`, `Return`, `Show`, `Save`, `Load`, `Delete`, `Reset`, its `SavedLayouts` and its `Summary`), the Inspector editing several entities at once, a component's menu (`Components[Health]`'s `ResetToDefaults`, `CopyValues`, `PasteValues`, `MoveUp`, `MoveDown` and `AddToAll`), picking entities for its fields (`PickEntity`), a color field's picker (`IsColorOpen`, then its `ColorPicker`'s `Hue`, `Saturation`, `Value`, `Alpha`, `Hex`, `Pick`, `SelectedPalette` and `Recent`), the Inspector's `Filter` and `Transform3D`, the Hierarchy's `Filter`...) can be read and worked with `ui_roots`, `ui_inspect`, `ui_set` and `ui_invoke`. Something picked from a list is given by its name, as a value or an argument: `ui_invoke` the Inspector's `AddComponent` with `["Health"]`. Entities and components are read this way but not changed: `ui_set` and `ui_invoke` refuse them and point to `entity_update`, so every change to a scene is an undo step.
- **Files**: `asset_write` writes scripts (compiled at once, errors reported), pictures and sounds; `asset_read` reads them, pictures as images; `asset_move` renames or moves a prefab, keeping its copies linked.
- **Checking work**: `editor_screenshot` shows the game or the editor, `game_input` plays the running game with keys and the mouse (pointing, clicking and dragging from one thing to another; it can pause it between moves, or set dialogue flags first to try a branch) and reports on what it's told to `watch` (by name, or by id or path to tell apart things with the same name), `editor_state` and `output_read` say what's going on (while the game plays, `editor_state` lists the dialogue flags set and the systems scripts have added, with their phase and the entity each goes with).

It listens on the loopback address only and refuses requests from web pages.

## Scripting

Scripts are plain C# classes in your project's `Assets` folder. **Entity → New Script** creates one, attaches it to the selected entity and opens it. Common namespaces (`System`, `System.Collections`, `System.Numerics`, `Griff`, `Griff.Input`, `Griff.Runtime`, `Griff.Scenes`) are imported automatically.

**The script editor.** Scripts open in Griff's own code editor, as tabs next to Scene and Game: from New Script, by double-clicking a `.cs` file in Assets, with the code button on a script component's header in the Inspector (**Edit Script**), or by double-clicking a script error in Output. It's C# with the compiler behind it, so it knows Griff's whole API and your other scripts, unsaved changes included:

- Colors by meaning: properties, methods, types, parameters and fields each have their own color. Code is set in JetBrains Mono, which ships with Griff (SIL Open Font License; see `Themes/Fonts`).
- Completion as you type, with each item's signature and documentation; signature help inside calls (Up and Down pick an overload); hover anything for its documentation, or for the compiler's message.
- Errors and warnings as you type: underlined where they are, counted in the header (click for the list), and in red in the line numbers.
- **Ctrl+S** saves, compiles and swaps the change into the scene, and into the game if it's playing. Playing saves open scripts first.
- **The component panel** beside the code (the **Component** button) shows the component the script declares: what it does, what it needs, and each property with its kind, starting value and description, plus its value on an entity in the scene that uses it (live while playing), editable right there. Click a property's name to go to it in the code; the property the caret is in lights up. New properties show up as soon as you type them, waiting for a save.
- F12 or Ctrl+click goes to a definition, Shift+F12 lists every use, F2 renames across all scripts, Shift+Alt+F formats, Ctrl+F finds and Ctrl+H replaces (with case, whole-word and regular-expression options), Ctrl+G goes to a line, Ctrl+/ comments lines, Ctrl+D duplicates them, Ctrl+Shift+L deletes them, Alt+Up and Alt+Down move them, and Ctrl+wheel zooms. Brackets and quotes close themselves, `}` lines up with its `{`, uses of the name at the caret are highlighted, blocks fold, and indent guides follow the nesting.
- Snippets: `prop` (a property for the Inspector), `update`, `start`, `trigger` and `coroutine` in a class; `if`, `for`, `foreach` and `while` in a method. Type the name and press Tab.

Scripts can still be edited in any other editor; Griff picks up the saved file either way.

```csharp
public class PlayerController : Behavior
{
    // Public properties are saved with the scene and editable in the Inspector.
    public float Speed { get; set; } = 7;
    public float JumpSpeed { get; set; } = 13;

    protected override void Update()
    {
        var body = GetComponent<Body>()!;
        var velocity = body.Velocity;
        velocity.X = Input.Horizontal * Speed;          // A/D or arrow keys
        if (body.IsGrounded && Input.WasPressed(Key.Space))
        {
            velocity.Y = JumpSpeed;
            Audio.Play("Sounds/jump.wav");
        }
        body.Velocity = velocity;
    }

    protected override void OnTriggerEnter(Entity other)
    {
        if (other.Name == "Coin")
            Game.Destroy(other);
    }
}
```

Describe a script for the component catalog and the Inspector's tooltips with a `///` summary over the class and its properties (say what units a number is in), and say how it fits with other components using attributes:

```csharp
/// <summary>Walks toward the player and explodes next to them.</summary>
[Category("Enemies")]                                  // where the catalog lists it (default: Scripts)
[Requires(typeof(Body), Note = "moves by velocity")]   // Add Component adds a Body along with it
[Requires(typeof(Collider))]                           // a base type: any kind of collider will do
[WorksWith(typeof(Health), Note = "so it can be shot")]
public class Creeper : Behavior
{
    /// <summary>How close it gets before exploding, in world units.</summary>
    [Distance]                                         // drawn as a circle around it in the Scene view, with a handle
    public float FuseRange { get; set; } = 1.5f;

    /// <summary>What it walks toward.</summary>
    [EntityName]                                       // picked from the scene's entities in the Inspector (Many = true for a list)
    public string? Target { get; set; } = "Player";

    /// <summary>Flash before exploding.</summary>
    [Group("Warning")]                                 // a subheading in the Inspector, over this and the properties after it
    public bool Flash { get; set; } = true;

    /// <summary>Flashes a second.</summary>
    [ShownWhen(nameof(Flash))]                         // faded in the Inspector while Flash is off
    public float FlashRate { get; set; } = 4;
}
```

Scenes keep what was set in them when a script changes shape. Rename a class or a property and say what it was called with `[FormerName]`: scenes saved under the old name still load, and save under the new one. When what a value means changes, raise the component's `[ComponentVersion]` and add an upgrade step from the version before, which older scenes run through as they load (steps see properties under their current names). Values a component has no property for (one you removed, or from a newer version of it) are kept and written back, so loading and saving loses nothing. Griff's own components follow the same rules.

```csharp
[FormerName("Creeper")]                                // was class Creeper: scenes that use it still load
[ComponentVersion(2)]                                  // version 1 measured the fuse in seconds; 2 in frames
public class Bomber : Behavior
{
    [FormerName("FuseTime")]                           // was FuseTime
    public int FuseFrames { get; set; } = 90;

    [UpgradeFrom(1)]                                   // brings version 1 data up to 2
    static void SecondsToFrames(SavedData data) => data.Set("FuseFrames", (int)(data.Get<float>("FuseFrames") * 60));
}
```

A few more things scripts often do:

```csharp
// Spawn a prefab (Assets/Prefabs/Bullet.prefab) where the player is.
var bullet = Game.Spawn("Bullet", new Vector2(Transform.Position.X, Transform.Position.Y));

// Do something later, or repeatedly. Timers belong to this entity and stop if it's destroyed.
After(0.5f, () => Game.Destroy(bullet));
Every(2f, () => Game.Spawn("Enemy", new Vector2(10, 0)));

// Is there ground just below? Ignore our own collider.
var below = Game.Physics.Raycast(Game.Scene, new Vector2(Transform.Position.X, Transform.Position.Y), -Vector2.UnitY, 0.6f, ignore: Entity);

// Juice: a burst of particles and a camera kick.
GetComponent<ParticleEmitter>()?.Burst(20);
Game.ShakeCamera(0.3f, 0.2f);

// Tweens: pop in, glide somewhere, then fade out and disappear.
Transform.Scale = new Vector3(0, 0, 1);
ScaleTo(Vector2.One, 0.4f, Ease.OutBack);
MoveTo(new Vector2(4, 2), 1f).Then(() => FadeTo(0, 0.3f).Then(() => Game.Destroy(Entity!)));

// Find things. Every copy of a prefab shares its name; scripts are found by type.
foreach (var enemy in Game.FindAll<Enemy>())
    enemy.Hit();
Game.Find<ScoreCounter>()?.Add(1);

// Random spawn points, loot rolls and variety.
if (Rand.Chance(0.25f))
    Game.Spawn(Rand.Pick(["Coin", "Gem"]), Rand.InsideCircle(3));

// Sequences that take time: a coroutine waits with `yield return` and picks up where it left off.
StartCoroutine(Waves());

IEnumerator Waves()
{
    for (var wave = 1; wave <= 3; wave++)
    {
        for (var i = 0; i < wave * 4; i++)
        {
            Game.Spawn("Enemy", Rand.InsideCircle(6));
            yield return Wait(0.3f);
        }
        yield return WaitUntil(() => Game.FindAll<Enemy>().Count == 0);
    }
    Game.LoadScene("Win", fade: 0.5f);
}

// Messages: tell whoever's interested, without knowing who they are. Listen in Start; listeners stop by themselves
// when their entity is destroyed or the script reloads.
Game.Send("Score", 10);
On<int>("Score", points => Total += points);
On("PlayerDied", () => Game.ReloadScene(fade: 0.5f));

// Named animation clips on one sprite sheet (Clips: "Idle 0-3, Run 4-9 @12, Jump 10, Die 11-15 once").
GetComponent<Animation>()!.Play(body.IsGrounded ? (moving ? "Run" : "Idle") : "Jump");

// Chase the player around walls: the waypoints to head for, one after another.
var path = Game.Find("Level")!.GetComponent<Tilemap>()!.FindPath(Transform.WorldPosition, player.Transform.WorldPosition);
if (path.Count > 0)
    body.Velocity = Vector2.Normalize(path[0] - new Vector2(Transform.Position.X, Transform.Position.Y)) * Speed;

// See what the code is thinking: shapes drawn over the game this frame (or for a few seconds).
Debug.Path(path, Color.Yellow);
Debug.Circle(Transform.Position, SightRange, Color.Red);
Debug.Text(Transform.Position + new Vector3(0, 1, 0), state);

// Line of sight that sees through other enemies: only walls (the Default layer) and the player block it.
var seen = Game.Physics.Raycast(Game.Scene, eye, toPlayer, 10, ignore: Entity, layers: "Default, Player");

// Game flow: start over after dying, keep the music playing from scene to scene, quit from a menu.
Game.ReloadScene(fade: 0.4f);
Game.KeepBetweenScenes(Entity!);
Game.Quit();

// Named input actions: Jump, Fire, Dash, Interact and Pause work on keyboard, mouse and gamepad from the start.
if (Input.WasPressed("Jump") && body.IsGrounded)
    velocity.Y = JumpSpeed;
Input.Bind("Dash", Key.LeftShift, Key.K, GamepadButton.B);   // change one, or add your own

// A jump pressed a moment before landing still jumps when you land (each press counts once), and one pressed a moment
// after running off a ledge still jumps (coyote time). Letting go early makes a short hop.
if (body.WasGrounded(0.1f) && Input.WasPressed("Jump", buffer: 0.12f))
    velocity.Y = JumpSpeed;
if (Input.WasReleased("Jump") && velocity.Y > 0)
    velocity.Y *= 0.5f;

// Move without going faster diagonally; aim with the right stick, or the mouse; shake the controller.
body.Velocity = Input.Move * Speed;
var aim = Input.AimFrom(Transform.Position);
if (Input.IsDown("Fire") && aim != Vector2.Zero)
    Shoot(aim);
Input.Rumble(0.6f, 0.25f);

// A controls screen: press a key for Jump. The player's choice is saved and comes back next time.
if (waitingForKey && Input.AnyPressed is { } pressed)
{
    Input.Rebind("Jump", pressed);
    waitingForKey = false;
}
label.Value = $"Jump: {string.Join(" / ", Input.BindingsOf("Jump"))}";   // "Jump: Space / Gamepad A"

// Type your name for the high score table.
PlayerName = Input.TypeInto(PlayerName, maxLength: 12);

// Hurt things, and make hits feel like hits.
Game.Find("Boss")!.GetComponent<Health>()!.Damage(2, source: Entity);
Flash(Color.Red);        // this entity's sprites, for a moment
Game.HitStop(0.08f);     // freeze the game for a split second
Game.FlashScreen(Color.Red with { A = 0.3f }, 0.2f);   // the whole screen
Game.SlowMotion(0.25f, 1);   // the last enemy falls: a second at quarter speed, easing back

// Scores without a counter of your own: add to the Counter, or read it.
Game.Find<Counter>()!.Add(100);
var score = Game.Find<Counter>()!.Value;

// Dialogue: change the text and the Typewriter types it out. A key press finishes the line, then moves on.
if (Input.WasPressed("Interact"))
{
    if (!typewriter.IsDone)
        typewriter.Skip();
    else
        text.Value = lines[++line];
}

Audio.MusicVolume = 0.5f;

Game.Fullscreen = true;
```

`Rand` gives random numbers for gameplay: `Range`, `Chance`, `Pick`, `Sign`, `Direction`, `InsideCircle`, `Color`, and `Seed` to make a run repeatable.

What a behavior can use:
- **Lifecycle:** `Start`, `Update` (every step of the game: 60 times a second whatever the screen's frame rate, so read the controls and move things here; the screen is drawn between steps, so it moves smoothly), `FixedUpdate` (every step just before bodies move, and only while game time moves: not while paused, at `Time.Scale` 0 or in a hit stop, when physics stops too; push bodies here), `LateUpdate` (every step once everything else has moved: physics, pickups and damage, cameras following their targets; screen anchors are placed after it, so a camera of your own moved here keeps the HUD pinned), `OnCollision`, `OnTriggerEnter`, `OnTriggerExit`, `OnClick` (the entity's button was clicked), `OnDamage` (its Health was hurt: how much, and by what), `OnDeath` (its Health reached zero, just before it's destroyed), `OnPickup` (it collected a Pickup), `OnDestroy`.
- **Services:** `Transform` (with `Teleport(position)`, to move it somewhere new without it being drawn gliding there), `Time` (`Delta`, the step's game time; `Total`, `Scale`; `Frame`, the steps taken; `StepRate`, steps a second, the project's Step Rate unless a script changes it, and `FixedDelta`, a step's length; `Blend`, how far the frame being drawn is between the last two steps; `DrawnFrames` and `RealTotal`, the frames the screen has shown and the real time they took, for a frame rate), `Input` (`IsDown`, `WasPressed`, `WasReleased`, `Horizontal`, `Vertical`, `Move`, `MouseWorld`, `Scroll` (mouse wheel notches this step); keyboard, mouse and Xbox-style gamepads, with `LeftStick`, `RightStick`, `LeftTrigger` and `RightTrigger` (also buttons, `GamepadButton.RightTrigger`, when pulled past halfway), `IsGamepadConnected`, `UsingGamepad` (for showing "Press A" rather than "Press Space") and `Rumble`; `AimFrom(position)`, the right stick or towards the mouse; named actions: `IsDown("Fire")`, `WasPressed("Jump")`, `WasPressed("Jump", buffer: 0.12f)` (a press in the last moment that hasn't been used yet), `WasReleased`, with `Bind` to set an action's keys and buttons or add a new one, and `BindingsOf` to show them. `Rebind` is for the player's own choice from a controls screen: it's saved, comes back next time and wins over `Bind`; `ResetBindings` forgets it, and `AnyPressed` is whatever was just pressed. Actions are case-insensitive; a misspelled one says which exist. `TypedText` and `TypeInto` are for typing names; `PromptFor("Jump")` is what to show for an action on the device in use, "Space" or "A", and `InputPrompts.Name` names any key or button), `Audio` (`Play` (optionally with a `pan` from -1, left, to 1, right, and a `pitch`: 2 is an octave up), `PlayMusic`, `CrossfadeMusic`, `FadeOutMusic`, `StopMusic`, `Loop` (a sound that repeats until stopped, whose `Volume`, `Pan` and `Pitch` can change as it plays), `Music` (what's playing), `MusicTime` (seconds of it heard, allowing for the sound card's delay), `MusicLength` and `MusicTempo` (see Keeping the beat), and `Volume`, `MusicVolume`, `SoundVolume` and `Muted` for an options menu; the same sound started several times in one frame plays once) and `Debug` (`Line`, `Ray`, `Box`, `Circle`, `Point`, `Path`, `Text`: drawn over the game for one frame, or for `seconds:`, with lines the same thickness however far the camera zooms; `Debug.Enabled = false` hides them all).
- **Multiplayer:** `Game.Net` hosts and joins network games and says who's playing. `Host()`, `HostServer()` (a dedicated server), `Join("192.168.1.5")` (or `Join("")` for the first game on the local network) and `Leave()`; `Role`, `IsOnline`, `IsHost`, `IsClient`, `IsConnecting` and `Status`; `Players`, `LocalPlayer` (each a `NetPlayer` with `Id`, `Name`, `Team`, `Color`, `Score`, `Ping`, `IsHost`, `IsLocal` and their `Avatar`), `PlayerJoined`, `PlayerLeft` and `Ended` events, and the messages "PlayerJoined" and "PlayerLeft". `Net.Send("Chat", "gg")` reaches every machine as a message (data can be a number, text, a vector, a color, an entity, a player or a list of them), with `Net.Sender` saying who sent it; `SendToHost` and `SendTo(player, ...)` reach one. `OwnerOf(entity)` and `IsMine(entity)` say whose something is, `Respawn(player)`, `AddScore`, `SetTeam` and `Kick` are the host's, `PlayerName` is remembered, and `FakeLag` pretends a bad connection. Scripts run on the host; their `Input` is the keys of whoever plays their entity (`Game.InputOf(entity)` for any entity), so a script on a player's character answers to that player.
- **Messages:** `Game.Send("Name")` or `Game.Send("Name", data)` reaches every listener straight away and returns how many heard it. `On("Name", () => ...)` and `On<int>("Name", value => ...)` listen until the entity is destroyed or the script is reloaded (like coroutines, so listen in `Start`); they return a listener you can `Cancel()`, and `StopListening()` stops all of a behavior's. Listeners in a scene you leave stop too, unless their entity is kept with `KeepBetweenScenes`. `Game.On` makes one that doesn't belong to a behavior.
- **Timers:** `After(seconds, action)` and `Every(seconds, action)` return a timer you can `Cancel()`. They run on game time, so they pause and slow down with it. `Game.After` and `Game.Every` make timers that don't belong to an entity.
- **Coroutines:** `StartCoroutine(SomeMethod())` runs an `IEnumerator` method straight away up to its first `yield return`, then picks it up again once what it yielded is done: `null` (the next frame), `Wait(seconds)` (or just a number), `WaitUntil(() => ...)`, `WaitWhile(() => ...)`, a tween, a timer, another coroutine, or another `IEnumerator` method (run to the end first, like calling it). They run on game time like timers and return a coroutine you can `Stop()`; `StopCoroutines()` stops all of a behavior's. They stop when the entity is destroyed and when the script is reloaded, and since `Start` then runs again on the new version, a coroutine started in `Start` restarts running your new code. `Game.StartCoroutine` makes one that doesn't belong to a behavior.
- **Tweens:** `MoveTo`, `ScaleTo`, `RotateTo` and `FadeTo` (sprite, text and tilemap alpha) animate this entity. `Tween(seconds, t => ...)` animates anything. Each takes an optional curve from `Ease` (`Linear`, `InQuad`, `OutQuad` (the default), `InOutQuad`, `InCubic`, `OutCubic`, `InOutCubic`, `InOutSine`, `InBack`, `OutBack`, `OutElastic`, `OutBounce`) and returns a tween you can `Cancel()` or chain with `.Then(...)`. A new `MoveTo` takes over from an unfinished one. Like timers, tweens run on game time and stop if their entity is destroyed; `Game.MoveTo(entity, ...)` and friends work on any entity.
- **Systems:** a `GameSystem` does something every step for a whole kind of thing at once, rather than for one entity: override `Update`, and `Phase` to say when it runs (`Begin`, `Logic`, `Scripts`, `Simulate`, `Physics`, `Resolve`, `Present` or `End`; `Logic` unless it says), after Griff's own steps there. `AddSystem(new Magnets())` in a script's `Start` runs it from that frame until the entity goes (destroyed, left behind by a scene change, or the script reloaded); `Game.AddSystem` runs one for the rest of the game, and its `SceneChanged` is called when the scene changes, to forget what it kept. A game has one system of each type, so adding another replaces it (and a scene that adds one each time it starts still runs just one). `Game.RemoveSystem`, `Game.GetSystem<T>()` and `Game.Systems` find and stop them, and `Game.Frame` lists every step of a frame in order, Griff's and yours. Systems run while the game is paused too (`Time.Delta` is 0 then). Override `Draw(FrameDrawing frame)` to draw what the system knows of that no one entity draws, once every entity has drawn itself: `frame.AddOverWorld(...)` puts quads (`FrameDrawing.Quad`, `Line`, `Label`, `Outline`, `Circle`) over the world and under the HUD, and `frame.Sprites` can be changed in place (Griff's strategy, tactics, block world and tool overlays draw this way). A system that sees the world its own way draws it in `DrawView`, before any system's `Draw` (as the first-person view does), and says whose eyes the game is seen through (`Eyes`, which `Game.Eyes` gives and sounds are heard from, and `LooksThrough`). A system that shows a HUD lays it out in `LayOutHud`, early each frame, gives its buttons as `HudButtons` (all of them are `Game.HudButtons`) and a short message as `Notice` (`Game.Notice`), and says when the mouse is over it (`MouseOnHud`), so clicks there aren't meant for the world; on the host of a network game `RunForPlayer` runs one player's own controls (a hotbar, building tools) for each other player, as them. One with something under way that Back (Esc) cancels says so (`ClaimsBack`), so a pause menu waits; `PlayersChanged` is called in a network game as each scene starts and whenever the players change; `GridStepStarting` hears of each grid step before it's taken (a move history remembers where everything was); and `SceneEnding` is called as the game leaves a scene or stops, to keep what it leaves behind (a block world saved).
- **Joining in with Griff's components:** a script component can be what a **Counter** counts (`ICounterSource`: Count Goals, Count Moves, Count Score, Count Floor or Count Kind show the first in the scene that answers), steer a **Grid Movement** itself instead of the player's keys (`IGridDriver`: its next step, and whether each step uses a turn's action), get ready as each of its side's turns begins (`ITurnReady`), do something when a message is sent (`IHearsMessages`, as a dungeon goes down a floor on its Next Floor Message), keep the number keys and the mouse wheel for itself (`IClaimsNumberKeys`, as a hotbar does, so building tools are picked by clicking), and tell an agent playing the game what it's up to (`IPlaytestReport`), the same way Griff's match boards, tactics units, goals and dungeons do. Each genre's types (`MatchBoard`, `Commander`, `TacticsUnit`, `Weapon`, `MoveHistory`) live in their module's namespace (`Griff.Modules.Strategy`, `Griff.Modules.FirstPerson`), which scripts get without a `using`, and what a genre offers on `Game` (`Game.Train`, `Game.Craft`, `Game.Equip`, `Game.Undo()`) comes with it.
- **Drawing:** a component that implements `IDrawable` draws itself, as Griff's sprites, tilemaps, text, bars, particles, boards, ropes and wires do: `Draw(Drawing drawing)` adds quads (`Rect` in the entity's own space; `Quad`, `Line`, `Outline`, `Circle`, `Text`, `Image` and `Bar` in the world), knowing what's in view (`View`), how thick a thin line is (`LineWidth`) and whether the game is running (`IsPlaying`). The game is drawn between its last two steps: transforms get there by themselves, and what a component draws from its own numbers gets there through `drawing.Blend`, a `StepBlend` saying how far the frame is between the steps. Keep a number, point or color that changes in a `Smoothed<T>` field (`_angle.Set(this, value)` as the game runs), and draw `drawing.Blend.Of(_angle)` (`TurnOf` for an angle, the short way round), or keep what it was yourself and draw `drawing.Blend.Between(was, now)`; it follows the transforms' rules, drawing as it is whatever jumped. A system drawing the game's own state asks `frame.Blend` (or `frame.BlendOf(entity)`), and draws a HUD it laid out over the camera's view during the step through `frame.PinToView`, so it keeps still on the screen as the camera moves, zooms and shakes between steps; an `IDrawEffect` asks `StepBlend.For(entity)`. `DrawOrder` places the entity among the others (0 unless it says; an entity's sprite sets it if it has one) and `DrawStep` places its draws among the entity's own (`DrawSteps.Shapes`, over the sprite, unless it says; from `DrawSteps.OverWorld` on, after the whole world). Each frame, `Draw` is called once for each entity in hierarchy order (the Hierarchy panel's, top to bottom), and for an entity's drawables in `DrawStep` order; the draws are then put back to front, each entity's kept together, so a drawable is called in the same order whatever layer it's on and doesn't see what's drawn under it (a system's `Draw` sees the whole frame). `IDrawEffect` changes how its entity is drawn without drawing (a tint for its sprite and text, a transform for it and what's inside it, or drawing it over everything), and `ILightSource` gives off light in the dark. The Scene view draws them too.
- **Networking:** see Multiplayer above for `[NetLocal]`, `[PlayerMovement]`, `[Sent]`, `[NotSent]` and `INetState`.
- **Game:** `Spawn` (an entity, or a prefab by name), `Destroy`, `Find` and `FindAll` (by name, or `Find<T>()` and `FindAll<T>()` by component type; `Game.Scene.Active<T>()` gives just those on enabled entities, and asking every frame costs next to nothing, since the scene keeps its components by type), `LoadScene` and `ReloadScene` (optionally with a `fade:` in seconds and a `color:`; fades use real time, so they work from a paused menu, and `IsChangingScene` is true until the switch), `KeepBetweenScenes` (carry an entity into the next scenes, still running; returning to its original scene doesn't duplicate it), `Quit`, `ShakeCamera`, `HitStop` (freeze game time for a moment of real time; `Time.IsFrozen` is true meanwhile), `SlowMotion` (game time at a fraction of normal speed for a moment of real time, easing back; on top of `Time.Scale`, and `Time.SlowMotionScale` says how much), `Flash` (an entity's sprites and its children's), `FlashScreen` (the whole view, fading out on real time), `Fullscreen` (remembered, like the volumes), `Focus` and `Focused` (the button or slider picked for the keyboard and gamepad), `OpenPanel`, `ClosePanel`, `TopPanel`, `OpenPanels` and `IsPaused` (see Pause menus and panels), `ListenForBinding` and `Rebinding` (see Controls screens), `FadeThrough` (fade out, call something while the screen is covered, fade back in), `LightAt` (how lit a place or an entity is, 0 to 1), `PlaySoundAt` (a sound from somewhere on or off screen), `StartDialogue`, `AdvanceDialogue`, `ChooseDialogue` (pick one of its choices) and `Talking` (the dialogue being said), `HasFlag`, `SetFlag` and `FlagChanged` (see Dialogue flags), `SaveData` (`GetInt`, `GetFloat`, `GetBool`, `GetString`, `Set`, `Delete`, `Clear`) and `Physics` (`Raycast`, `OverlapPoint`, `OverlapBox`, `OverlapCircle`; each takes `layers:` to only find colliders on those layers).

- `Attack` (`Swing`, `IsAttacking`, `IsHitting`, and `Swung` and `Hit` events); see Swords and close-up attacks above. `TurnOrder` (`EndTurn()`, `IsTurn(side)`, `IsComputer(side)`, `IsBetweenTurns`, `SideList`, and `TurnStarted` and `TurnEnded` events; set `Current` to hand the turn to a side) and `TurnTaker` (`IsTurn`, `Act(count)`, `ActionsLeft`, and `TurnStarted` and `TurnEnded` events); see Turns above. Tilemaps have `SlopeOf`, `SlopeCells` and `Physics.SlopeShapes`;
- `BoxCollider`, `CircleCollider` and `Body`, for physics. Boxes and circles collide in any combination. `GetComponent<Collider>()` finds either kind. A body's `Bounciness` (0 to 1) makes it bounce off what it hits, settling once the bounces get small. `Ground` is what it's standing on; when that moves (a platform moved by a script or a tween) the body rides along with it. `WasGrounded(0.1f)` is true while grounded and for that long after walking off an edge (but not after jumping), `AirTime` is how long it's been off the ground, and `WallSide` is -1 or 1 while a wall on that side stops it (for wall jumps), and `DropThrough()` falls through one-way platforms for a moment (`IsDroppingThrough`; `Game.Physics.OnlyOneWayBelow` says whether there's anything else underneath). Give colliders a **Layer** (`Player`, `Enemy`, `Bullet`; empty is `Default`) and list what they pass through in **Ignore Layers**, e.g. `Player, Bullet` on a bullet: ignored pairs don't block each other, collide or trigger, and only one side has to list the other. Tilemaps have both too.

Saving a script while the game is playing applies it immediately, in every player's window when playing with several. Public properties are kept, and `Start` runs again on the new version.

Rewinding restores everything a scene file holds. Things that only exist while running (timers, coroutines, message listeners, systems scripts added (their scripts start again and add them afresh), tweens, live particles, camera shake, a scene fade, debug shapes, hit flashes, screen flashes, slow motion, invincibility, hit stop, a typewriter's progress, spawner and lifetime clocks, which control is picked, a bar's trail, a chaser's sense of where the target is, a conversation, a respawn countdown, a rebind button waiting for a key, a dash or a climb under way, a player's air jumps, a crumbling ledge's shake or its countdown back, a swing under way, a light's flicker, weather's drops and the next lightning, the camera's slide into a room and the room's light fading in, sound sources' loops, which sound players and background music have started, a beat clock's count and a beat pulse's bop (both follow the music, which plays on), and in a strategy game the selection, orders, paths, training queues, arrows in flight and what the fog has uncovered) start fresh from the moment you resume, the same way private fields do. Movers, patrols, checkpoints and panels keep their place: a mover's **Time**, a patrol's **Walked**, a checkpoint's **Active** and a panel's **Open** are saved, so rewinding into a pause keeps the pause menu up. So do puzzles: switches, what they've switched (**Running**, **Locked**), keys used up, inventories and a match-3 board's gems and **Swaps** are all in the scene (the slides and falls under way start again from the tiles), while trail copies and the animated tiles' clock start fresh. A ledge that has crumbled stays gone (its **Crumbled** is saved) and comes back in its own time.

**Rewind in big scenes.** A rewind snapshot holds what the scene file would and loads back as the file would (the same ids; a color keeps its 8 bits a channel, and a number that isn't finite comes back as the property's default), kept in a compact binary form made without garbage. Taking one is split so the game waits for as little of it as it can. Between two steps, each entity's values are copied out into a record of its own, its strings kept as they are (a string never changes) and lists of lines copied, so the snapshot is that moment whatever happens after; the code that copies a component is compiled for its type the first time it's seen, so this is little more than reading the values, even in a Debug editor. Then, on another thread (it reads nothing but the copies), each record is compared with the entity's record in the last snapshot and only the bytes that changed are kept: about 25 bytes for a bunny that moved, 4 for a wall that didn't. Every tenth snapshot is kept whole, so rewinding to one decodes at most ten, and one that's dropped stays in memory (and counted) until nothing kept is written against it. Snapshots are taken ten times a second. A big scene's are taken less often only so that they fit in 256 MB (`Timeline.MemoryBudget`) while still reaching back about 20 seconds, or so that copying them out costs a step half a millisecond at most on average in the editor (`Timeline.CostPerStep`, at most two seconds apart): a step waits for it, and a slow one makes that frame late. A BunnyMark bunny takes about 35 bytes a snapshot (200 before). 20,000 of them take 1.1 ms to copy out (2.8 ms in a Debug editor) and 1.5 ms (7.2 ms) to settle on the other thread, and they're kept ten times a second in both (before: 3.5 ms (15 ms) all on one step, every 0.6 seconds (every two)); 50,000 take 2.9 ms (6.6 ms), kept about seven times a second (four). Rewinding a script rebuilt while the game ran reads its values by name, as the scene file would. While the game plays, the Hierarchy and the Inspector catch up with it ten times a second (`SceneDocument.RuntimeRefreshed`) rather than every frame, and as it pauses or steps; the Hierarchy keeps the rows of entities still there and makes only the rows in sight, so a scene of 20,000 entities fills it at once.

## Architecture

```
src/
  Griff                    Engine core: scenes, serialization, editing/undo, runtime, physics,
                           render lists, audio (mixer, sound and music synths), networking
                           (UDP with reliable and unreliable messages, and multiplayer on top),
                           projects and templates. No UI dependencies.
    Modules/               The genres, each in a folder of its own: BlockWorlds, Contraptions, Dungeons,
                           FallingBlocks, FirstPerson, Match3, Puzzles, Strategy, Tactics
  Griff.Scripting          Roslyn script compiler, hot reload, game exporter
  Griff.Graphics           OpenGL 3.3 sprite and text renderer
  Griff.Platform.Windows   WGL context, WPF game surface, frame pacing, input, sound output and decoding,
                           text rasterizing
  Griff.Editor             WPF editor: modules, commands, panels, design system (Themes/)
  Griff.Player             Runs exported games
tests/
  Griff.Tests              Unit and integration tests, including playing every template
  Griff.Editor.Tests       The editor's real services, the agent surface, and exported games run for real
```

The engine lives in `Griff` so that the editor, the player and the tests all use one model. Its layers: `Griff.Scenes` is the data (entities, components and how they're saved), `Griff.Runtime` runs it and `Griff.Rendering` turns it into draw lists. What both sides need lives below them, in `Griff` (`Color`, `Box`, `Ease`, `Rand`) and `Griff.Input` (`Key`); an entity knows whether it's active (`IsActive`, counting its parents) and which machine runs it in a network game, and a component that needs the running game gets a small `IGameContext` rather than the `Game`.

A game moves in fixed steps (`Time.StepRate`, 60 a second): each frame the screen shows, `Game.Advance` takes as many as the real time since the last frame holds (none on a fast screen's frame, two on a slow one's; at most a tenth of a second's worth, so a stall slows the game rather than piling up steps), keeps the rest for the next frame, and says how far the frame is between the last two steps (`Time.Blend`). A frame within 12% of a whole number of steps counts as exactly that many, so a steady screen takes the same steps every frame instead of now and then none and then two, and the player times frames by the compositor's vertical blank (`FramePacer`, from `DwmGetCompositionTimingInfo`), the screen's steady beat, rather than when the UI thread got to them. Input counts in the next step taken, once: each step ends the input's frame, and a frame with no step keeps what was pressed for the next. While the renderer builds a running game's frame, each transform's `WorldMatrix` is where it's drawn: one that moved in the last step is placed between where it was as the step began (each transform remembers that the first time it moves in a step) and where it is, inside wherever its parent is drawn, so children, the camera and everything pinned to it glide together. What jumped in the step is drawn where it is: added to the scene, moved to another parent, `Teleport`ed (respawns and doors do), a camera snapping to its target, a move further than a quarter of the view, a turn of more than 90 degrees or a flip; so is everything on the step a scene came in on, and a game held still (`Game.ShowAsIs`, which the editor's pause and rewind use). What's drawn from a component's own state rather than a transform goes between steps the same way, by the same rules (`StepBlend`, from `Drawing.Blend` or `FrameDrawing.Blend`): a `Smoothed<T>` value remembers what it was the first time it changes in a step, as a transform does, and is drawn between that and what it is only if it changed in the last step and its entity (or one it's inside) didn't jump. Particles and rain keep where each was before it last moved (and how it was turned and how far through its flutter or flicker), a trail's copies how old they were, a sprite's, text's or tilemap's tint, a sprite's flash and a sprite's and text's size are `Smoothed`, as are the screen's fade and flash, the camera's zoom, shake, background and turn, the screen effects' settings, a light's reach, color, brightness and flicker, the ambient light of a room fading in, a bar's value and trail, health (for the meters that show it), a beat pulse settling back (a beat landing is drawn as it is), a crumbling platform's shake, and a first-person viewer's look up and down and walk's bob, its weapon's kick, flash and coming up, and billboards bobbing; debug shapes a step draws are drawn between those of the step before when they're the same shapes in the same order. A match-3 board's slides, falls and clears each keep how far along they were as the step began, and the gems that came home or cleared away in the step are drawn the rest of the way; a falling-block board's piece is drawn between where it was and is when it slid or fell (as it is when it comes in or turns), full rows shrink between steps, and the step they go in they shrink the rest of the way as the rows above drop into their place. Strategy and tactics meters, building and training progress, order markers and notices, and a block world's digging are drawn between steps too, and every system HUD is pinned to the view as it's drawn. What changes all at once (a board's tiles, a sprite's frame, a turned piece) is drawn as it is. Ropes and wires follow the transforms they join. The step rate is the project's (`Project.StepRate`, in `project.griff`), which every host sets as it starts a game (`game.UseProjectSettings(project)`, with the controls). `FixedStepTests` plays the same input at 30, 60 and 144 frames a second and with frames all over the place, slow motion and hit stop in it, and gets the same game to the last bit; and a host and a player on screens of different rates stay in step. Each step goes through the game's frame: a list of steps in eight phases (begin, logic, scripts, simulate, physics, resolve, present, end), taken in order (`Game.Tick` takes one step by hand, of any length); `Game.Frame` lists them, and `FrameTests` pins the order, so changing it is always on purpose. The steps belong to systems: Griff's keep what a kind of game tracks while a scene runs (levels' doors and conversations, switches, dragging, panels, and each module's: block worlds, contraptions, dungeons, falling and match boards, first person, puzzles, strategy, tactics) and forget it when the scene changes, and scripts add their own. The scene keeps its components by type as it changes (`Scene.All<T>()`, `Scene.Active<T>()`, in hierarchy order, and safe to go through while spawning and destroying), each entity keeps whether it's active, and each transform keeps its world matrix until it or something it's inside moves, so a step finds what it works on without walking the scene; a frame of sprites and scripts allocates nothing, and nor does drawing it into the last frame's lists (`BigSceneTests`). A script is called only for the steps its type has (`Update`, `FixedUpdate`, `LateUpdate`), from lists kept until the scene's scripts change, and only the sprites flashing or blinking are moved on each step. A frame is built in one pass over the entities: each is drawn as it's found, in hierarchy order, and then the runs of draws are put back to front by what their entities sort by, each run kept together. Entities in order but for a few stretches (a crowd on one layer with a HUD over it) are merged; others are sorted by key (`StableSort`, a radix sort that skips whatever every entity shares) rather than by comparing them; and the draws move as little as they can (none in a scene already in order; otherwise the biggest block that stays together moves in place and the rest by way of a spare array). An entity knows what kinds of component it has and whether a sprite is all it draws, so the renderer skips the rest without asking each component, and the OpenGL renderer draws each quad as one instance of 80 bytes, in batches of up to 16,384 that each go into fresh buffer storage, so the GPU never waits for the last batch to be read. `FastPathTests` checks each shortcut gives the same game and picture as going through everything. The editor's rewind snapshots (`Timeline`) are the scene file in binary (`SnapshotCodec`: each property read and written through typed delegates, with no text and no boxing, one record an entity, shared with the last snapshot when it hasn't changed), and `TimelineTests` plays every template, rewinds it, and checks each snapshot loads as its scene file would and plays on the same. Physics files shapes in a grid each step (`ShapeGrid`), so a body or a trigger only tests what's near it, in the order it would have met them testing everything. A running game advances once for every frame the screen shows, timed by Windows' compositor (`FramePacer`) rather than WPF's render loop, which on some machines only gets every other one. The editor itself is built from modules: each feature registers its services, commands, panels and menus, and the shell assembles them. Services reach each other through dependency injection (there are no static locators), and a window's or panel's state lives in its view model, which agents reach. The Scene view, for one, is the `SceneView` module: `SceneViewModel` (what it does with the selection, and painting), `SceneViewport` (where it looks) and `TilePalette` are services, and `SceneViewControl` only puts together drawing (`SceneViewDrawing`), where the handles are (`SceneHandleGeometry`) and the mouse and keys (`SceneViewInput`). See `src/Griff.Editor/Themes/README.md` for the design system.

Drawing and networking are open to every component, so the renderer and the network schema know no genre by name. Each component that draws is an `IDrawable` (its draw order, and its step among its entity's draws), changed by its entity's `IDrawEffect`s, and a running game's systems draw what the game itself knows of (`GameSystem.Draw`): a strategy game's fog and HUD, a tactics game's reachable cells, a block world's hotbar, the building tools, and (in `DrawView`, before the rest) a first-person view in place of the top-down one. Each component says what it sends over the network with attributes (`[NetLocal]`, `[PlayerMovement]`, `[Sent]`, `[NotSent]`) or `INetState`. `SceneRenderer` keeps only what a view is made of: cameras, parallax, light and dark, and what's pinned to the screen.

A genre is a module: everything of it in one folder under `src/Griff/Modules` with a namespace to match (`Griff.Modules.Match3`), its components, its system (which runs it, keeps what it tracks and draws what no one entity does), its network commands, its template with its pictures, and the Entity menu's presets for it (`EntityPreset`) and things it does to the selection (`EntityAction`, such as Generate Dungeon), which the editor makes into commands and agents' tools. Every genre is a module: Block Worlds, Contraptions, Dungeons, Falling Blocks, First Person, Match-3, Puzzles (goals and undo for grid puzzles), Strategy and Tactics. A module registers once, in its `GameModule` class's `AddTo`: its systems, and each of their steps next to the step of the frame it follows or goes before (`StepAfter("Dragging", "Match Boards", ...)`), so the frame keeps its order; the commands players' machines send its host (`OnNetCommand`), and those the host sends one player's machine (`OnHostCommand`). Everything else of it is found. The core names no module, and no module names another, so each stands alone: Griff's own components reach a module's through interfaces they call (a Counter counts from an `ICounterSource`, grid movement asks an `IGridDriver` for its steps, turns tell `ITurnReady` components a turn has begun, `Game.Send` tells `IHearsMessages` components, building tools leave the number keys to an `IClaimsNumberKeys` component, `IPlaytestReport` adds to what an agent playing the game is told, and a module's component lists itself on a core component's Works With, `[WorksWith(..., Back = ...)]`), and the game asks its systems (`GameSystem.MouseOnHud`, `ClaimsBack`, `PlayersChanged`, `DrawView` and `Eyes`, `LayOutHud`, `HudButtons` and `Notice`, `RunForPlayer`, `GridStepStarting`, `SceneEnding`). Turns, grid movement, switches, inventories and trails stay in the core: Griff's own components build on them (grid movers step a turn at a time, attacks use a turn's actions, a door takes a key, a dash leaves a trail) and so do several genres, so each is a foundation with its own extension points rather than a genre. A script component or system can use every one of them. What a module offers scripts as part of `Game` is in extension members (`Game.Train`, `Game.Selected`, from `StrategyGame`), and scripts get every module's namespace without a `using`; components keep the names they're saved under (`[ComponentName("Griff.MatchBoard")]`), so scenes made before the move load as they were.

Components and templates are found rather than listed. Every component in `Griff` is registered without being named anywhere (`ComponentRegistry.CreateDefault`, which scripts' components join the same way on each compile), and every `ProjectTemplate` (a class with its description, its place in New Project's list and what it builds: scenes, prefabs, scripts, pictures and music) is offered by New Project and played by the template tests (`ProjectTemplates.All`). Adding either is adding a class. Scene data outlasts changes to them: a component can say what it was called before (`[FormerName]`), version its data with upgrade steps (`[ComponentVersion]`, `[UpgradeFrom]`), and keeps values it has no property for. `ComponentSwap` is what hot reload uses to rebuild script components in place when their types change.

Tests keep the architecture in shape, so a change that breaks it fails rather than slipping in:
- **Layers:** nothing in `Griff.Scenes` (data) uses `Griff.Runtime` or `Griff.Rendering` (`LayerTests`).
- **Extension points:** the renderer, the frame systems draw into and the network schema name no component but those a view or an entity's networking is made of (`ExtensionPointTests`).
- **Genre modules:** the core (everything in `Griff` outside `Modules`) names no module's types, no module names another's, each module keeps to its own folder and namespace with one module class, and a module's components are data like the core's, using neither `Griff.Runtime`, `Griff.Rendering` nor its systems (`ModuleTests`, `LayerTests`).
- **Agents:** every command is an MCP tool with a description, every panel and service is reachable (`AgentSurfaceTests`), and no code-behind (`*.xaml.cs`) runs an edit or starts a process, where agents couldn't reach it (`CodeBehindTests`).
- **Editor modules:** the shell registers none of a feature's types (each module registers its own), and no editor service sets a static of itself for others to reach it by (`SceneViewStructureTests`).
- **Players:** a template is exported with both players and run for real; it has to draw a frame (`PlayerSmokeTests`).

Neither the layer test nor the code-behind test has exceptions left.

## Building

Requires the .NET 10 SDK on Windows.

```
dotnet build
dotnet test
dotnet run --project src/Griff.Editor
```

Every project shares `Directory.Build.props` (nullable on, warnings are errors, the .NET analyzers' recommended rules), package versions live in `Directory.Packages.props`, and `.editorconfig` holds the code style. CI (`.github/workflows/ci.yml`) builds the editor and runs both test projects on Windows for every push and pull request, with Mesa's software OpenGL so the player smoke test can draw.

**BunnyMark** measures an exported game: `powershell -NoProfile -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File tools\bunnymark.ps1` builds the editor in Release, exports the BunnyMark template with the standalone player, plays it on screen until it has found its count and held it for ten seconds, and prints the count with the hold's frame rate, 1% low and garbage collections (`-Runs 3` prints the middle of three; `-Count 50000` holds that many instead of finding the count; `-NoBuild` skips the build). Close other programs first. It's a heuristic, not a test: the count varies by about a sixth from run to run, so look into a drop rather than trust one run. Changes to how the game runs record their count before and after. **BunnyBench** (`dotnet run -c Release tools\bunnybench.cs -- --count 50000`) measures the same bunnies with nothing on the screen, in seconds: it times each frame's steps, building the frame, OpenGL drawing it (into a hidden window) and the GPU's part, and counts the garbage collections and bytes allocated (`--steps 2` takes two steps a frame, as after a missed one; `--by-step` adds what each of the game's steps costs; `--no-draw` leaves OpenGL out; `--timeline` keeps rewind snapshots as the editor does while playing and times them as Rewind, with the memory they take and how long copying them out and settling them take). On the RTX 3060 Ti PC, 50,000 bunnies take 2.1 ms of steps, 6.5 ms to build and 1.4 ms to draw (10 ms of a 16.7 ms frame; 12 ms with two steps), with the GPU's 5.3 ms alongside, and allocate 700 bytes a frame; before the BunnyMark work they took 4.4, 13.8 and 6.9 ms, and drawing made 14 MB of garbage a frame. It counts the frames the screen shows, as it always did; since the game moves in fixed steps, a frame that falls behind takes two steps the next time, so past the count the frame rate drops off sharply rather than gently, and the count is where the game can no longer catch up after a missed frame: the frame after one takes two steps, and if those and drawing don't fit in a sixtieth of a second the game settles at 30 frames a second. Fixed steps cost the count about 7% (30,600 before, about 28,500 after); drawing between steps costs next to nothing (with one step a frame and the drawing still blended, it held 30,634).

Exported games' scripts are compiled optimized, like a Release build; the editor compiles them for debugging, so hot reload can step through them. Exported games are self-contained: they include the .NET runtime and run on any 64-bit Windows PC without installing anything (expect a zipped download of about 60 MB).

## Status

Griff is young. Known gaps:
- CI runs on Windows only, and Griff.Graphics and Griff.Platform.Windows have no tests of their own: the player smoke test covers them end to end. The layer test checks Griff.Scenes; Runtime still reaches into Rendering.
- Modules are found only in Griff itself: a project can't add one. Templates several modules share pictures from (the crate, the crypt's hero and walls) keep those in the core's `TemplateArt`.
- The Scene view draws sprites, tilemaps, text and particles itself and every other drawable from its quads, but not a board that draws its tilemap in the game (there you paint the tiles).
- No web export yet.
- Templates are found only in Griff itself: a project can't add one of its own to New Project. A component saved by a newer version of it keeps the values it doesn't know, but is written back at this version.
- Big scenes: an exported game holds about 78,600 BunnyMark bunnies at 60 fps (75,224 to 78,666 over 3 runs on an RTX 3060 Ti PC, October 2026; about 69,000 with a frame built in two passes over the entities, 28,200 before that), and 50,000 at 60 fps with 1% lows at 60 fps and no garbage collections in ten seconds, the aim set for it. Building a frame of 50,000 takes 5.4 ms of the CPU's 9.2 (`tools/bunnybench.cs`; 6.5 of 10.2 in two passes): most of it is each entity's own drawing (placing it between steps, making its quad). A crowd that comes after what's drawn over it in the hierarchy (BunnyMark's bunnies come after its HUD) still has its draws moved along once a frame, about 0.5 ms at 50,000. Text whose words change (a score, the frame rate) is drawn again with WPF, which makes garbage: the odd collection in a long run. Playing in the editor keeps up with them now (see Rewind in big scenes): a Debug editor holds 5,000 bunnies at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60 and 1 garbage collection in ten seconds (before: 39 to 45 fps, a 1% low of 7, 69 to 81 collections), and 20,000 at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60 (before: 59 fps, a 1% low of 30), snapshotting them ten times a second (before, every two seconds); a Release editor holds 20,000 at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60, and 50,000 at 59 fps with a 1% low of 60 (before: 58 fps, a 1% low of 30). Copying a snapshot out still happens on one step (it has to, to be one moment), so the biggest scenes are snapshotted less often to keep their frames on time: 50,000 bunnies about seven times a second in a Release editor and four in a Debug one, and just after rewinding a big scene, whose entities are all fresh, a little less often for a while.
- Mouse look turns once a step, and the screen shows the game up to a step behind, so it trails the mouse by a step or two; so does what follows the mouse (a strategy game's drag box and placing ghost, the contraptions tools' line, something being dragged), which is drawn where the mouse was at the last step. A drop of rain wrapping round the view is drawn where it lands a step early (out of view).
- Physics uses axis-aligned boxes and circles. Rotation is ignored, slopes are tilemap tiles at 45 degrees only, and circle bodies slide rather than roll.
- Shadows are hard-edged, and come from colliders and tiles rather than sprites' shapes.
- A tilemap's extra tilesets are numbered by their place in **More Tilesets**: taking one out, or putting one before another, renumbers the ones after it, and cells painted from them change tile. The Sprite Painter's **Paint** button on a tilemap paints its first tileset (open the others from the Assets panel).
- A tile drawn at a size its pixels don't divide evenly can pick up a column of the pixels beside it in the tileset along an edge nothing covers (an isometric tile's left and right corners): leave a tile's outermost columns clear, as the Isometric template's tileset does.
- A tilemap's layers are switched and added under the palette, but put in order, hidden and removed in the Hierarchy, and every layer of a map shares its tilesets and tile settings (an isometric map's shape and **Tile Height** too, so a tileset's tiles are all one height).
- Isometric maps don't cast shadows yet. Hex maps are drawn and painted, but don't collide or cast shadows, grid movers don't step from hexagon to hexagon on them yet (nor do Tactics and turn-based play), autotiles join on a square's four sides (not a hexagon's six), and hexagons only point up and down (no flat-topped hexagons in columns). On an isometric map, bodies meet other colliders as boxes (only the walls are diamonds), welded contraptions pass through its walls, a sword or other close-up attack swings to the side of the screen a grid mover faces (not along the diagonal), and the Tactics template's highlighted cells are drawn as squares. A row of standing tiles sorts among the sprites by its middle, so something wider than a cell (a two-cell sofa, a big monster) can be drawn wrongly against the tiles beside it.
- A clip's frames are one run of the sprite sheet in order (no skipping or repeating frames, or playing backwards), and a clip given its own speed or looping in the Clip Editor can only go back to the animation's by editing **Clips**. Renaming a clip in the Clip Editor doesn't rename it in scripts that play it by name (`Play("Run")`) or in the clip names movement components look for (`Idle`, `Run`, `Jump`...).
- A floating window holds panels tabbed together, not side by side, and the document tabs (Scene, Game, prefabs, scripts) stay in the main window.
- The Scene view's handles resize sprites, box and circle colliders, distances (light radii, ranges, sight) and paths only on the entity picked last, even with several selected, and distances of 0 have no handle until they're set in the Inspector. A mover's start is where it's placed (move the entity), and a patrol's route is level, left and right.
- Dragging rows in the Hierarchy moves one at a time, even with several selected.
- The Inspector's and Hierarchy's filters match names only: not a field's value, or the components an entity has. The color picker has no eyedropper for picking a color off the screen.
- Copied component values stay inside the editor until it closes (not on the system clipboard), and paste only onto the same kind of component. Components aren't dragged to reorder them; use Move Up and Move Down.
- Fields that name entities hold their names: renaming an entity updates them in the open scene, but not in other scenes, prefabs or scripts. Fields naming prefabs, scenes, layers and messages aren't renamed with them either (they're flagged instead). Messages and layers in scripts are only found where they're written out (`Game.Send("Score")`, `On("Score", ...)`, `Layer = "Bullet"`), not built up in code, and a script listening by a name it builds can leave its message flagged. Items, teams, turn sides and save names are typed (they have no list yet). In a prefab's tab, only fields naming something inside their own entity (an attack's **Hitbox**) are flagged; the rest name things in whatever scene it's placed in.
- A copy placed inside another copy in a scene follows its own prefab (what the outer copy changes on it isn't kept). Renaming or moving a prefab outside Griff (in Explorer) unlinks its copies, renaming one doesn't rename it where it's spawned by name (a Spawner's **Prefab**, `Game.Spawn`), and only prefabs can be renamed or moved in Assets (not other files or folders). Children of a copy are matched to the prefab's by name, so renaming one counts as taking it away and adding another (and renaming one in the prefab's tab loses what copies changed on it). A prefab's tab keeps no crash-recovery copy, as scenes do. A prefab file changed outside Griff (not through Save as Prefab, Apply to Prefab or an agent's asset_write) doesn't update its copies.
- Grid crates are pushed, never pulled, and stop after one cell (no ice to slide across). Undo puts back only grid movers (not what was collected or destroyed, counters, switches or turns), levels are built from entities on a tilemap (there's no reading Sokoban text levels in, and the Puzzle template's menu remembers which levels are solved, not the fewest moves), an editor rewind starts the move history afresh from the moment rewound to, and a network game has no undo. In a network game a player's push moves the crate once the host hears of it, so their character steps into the crate's cell a moment before the crate moves off. A rewind that lands partway through a step puts a grid mover on the nearer of the two cells (and a grid patrol waiting at a point waits afresh). Grid enemies chase and patrol but don't flee or wander at random, and grids are square or isometric (not hex yet).
- Match-3 gems are plain kinds: there are no special gems (stripes, bombs) or obstacles over the gems (ice, chains), every cell of the board's rectangle is in play (blocks stand in for holes), gems only fall straight down (never sliding past a block's corner), and swaps wait until the board is still. A won level doesn't turn the moves left into points, and goals are a score and gems of a kind (not blocks to break or things to bring down). A rewind's refills come from the board's Seed afresh, so they can differ from the first time round. In a network game everyone shares one board. The Match-3 template's menu remembers which levels are won, not their best scores.
- A falling-block board has no holding a piece back for later, no T-spins or combo and back-to-back bonuses, no points for dropping a piece, and its turns step aside a cell or two rather than following any one game's rules. Rows only clear when full (no garbage rows rising from below, no gravity pulling loose blocks down after a clear), and a game is one board: there's no two-player versus. A rewind brings in a new piece rather than the one that was falling, and in a network game everyone moves the same piece.
- Dungeons are rectangular rooms joined by straight corridors that turn once: no caves, doors, secret rooms or rooms of a theme (a treasury, a boss's lair). Spawns come in the same numbers on every floor (the Roguelike template's floors don't get harder), monsters stand still until they notice the player (no wandering), and nothing remembers which cells the player has seen (no fog over the unexplored, or minimap).
- Items are used by number key or script, not picked from a menu (there's no inventory screen yet, or using an item with a gamepad), and they only heal, spawn a prefab or send a message: there's no throwing, aiming or equipping. A bump attack always hits for its Damage (no misses, armor or critical hits).
- Turns go side by side, not unit by unit: there's no initiative order, and everyone on a side acts at once during its turn (on a grid, all its monsters step together). Only a Grid Chase or Grid Patrol passes by itself when it has nothing to do; a turret or a scripted monster with actions holds its side's turn until it uses them or Computer Turn Time runs out. A side wiped out is beaten (its Defeat Message) but the game doesn't end by itself: a panel opening on the message stops it, and there's no other way to win (holding a spot, surviving a number of rounds) without a script. In a network game the sides are the Multiplayer's teams.
- Tactics units block each other, friend or foe (no walking through your own side), every unit has one way of attacking (no weapons or spells to choose between, no counterattacks, no hit chances or terrain that slows or shelters), and ranges reach over walls. The computer plays each unit on its own, a go at a time: it doesn't plan moves together (surrounding a foe, shielding a hurt unit), retreat to heal, or think ahead to what you'll do next. The camera doesn't follow the cursor, so a battle has to fit on screen.
- Dialogue flags are on or off: there are no counters or numbers to add to (scripts keep those in save data), and conditions are all-of (no "or"). Flags are kept on each machine (in a network game, each player has their own) and a rewind doesn't take them back. A speaker has one portrait (no expressions to switch between). Choices are rows of the box's text rather than buttons, and only the first nine have number keys.
- A Grabber picks things up by their middle and carries them there, so a big thing held by a small cursor covers it. Several things on one target stack in its middle rather than fanning out like a hand of cards, and a target that moves leaves what's on it behind. In a network game a player's drag goes through the host, so it trails their mouse by their connection, and only the host draws a held thing over everything.
- Strategy games' units only walk (no flying or water), and the fog of war is drawn in squares.
- The beat clock allows for the sound card's own delay, worked out from its buffers, but not for speakers that add their own (Bluetooth headphones, some TVs): there's no setting yet for players to tune it to theirs. It counts at one tempo the whole way through (no tempo changes within a piece), needs music playing to count, takes each loop of the music to be a whole number of beats,. Spawners, shooters, movers and switches step to it, but other things that count seconds (timers, animations, particles, patrols) don't yet.
- Multiplayer connects directly: to play over the internet, the host forwards their port (7777 by default) on their router; there's no matchmaking, relay or lobby server yet. Players' own machines move their own characters, which suits friends better than strangers (pick **Host** movement where cheating matters), and shots are judged where the host sees things, without rewinding for lag. Rewinding in the editor works with one player, and a network game can't hit-stop or slow down (it's everyone's).
- Block worlds are side-on (Terraria style); in first person, digging works on the flat map's walls. Water and lava don't flow, and light from glowing blocks casts no shadows.
- Contraptions don't turn: welded things move together but don't rotate, wheels don't roll, and ropes are straight lines that keep things within reach rather than swinging, sagging ropes.
- First-person games are drawn the classic way, from a flat map: walls are all one height, floors and ceilings are flat colors (no textures, stairs or rooms above rooms), and there's no aiming up or down at things.
