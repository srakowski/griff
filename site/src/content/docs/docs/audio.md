## Choosing sounds

Every sound field in the Inspector (**Sound**, **Jump Sound**, **Music**, a script's `HitSound`) has three buttons: **Play** to hear it, a list of every sound in the project to pick from, and the Sound Maker, which opens on the field's sound if it was made there (or on a new one) and sets whatever it saves. Dragging a sound from the Assets panel onto a field sets it too. A field naming a file that isn't there turns red. Griff plays WAV, MP3 and OGG files.

## Muting the editor

The speaker button on the toolbar (**Game → Mute**, Ctrl+Shift+M) silences everything the editor plays: the game in play mode, the windows of **Play with Players**, the Sound Maker and previews. The sound device is shut while it's on, and the game plays on just as it would with sound (its music keeps time, and scripts play their sounds as before), so nothing about the game changes: its own **Muted** and volume settings, and its saves, are left alone. It's kept between sessions. `GRIFF_MUTE=1` makes an editor silent for that session whatever Mute says, without changing the saved choice (Mute switched there isn't kept), and everything it starts (an exported game run from it) inherits it; exported games take `--mute` too.

## Music and sound players

Add a **Background Music** to a scene and its **Music** starts with the scene, crossfading over **Fade** seconds from whatever was playing (the same music just carries on, so every level can name it). A **Sound Player** plays its **Sound** whenever its **Message** is sent (`Score` from a pickup, a switch's **On Message**, an enemy's **Death Message**) and, with **On Start**, when it appears; **Pitch Variation** changes its pitch a little at random each time, so a sound heard over and over doesn't grate, and **Positional** makes it come from where it is. A **Health** plays its **Hurt Sound** when damage lands and its **Death Sound** when it dies, and a **Button** plays its **Sound** when clicked.

## Sounds in the level

Add a **Sound Source** to a waterfall, a fire or a machine and its **Sound** plays over and over, loudest near the middle of the screen and fading out by its **Range** (0 is heard everywhere at full volume: rain, wind), from the left or right speaker as it's to that side. Untick **Playing** to start it silent; a switch's **Targets** turn it on and off. Its **Pitch** can change as it plays: an engine that revs. Scripts can play a one-off sound where something is with `Game.PlaySoundAt`.

## Keeping the beat

Add a **Beat Clock** (next to the scene's **Background Music** is a good place) and it keeps time with whatever music is playing: a scene's, a room's or a script's. It counts what's been *heard*, not what's been sent to the sound card, which holds a few hundredths of a second of sound before it reaches the speakers; so a beat lands with the drum you hear. Speakers with a delay of their own (Bluetooth headphones, a TV) are allowed for too, by each player's **audio offset**, which they set by tapping along to the clicks of a **Calibration** (**Entity → Audio → Create Calibration Screen**); see [Rhythm games](/docs/rhythm/). On every beat it sends its **Beat Message** (`Beat`, with the beat's number, 0 for the first) and on the first beat of every bar its **Bar Message** (`Bar`, with the bar's number), so a **Sound Player** can tick along and a script do anything on the beat with `On<int>("Beat", beat => ...)`. Music made with **Audio → New Music** says its own tempo, so the clock needs no setting up; for other music set its **Tempo** (beats a minute), and **Offset** (seconds into the music of its first beat) for music with a lead-in, or to nudge the beat until it sits right. **Beats Per Bar** is 4 (3 for a waltz). As the music loops the count carries on (beat 33 after a 32-beat tune, not 0 again), each loop starting on a beat of its own. With no music it waits. While it plays, its **Beat**, **Bar**, **Beat In Bar** (0 is the downbeat), **Phase** (how far through the beat, 0 to 1), **Beats** (the count with the fraction), **Position** (seconds into the music) and **Current Tempo** say where it is, and scripts can time a press to the music with `SecondsFromBeat` (negative just before a beat, positive just after) or follow its `BeatStarted` and `BarStarted` events. Give anything a **Beat Pulse** and it bops on the beat: drawn bigger by its **Size** as each beat lands, shrinking back before the next, **Every** that many beats (4 for once a bar), with its children; only how it's drawn changes, not its transform or collider. A pulse follows the scene's first clock, or the one on the entity its **Clock** names. **Spawners**, **Shooters**, **Movers** and **Switches** step to the beat too: set their **Beats** and they spawn, fire, set off for their next point or flip on or off on every that many beats instead of counting seconds. A spawner or shooter still waits out its **Delay** first, then goes on the next beat it steps on (a spawner with no room waits for the next one after that; a shooter with an **Action** fires on the beat while it's held). A mover sets off on a step, gets to the next point at its **Speed** (faster if that wouldn't get it there by the next step), and waits there for the step after rather than for its **Wait**; it waits at its start for the first. A switch flips whoever has worked it, so with **Targets** it brings out spikes every other bar, or opens a gate on the beat (a plate goes back to how it's held). **Beat Offset** shifts each one later by that many beats, so two turrets on every 2 beats with offsets 0 and 1 take turns, and **Clock** names the clock to step to (empty is the scene's first). Scripts can step the same way with `clock.OnBeat(every, offset)`, true in the frame a beat it steps on lands. Scripts can read the music's time themselves: `Audio.MusicTime` is the seconds heard (before the player's audio offset, which a clock takes off), `Audio.MusicLength` how long it lasts and `Audio.MusicTempo` the tempo it says; `clock.SecondsFrom(beat)` times a press against any beat, and `Audio.PlayMetronome(tempo)` plays a click track as the music. In a network game every machine keeps time with its own music, and the host's clock sends the messages to everyone.
