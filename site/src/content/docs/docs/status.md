---
title: "Known gaps"
description: "What Griff doesn't do yet."
---

Griff is young. Known gaps:
- CI runs on Windows only, and Griff.Graphics and Griff.Platform.Windows have no tests of their own: the player smoke test covers them end to end. The layer test checks Griff.Scenes; Runtime still reaches into Rendering.
- Modules are found only in Griff itself: a project can't add one. Templates several modules share pictures from (the crate, the crypt's hero and walls) keep those in the core's `TemplateArt`.
- The Scene view draws sprites, tilemaps, text and particles itself and every other drawable from its quads, but not a board that draws its tilemap in the game (there you paint the tiles).
- Games play in a browser only as a proof, from `tools\web.ps1` rather than the Build menu, and there they draw sprites and text and take the keyboard and mouse but have no lights, screen effects, sound, saves, controllers or multiplayer (see Web).
- Templates are found only in Griff itself: a project can't add one of its own to New Project. A component saved by a newer version of it keeps the values it doesn't know, but is written back at this version.
- Big scenes: an exported game holds about 128,000 BunnyMark bunnies at 60 fps (127,932 to 144,686 over 3 runs on a mid-range desktop PC, October 2026, each held with a 1% low of 60 fps; 76,283 to 78,666 on the same PC before the frame was built and the bunnies' scripts run across the cores, about 69,000 with a frame built in two passes over the entities, 28,200 before that), and 100,000 at 60 fps with 1% lows at 60 fps and no garbage collections in ten seconds, the aim set for it, in 3 runs out of 3 (before: 30 fps, a 1% low of 30, in 3 out of 3). A frame of 100,000 takes 7.4 ms of the CPU (`tools/bunnybench.cs`): 1.6 ms of steps, 2.9 to build and 2.8 to draw with OpenGL, which is done on the game's thread, as are what a frame's chunks leave it (scripts' drawables, what's inside other entities) and copying every draw to its place once (about 0.6 ms at 100,000). On a computer with fewer cores the count is lower: with everything on one thread (`--workers 1`), 100,000 take 15 ms (4.6 ms of steps, 7.5 to build, 2.7 to draw; 18 ms before). Text whose words change (a score, the frame rate) makes no garbage: it's laid out from glyphs already on an atlas page (before, it was drawn again with WPF, which made the odd collection in a long run). Playing in the editor keeps up with them now (see [Rewind in big scenes](/docs/scripting/#rewind-in-big-scenes)): a Debug editor holds 5,000 bunnies at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60 and 1 garbage collection in ten seconds (before: 39 to 45 fps, a 1% low of 7, 69 to 81 collections), and 20,000 at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60 (before: 59 fps, a 1% low of 30), snapshotting them ten times a second (before, every two seconds), and with the frame built and the bunnies' scripts run across the cores, 50,000 at 55 fps with a 1% low of 30 (before: 19.6 fps and a 1% low of 12; a rewind snapshot of 50,000, copied out on the game's thread, still takes 6.6 ms there); a Release editor holds 20,000 at 60 fps with a 1% low of 60, and 50,000 at 59 fps with a 1% low of 60 (before: 58 fps, a 1% low of 30). Copying a snapshot out still happens on one step (it has to, to be one moment), so the biggest scenes are snapshotted less often to keep their frames on time: 50,000 bunnies about seven times a second in a Release editor and four in a Debug one, and just after rewinding a big scene, whose entities are all fresh, a little less often for a while.
- Mouse look turns once a step, and the screen shows the game up to a step behind, so it trails the mouse by a step or two; so does what follows the mouse (a strategy game's drag box and placing ghost, the contraptions tools' line, something being dragged), which is drawn where the mouse was at the last step. A drop of rain wrapping round the view is drawn where it lands a step early (out of view).
- Physics shapes are boxes and circles (no polygons yet), and boxes stay upright unless their body **Rotates**. Slopes are tilemap tiles at 45 degrees only, and circle bodies that don't rotate slide rather than roll. A crate resting on another sinks into it by about a five-hundredth of a unit (overlaps are eased apart, not kicked), and a sleeping one still costs about two microseconds a step, checking it still has something under it. There are no hinges or other joints.
- Shadows are hard-edged, and come from colliders and tiles rather than sprites' shapes.
- A tilemap's extra tilesets are numbered by their place in **More Tilesets**: taking one out, or putting one before another, renumbers the ones after it, and cells painted from them change tile. The Sprite Painter's **Paint** button on a tilemap paints its first tileset (open the others from the Assets panel).
- A tile drawn at a size its pixels don't divide evenly can pick up a column of the pixels beside it in the tileset along an edge nothing covers (an isometric tile's left and right corners): leave a tile's outermost columns clear, as the Isometric template's tileset does.
- A tilemap's layers are switched and added under the palette, but put in order, hidden and removed in the Hierarchy, and every layer of a map shares its tilesets and tile settings (an isometric map's shape and **Tile Height** too, so a tileset's tiles are all one height).
- Isometric maps don't cast shadows yet. On hex maps, welded contraptions pass through the walls, dungeons are generated on square maps only, autotiles join on a square's four sides (not a hexagon's six), and hexagons only point up and down (no flat-topped hexagons in columns). On an isometric map, bodies meet other colliders as boxes (only the walls are diamonds), welded contraptions pass through its walls, and a sword or other close-up attack swings to the side of the screen a grid mover faces (not along the diagonal; on a hex map, up or down for the four slanted ways). A row of standing tiles sorts among the sprites by its middle, so something wider than a cell (a two-cell sofa, a big monster) can be drawn wrongly against the tiles beside it.
- A clip's frames are one run of the sprite sheet in order (no skipping or repeating frames, or playing backwards), and a clip given its own speed or looping in the Clip Editor can only go back to the animation's by editing **Clips**. Renaming a clip in the Clip Editor doesn't rename it in scripts that play it by name (`Play("Run")`) or in the clip names movement components look for (`Idle`, `Run`, `Jump`...).
- A floating window holds panels tabbed together, not side by side, and the document tabs (Scene, Game, prefabs, scripts) stay in the main window.
- The Scene view's handles resize sprites, box and circle colliders, distances (light radii, ranges, sight) and paths only on the entity picked last, even with several selected, and distances of 0 have no handle until they're set in the Inspector. A mover's start is where it's placed (move the entity), and a patrol's route is level, left and right.
- Physics makes a little garbage each step in a scene with a solid tilemap (about 600 bytes with 5,000 BunnyMark bunnies among walls and crates); building and drawing the picture make none, in the dark too.
- The Profiler splits the game's steps by script, step and system, and building the picture by what's drawn, but drawing it only as a whole; an entity's upkeep counts with the kind of thing it draws first (a sprite with text under its sprite); script code run from timers, coroutines, tweens and messages counts in the step that ran it. Garbage is counted on the game's thread only (not the rewind snapshots settled in the background). It profiles the Game tab's game only (not the other players' windows of Play with Players, or an exported game), and drawing is timed as the CPU hands it to the graphics card, not the GPU's own part.
- Dragging rows in the Hierarchy moves one at a time, even with several selected.
- A breakpoint, and a step, stops at the first statement on its line, once each time the code gets there (not again for a second statement on the same line, so Step Out lands on the line after the call rather than the rest of the calling line), and only in a game's own frame: not in code the editor runs itself (the Inspector reading a property, a rewind snapshot), or a field's starting value. Values can be looked at but not changed while the game stands there, and a property worked out in code (rather than stored) isn't shown for a script's own object, since showing it would run the game's code.
- Stopped where a script threw, the values in scope aren't known for a line of an iterator's that something other than a coroutine runs (a `foreach` over it) and no guard can stand around (a loop holding a `yield return`, say); a coroutine's show its variables as they were. Rewinding to before an exception brings back what a snapshot holds (a script's private fields start afresh, as in any rewind), so an exception that hangs on one may not come again.
- The Inspector's and Hierarchy's filters match names only: not a field's value, or the components an entity has. The color picker has no eyedropper for picking a color off the screen.
- Copied component values stay inside the editor until it closes (not on the system clipboard), and paste only onto the same kind of component. Components aren't dragged to reorder them; use Move Up and Move Down.
- Fields that name entities hold their names: renaming an entity updates them in the open scene, but not in other scenes, prefabs or scripts. Fields naming prefabs, scenes, layers and messages aren't renamed with them either (they're flagged instead). Messages and layers in scripts are only found where they're written out (`Game.Send("Score")`, `On("Score", ...)`, `Layer = "Bullet"`), not built up in code, and a script listening by a name it builds can leave its message flagged. Items, teams, turn sides and save names are typed (they have no list yet). In a prefab's tab, only fields naming something inside their own entity (an attack's **Hitbox**) are flagged; the rest name things in whatever scene it's placed in.
- A copy placed inside another copy in a scene follows its own prefab (what the outer copy changes on it isn't kept). Renaming or moving a prefab outside Griff (in Explorer) unlinks its copies, renaming one doesn't rename it where it's spawned by name (a Spawner's **Prefab**, `Game.Spawn`), and only prefabs can be renamed or moved in Assets (not other files or folders). Children of a copy are matched to the prefab's by name, so renaming one counts as taking it away and adding another (and renaming one in the prefab's tab loses what copies changed on it). A prefab's tab keeps no crash-recovery copy, as scenes do. A prefab file changed outside Griff (not through Save as Prefab, Apply to Prefab or an agent's asset_write) doesn't update its copies.
- Grid crates are pushed, never pulled, and stop after one cell (no ice to slide across). Undo puts back only grid movers (not what was collected or destroyed, counters, switches or turns), levels are built from entities on a tilemap (there's no reading Sokoban text levels in, and the Puzzle template's menu remembers which levels are solved, not the fewest moves), an editor rewind starts the move history afresh from the moment rewound to, and a network game has no undo. In a network game a player's push moves the crate once the host hears of it, so their character steps into the crate's cell a moment before the crate moves off. A rewind that lands partway through a step puts a grid mover on the nearer of the two cells (and a grid patrol waiting at a point waits afresh). Grid enemies chase and patrol but don't flee or wander at random, and grids are square, isometric or hex.
- Match-3 gems are plain kinds: there are no special gems (stripes, bombs) or obstacles over the gems (ice, chains), every cell of the board's rectangle is in play (blocks stand in for holes), gems only fall straight down (never sliding past a block's corner), and swaps wait until the board is still. A won level doesn't turn the moves left into points, and goals are a score and gems of a kind (not blocks to break or things to bring down). A rewind's refills come from the board's Seed afresh, so they can differ from the first time round. In a network game everyone shares one board. The Match-3 template's menu remembers which levels are won, not their best scores.
- A falling-block board has no holding a piece back for later, no T-spins or combo and back-to-back bonuses, no points for dropping a piece, and its turns step aside a cell or two rather than following any one game's rules. Rows only clear when full (no garbage rows rising from below, no gravity pulling loose blocks down after a clear), and a game is one board: there's no two-player versus. A rewind brings in a new piece rather than the one that was falling, and in a network game everyone moves the same piece.
- Dungeons are rectangular rooms joined by straight corridors that turn once: no caves, doors, secret rooms or rooms of a theme (a treasury, a boss's lair). Spawns come in the same numbers on every floor (the Roguelike template's floors don't get harder), monsters stand still until they notice the player (no wandering), and nothing remembers which cells the player has seen (no fog over the unexplored, or minimap).
- Items are used by number key or script, not picked from a menu (there's no inventory screen yet, or using an item with a gamepad), and they only heal, spawn a prefab or send a message: there's no throwing, aiming or equipping. A bump attack always hits for its Damage (no misses, armor or critical hits).
- Turns go side by side, not unit by unit: there's no initiative order, and everyone on a side acts at once during its turn (on a grid, all its monsters step together). Only a Grid Chase or Grid Patrol passes by itself when it has nothing to do; a turret or a scripted monster with actions holds its side's turn until it uses them or Computer Turn Time runs out. A side wiped out is beaten (its Defeat Message) but the game doesn't end by itself: a panel opening on the message stops it, and there's no other way to win (holding a spot, surviving a number of rounds) without a script. In a network game the sides are the Multiplayer's teams.
- Tactics units block each other, friend or foe (no walking through your own side), every unit has one way of attacking (no weapons or spells to choose between, no counterattacks, no hit chances or terrain that slows or shelters), and ranges reach over walls. The computer plays each unit on its own, a go at a time: it doesn't plan moves together (surrounding a foe, shielding a hurt unit), retreat to heal, or think ahead to what you'll do next. The camera doesn't follow the cursor, so a battle has to fit on screen.
- Dialogue flags are on or off: there are no counters or numbers to add to (scripts keep those in save data), and conditions are all-of (no "or"). Flags are kept on each machine (in a network game, each player has their own) and a rewind doesn't take them back. A speaker has one portrait (no expressions to switch between). Choices are rows of the box's text rather than buttons, and only the first nine have number keys.
- A Grabber picks things up by their middle and carries them there, so a big thing held by a small cursor covers it. Several things on one target stack in its middle rather than fanning out like a hand of cards, and a target that moves leaves what's on it behind. In a network game a player's drag goes through the host, so it trails their mouse by their connection, and only the host draws a held thing over everything.
- Strategy games' units only walk (no flying or water), and the fog of war is drawn in squares.
- The beat clock allows for the sound card's own delay, worked out from its buffers, but not for speakers that add their own (Bluetooth headphones, some TVs): there's no setting yet for players to tune it to theirs. It counts at one tempo the whole way through (no tempo changes within a piece), needs music playing to count, takes each loop of the music to be a whole number of beats,. Spawners, shooters, movers and switches step to it, but other things that count seconds (timers, animations, particles, patrols) don't yet.
- Multiplayer connects directly: to play over the internet, the host forwards their port (7777 by default) on their router; there's no matchmaking, relay or lobby server yet. Players' own machines move their own characters, which suits friends better than strangers (pick **Host** movement where cheating matters), and shots are judged where the host sees things, without rewinding for lag. Rewinding in the editor works with one player, and a network game can't hit-stop or slow down (it's everyone's).
- Block worlds are side-on (Terraria style); in first person, digging works on the flat map's walls. Water and lava don't flow, and light from glowing blocks casts no shadows.
- Contraptions don't turn: welded things move together but don't rotate, wheels don't roll, and ropes are straight lines that keep things within reach rather than swinging, sagging ropes.
- First-person games are drawn the classic way, from a flat map: walls are all one height, floors and ceilings are flat colors (no textures, stairs or rooms above rooms), and there's no aiming up or down at things.
